\title{Streaming Latency Benchmarks for Real-Time Football Feeds: \redis{} Streams versus Apache \kafka{}, and the Consistency Check That Overturned Our First Answer}

\author{Gustavo Pedro Ricou\affilnum{1}}

\affiliation{\affilnum{1}School of Computer Science and Statistics, Trinity College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland}

\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Aim.} Live football analytics depends on streaming middleware, and teams choose between
Apache \kafka{} and \redis{} Streams using benchmarks run at data rates no sport produces. We set
out to compare the two on real football feeds, under the number of simultaneous matches football
actually generates.

\textbf{What we did.} We replayed 3{,}315 matches from the StatsBomb open dataset (2003--2023) at
their true event rate through both systems, on a four-machine cloud testbed, with open
load-generation code. We measured $2{,}266$ runs in total.

\textbf{The unexpected result.} Our first complete answer was physically impossible. It showed
\redis{} slowing down as matches were added while \kafka{} stayed flat, with very strong
statistical support. Checking every run against a simple physical rule --- a message cannot
arrive before it is sent --- showed that $58\%$ of our runs contained impossible timestamps,
including every run behind that finding. The faulty runs looked completely normal in every
summary statistic we or a reviewer would inspect.

\textbf{The expected results.} After discarding the faulty runs, the two brokers are equivalent
to within one millisecond and neither slows down as matches are added, which is what the
sport's low data rate predicts. Two practical differences remain: end-to-end delay is 20 times
lower for \redis{} ($5.2$ vs $105.5$~ms), caused by the client rather than the broker; and this
reverses when the consumer sits across a network, where \kafka{} stays under $100$~ms and
\redis{} reaches $31$~seconds unless one line of consumer code is changed.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Real-time sports analytics, football, streaming systems, Apache Kafka, Redis Streams,
latency benchmarking, measurement validity, reproducible research}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Live football analytics products --- in-play odds engines, coaching dashboards, broadcast
statistics overlays --- all share the same shape. Events are recorded as the match happens, sent
across a network, and delivered to a model that must react before the moment passes. The software
that carries those events is called a message broker, and two products dominate the role.

Apache \kafka{} \cite{kreps2011kafka} stores messages in a partitioned, replicated log written to
disk. It is designed for durability and high throughput. \redis{} Streams
\cite{redis2017streams} keeps messages in memory in an append-only structure. It is designed for
minimum delay on each operation. Practitioners generally choose between them using published
comparisons that report \redis{} as two to five times faster
\cite{medium_benchmark_2025,github_benchmark_2025,jusdb2025}. None of those comparisons uses a
sports workload, and most cannot be reproduced.

This paper asks the practical question directly. Using the StatsBomb open dataset
\cite{statsbomb2023} and open load-generation scripts, we compare end-to-end delay for the two
systems at the number of simultaneous matches football actually produces. We then ask three
follow-up questions that a practitioner needs answered: where in the pipeline does any difference
come from, under which deployment conditions does the answer change, and how much configuration
effort does each system demand.

\subsection{The answer we obtained first}

Our first campaign returned a clean result. \redis{}'s broker delay rose steadily as we added
concurrent matches ($1.006 \rightarrow 1.197 \rightarrow 1.346$~ms across one, five and ten
feeds), while \kafka{} stayed flat to within $0.3\%$. Every comparison was statistically
significant after correction for multiple testing, the strongest at $p = 9.0\times10^{-11}$, and
at five feeds and above the two systems' results did not overlap at all.

The finding was not only significant, it was explicable. A \redis{} server executes commands on a
single thread, so concurrent streams must queue for one execution context, whereas \kafka{}
divides a topic into partitions that can be served in parallel. Our measurement appeared to
reproduce the textbook difference between the two designs, in the expected direction and at the
expected scale.

It was an artefact. We compute broker delay by subtracting the time the broker confirmed a
message from the time the consumer received it. Those two times are recorded by two different
programs. A message cannot arrive before it is sent, so a negative value is not measurement
noise; it is proof that the instrument has failed. We checked every run in the study against that
rule --- not only the runs whose results looked odd --- and found impossible timestamps in $862$
of $1{,}382$ single-machine runs and $459$ of $884$ multi-machine runs. That included every run
behind the result above.

\subsection{Why we make this the centre of the paper}

Timestamp inversion of this kind is invisible to every check a reader would apply. The medians
stay positive and plausible. The confidence intervals stay narrow. The effect sizes are large and
the direction matches theory. Only $5$ to $14\%$ of individual events invert, which is enough to
shift a median by a fraction of a millisecond in a measurement whose entire effect is a fraction
of a millisecond, and far too little to make any summary look wrong.

A benchmark can therefore produce a complete, internally consistent and entirely false set of
results, and no amount of statistical care will expose it. Only a check against a physical
constraint will, and it has to be applied to every condition as a rule rather than to the
conditions that look suspicious.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A clock-integrity check for streaming benchmarks}, stated as a rule rather than a
judgement, applied to all $2{,}266$ runs in this study and released as open code. We report what
it rejects, why the rejected data looked correct, and one property that makes such benchmarks
fragile in general: the check bites hardest exactly where the measured effect is smallest
(Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
\item \textbf{The benchmark itself, inside the check.} At football's real concurrency levels,
\redis{} Streams delivers a median end-to-end delay of $5.2$~ms against \kafka{}'s $105.5$~ms,
while the two brokers themselves are equivalent to within one millisecond and neither slows down
as matches are added (Section~\ref{sec:e1}).
\item \textbf{An explanation and a configuration account.} The twentyfold difference comes from
the client library, not the broker, and it only appears at football's low event rate. Each system
has exactly one client setting worth one to two orders of magnitude in delay, and in each case
the introductory example in the documentation uses the slow value
(Sections~\ref{sec:attribution} and~\ref{sec:config}).
\item \textbf{A description of the football event feed as a workload}, covering 3{,}315 matches,
which supplies the event rate, burst structure and concurrency levels the benchmark is driven at
(Section~\ref{sec:workload}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 (delay):] Replaying real match feeds at their true event rate, what end-to-end delay do
\kafka{} and \redis{} Streams deliver, and do they differ?
\item[RQ2 (concurrency):] Does that delay change across the number of simultaneous matches
football produces, and does either system degrade faster than the other?
\item[RQ3 (cause):] Where does any difference come from --- the broker itself, or the client
software on either side of it?
\item[RQ4 (deployment):] Does the answer survive when the consumer sits across a network, and
what explains the failure if it does not?
\item[RQ5 (validity):] How much of a benchmark conducted in the usual way survives an explicit
physical-consistency check, and what does the rejected portion look like beforehand?
\end{description}

We answer RQ5 first, in Sections~\ref{sec:firstanswer} and~\ref{sec:audit}, because its answer
decides which evidence RQ1--RQ4 are allowed to use.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:literature}

This study sits between four literatures. We set out what each contributes, and what it does not
supply, so that every assumption made later has a stated source.

\subsection{The two systems, and how their clients work}
\label{sec:lit_systems}

Stonebraker et al.\ \cite{stonebraker2005stream} set out eight requirements for real-time stream
processing, including keeping data moving and processing it with predictably low delay. These
remain the standard against which streaming middleware is judged.

\kafka{} \cite{kreps2011kafka} implements them as a distributed commit log. A topic is divided
into partitions; each partition is an ordered, append-only sequence written to disk and
replicated. Throughput comes from batching writes and reading sequentially; parallelism comes
from assigning partitions to independent consumers. \redis{} Streams \cite{redis2017streams}
takes the opposite position: an in-memory append-only structure with consumer groups, trading
storage capacity for the lowest possible delay per operation.

One architectural consequence matters throughout this paper. A \redis{} server executes commands
on a single thread, so concurrent streams contend for one execution context, whereas \kafka{}'s
partitioning permits genuine parallelism inside a broker. This is the prediction our invalid first
result appeared to confirm (Section~\ref{sec:firstanswer}), and it is why that result was
persuasive.

A second consequence concerns the client libraries rather than the servers, and it turns out to
matter more. Both systems place substantial machinery on the client side. A \kafka{} producer
does not send each record immediately; it appends the record to an in-memory batch which a
background sender thread transmits, governed by settings including \texttt{linger.ms} (how long
to wait for a batch to fill) and \texttt{max.in.flight.requests.per.connection} (how many
unacknowledged requests may be outstanding). A \kafka{} consumer likewise serves \texttt{poll()}
from a locally prefetched buffer and commits its position on a timer. A \redis{} Streams consumer
by contrast issues an explicit blocking read (\texttt{XREADGROUP}) and an explicit acknowledgement
(\texttt{XACK}) per message, each of which is a separate request and reply. Vendor documentation
for both systems discusses these settings
\cite{confluent2025kafka,redis2025performance}. Our results in
Sections~\ref{sec:attribution} and~\ref{sec:netdelay} turn on them.

The CAP theorem \cite{brewer2012cap} and its extension PACELC \cite{abadi2012pacelc} frame the
durability--latency trade-off both systems expose. PACELC's ``else'' clause --- that even without
a network partition a system trades latency against consistency --- is what our durability
comparison probes.

\subsection{How streaming systems are benchmarked}
\label{sec:lit_bench}

Stream processing has a mature benchmark literature. Linear Road \cite{arasu2004linear} is the
canonical application benchmark, posing tolling and traffic queries over a simulated stream of
vehicles. NEXMark \cite{tucker2008nexmark} models an online auction and has become the standard
query suite for streaming dataflow engines. ESPBench \cite{hesse2021espbench} constructs an
enterprise workload spanning transactional and analytical operators. RIoTBench
\cite{shukla2017riotbench} supplies Internet-of-Things dataflows with realistic sensor arrival
patterns. The OpenMessaging Benchmark \cite{openmessaging2018} is the closest analogue to this
work, measuring broker throughput and delay directly across messaging systems.

Three observations follow, and each shapes a design decision here.

First, every one of these benchmarks drives its system with a \emph{synthetic} arrival process
--- generated vehicles, auctions, sensor readings, or fixed-rate publishers --- chosen for
analytical tractability rather than fidelity to a domain. That is the right choice for a
general-purpose benchmark and the wrong one here. Our central explanatory result
(Section~\ref{sec:attribution}) is that the difference between the two systems appears at
football's low event rate and \emph{disappears} when the same data is replayed ten times faster.
A fixed-rate synthetic publisher would have reported no difference at all.

Second, these benchmarks are built to locate the point at which a distributed system breaks.
Football does not come close to one, as Section~\ref{sec:workload} shows, so the interesting
question for this domain is not where the system breaks but whether it is anywhere near breaking.

Third, and most relevant to this paper, a recent survey of benchmark practice in \kafka{}
event-streaming systems \cite{mohammad2025kafka} observes that published results are frequently
not comparable, because client configuration, acknowledgement semantics and measurement
instrumentation differ between the systems under test. Our experience is a strong instance of
that critique and extends it in one direction: we find that comparable \emph{configuration} is
not sufficient, because the measurement instrument itself can fail silently under load. We are
not aware of a streaming benchmark that reports what fraction of its runs it discarded on
integrity grounds. Given our findings, we take that to be a gap in reporting practice rather than
evidence that the problem is rare.

\subsection{Measuring time across processes}
\label{sec:lit_clocks}

The failure at the centre of this paper is a timing failure, and the distributed systems
literature supplies the vocabulary for it.

Lamport \cite{lamport1978time} established that in a distributed system there is no single
physical time, only a partial order induced by causality: if event $a$ can influence event $b$
then $a$ must precede $b$ in any correct account. Our check is the simplest possible application
of that principle. A broker's acknowledgement of a message causally precedes the consumer's
receipt of it, so a measurement in which receipt is timestamped earlier is not a small error but
a violation of the causal order, and any conclusion drawn from it is unsupported.

In practice, systems approximate physical time using clock synchronisation protocols, of which
the Network Time Protocol \cite{mills1991ntp} is the most widely deployed. Spanner
\cite{corbett2013spanner} takes the further step of exposing clock \emph{uncertainty} to the
application through an explicit interval API, on the grounds that a system which pretends to know
the time exactly will eventually be wrong in a way it cannot detect. Our situation is milder ---
both of our processes run on one machine and read the same operating-system clock, so there is no
synchronisation error to speak of --- and this is precisely why the failure is instructive. The
inversion arises not from clock skew but from \emph{when the timestamp is taken}: a thread that
is descheduled between the event it is recording and the call that records it will stamp a time
later than the event. Under CPU saturation this delay is routinely larger than the interval being
measured.

Jain \cite{jain1991art} sets out the standard methodology for computer systems performance
measurement, including the requirement that an instrument be validated on the system it will
measure. The check we describe in Section~\ref{sec:gate} is best understood as a specific,
cheap instrument validation for cross-process delay measurement.

\subsection{Football event data as a workload}
\label{sec:lit_football}

Live football event data is produced by trained human operators who watch the match and record
each action through specialised software. Pappalardo et al.\ \cite{pappalardo2019public}, in
releasing a large public event dataset, describe this collection process directly. Liu et al.\
\cite{liu2013interoperator} measured its reliability: two independent groups of experienced
operators coding the same Spanish league match reached very good agreement on team events (Cohen's
$\kappa$ of $0.92$ and $0.94$), with a mean difference in recorded event time of
$0.06 \pm 0.04$~s.

Two things follow for this study. The reliability result establishes that event data is accurate
enough for the timing comparisons we make, since operator disagreement about \emph{when} an event
occurred is around $60$~ms and our replay uses the recorded times identically for both systems.
But no peer-reviewed measurement exists of how long the annotation pipeline takes end to end ---
that is, the delay between an action on the pitch and the corresponding event becoming available
to a consumer. Vendor descriptions of these pipelines report figures in seconds. Because those
are claims rather than measurements, we do not adopt a point estimate; we sweep the value across a
range and report what share of the budget each component owns throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:budget}).

The StatsBomb open dataset \cite{statsbomb2023} used here is a sample of matches released for
research, covering multiple competitions and seasons. We treat it as a sample throughout, which
constrains the concurrency claim in Section~\ref{sec:realconcurrency}.

\subsection{Latency requirements in sport, and where they come from}
\label{sec:lit_requirements}

Practitioner sources state latency budgets for live sports applications. Betting operators
require updates within a few hundred milliseconds for in-play odds
\cite{v2solutions2025,ververica2025}; broadcast overlays tolerate up to a few seconds
\cite{dolby2025,promwad2025}. Table~\ref{tab:latency_requirements} collects these.

We must be explicit about the status of that table, because a reader could reasonably mistake it
for measured evidence. Every figure in it is \emph{asserted} by a vendor or industry source, not
derived from any published measurement of what missing the budget costs. We use it as the
practitioner context our results are interpreted against, and not as a criterion of success. One
consequence is worth stating at the outset: every configuration we measure soundly meets every
budget in the table, and the interesting differences between the two systems are two orders of
magnitude below the tightest of them --- until a network hop is introduced, at which point one
configuration misses every budget by four orders of magnitude.

We also draw on the Age-of-Information framing \cite{kaul2012realtime}, which formalises the cost
of acting on stale data: a monitor acting on information of age $L$ incurs an error that grows
with $L$. We use this only to express our measured milliseconds on a decision-relevant scale in
Section~\ref{sec:budget}. For that expression we need an in-play win-probability model. The state
of the art for soccer is the Bayesian model of Robberechts et al.\ \cite{robberechts2021bayesian},
whose implementation is not public; we therefore use a transparent calibrated stand-in and are
explicit about its limitations.

\subsection{Statistical methods}
\label{sec:lit_stats}

Latency distributions are strongly non-normal, so we use rank-based tests throughout: the
Mann--Whitney U test \cite{mann1947test} for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal--Wallis test
\cite{kruskal1952use} across concurrency levels, with Holm's step-down procedure
\cite{holm1979simple} controlling the family-wise error rate.

Several of our claims are negative --- that two systems do \emph{not} differ. Failing to reject a
null hypothesis is not evidence for it, so we state those claims using the two one-sided tests
procedure \cite{schuirmann1987tost}, which reverses the burden of proof by testing whether the
difference is smaller than a pre-specified margin. Lakens \cite{lakens2017equivalence} gives an
accessible treatment and the practical guidance we follow on choosing margins. As a point
estimate of the difference between two systems we use the Hodges--Lehmann shift
\cite{hodges1963estimates}, the median of all pairwise differences, with confidence intervals
from the percentile bootstrap \cite{efron1979bootstrap}. Section~\ref{sec:stat_methods} explains
why we moved to this estimator from a difference of means.

\subsection{What is missing, and what this paper adds}

Streaming benchmarks measure delay but use synthetic workloads and do not report integrity
audits. Sports systems literature asserts latency budgets without measuring the transport term
inside them. The football event-data literature characterises annotation accuracy but not
pipeline delay. This paper measures the transport term on a real football workload, reports the
integrity audit that most of our own measurements failed, and states what a practitioner should
do as a result.

\section{The Football Event Feed as a Workload}
\label{sec:workload}

A latency benchmark needs three parameters: how fast events arrive, how bursty they are, and how
many feeds run at once. These are usually chosen for convenience. Because our main explanatory
result depends on the arrival rate being football's rather than a generator's, we derive all
three from the data first.

We use the StatsBomb open dataset across 2003--2023: 52 competition-seasons, 15 competitions and
3{,}315 matches, of which 2{,}806 are men's and 509 women's. The corpus is fetched from a pinned
upstream commit by \texttt{scripts/fetch\_statsbomb\_corpus.py}, so it can be reconstructed
exactly.

\subsection{Arrival rate and burstiness}
\label{sec:burstiness}

Football event data is sparse. A match produces a median of $3{,}507$ events over roughly
$8{,}412$ seconds of match clock, a mean rate of \textbf{0.415 events per second}
(Figure~\ref{fig:workload}a). That is the number a capacity calculation would use, and on its own
it is misleading. Measured over a sliding ten-second window, the same feeds peak at
\textbf{2.70 events per second}. The ratio of peak to mean is \textbf{6.45}
(Figure~\ref{fig:workload}b, Table~\ref{tab:feedstats}). Half of all gaps between events are one
second or shorter, and the fifth percentile is indistinguishable from zero. Events cluster because
football clusters: a restart, a sequence of quick passes, a goal and the play around it all
arrive in tight groups separated by long quiet periods.

The pattern is stable across the corpus. Every competition falls between $0.35$ and $0.44$ events
per second with a peak-to-mean ratio between $6.2$ and $7.9$, and the men's and women's game
differ by less than seven percent on both measures. The sparsest competitions are the burstiest:
the African Cup of Nations and the Indian Super League have the lowest mean rates and the highest
ratios, so a low average is not a licence to provision for the average.

Two consequences carry into the rest of the paper. A buffer sized from the mean is undersized by
more than a factor of six. More importantly, a feed that is idle most of the time and busy in
short bursts is exactly the pattern under which a client that must wake from idle pays that cost
on every burst. Section~\ref{sec:attribution} measures a $103$~ms cost of precisely that shape.

\subsection{How many matches run at once}
\label{sec:realconcurrency}

Concurrency levels in this literature are chosen by hand. Our own earlier design swept one, five,
ten and twenty feeds, and later ten, twenty-five, fifty and one hundred. That makes ``does delay
scale with concurrency?'' a question about an invented variable. Football supplies the variable
directly, because match records carry a kick-off date and time.

Across 2{,}346 distinct kick-off instants in the corpus, the largest carries \textbf{12
simultaneous matches}, and allowing for a nominal $115$-minute match the peak number of matches
\emph{in play} at any instant is \textbf{21} (Figure~\ref{fig:concurrency}). The median slot
carries one match. These maxima are not accidents of sampling: domestic leagues schedule the
final matchday simultaneously to protect competitive integrity, so a 20-team league plays ten
concurrent matches by rule, and our largest observed slots are exactly such fixtures.

We therefore benchmark at $N \in \{1, 9, 10, 12\}$: the median slot, the upper quartile of
occupancy, the structural league-matchday bound, and the largest simultaneity observed.

Two limits on this must be restated wherever the numbers are used. First, StatsBomb open data is a
\emph{sample} of matches, not a complete record of any league-season, so observed simultaneity is
a \textbf{lower bound}; the structural bound of half the teams in a league is the safer planning
figure. Second, that bound is \emph{per league}. A commercial feed platform serving many leagues
and divisions at once faces their sum, which on a European Saturday afternoon runs into the
hundreds of matches. Our concurrency result therefore describes a consumer of a single
competition. The connection experiment of Section~\ref{sec:conncluster} is the arm that would
speak to the multi-league case, and it is the arm the integrity check largely removed.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Two testbeds, both disclosed}
\label{sec:testbeds}

Several numbers in earlier versions of this work turned out to be properties of the machine
rather than of either broker, so we state both testbeds fully and measure the parts that limit
resolution (\texttt{scripts/platform\_probe.py}; raw output in \texttt{docs/results/platform/}).

\emph{Testbed A (single machine).} Windows~11 (build 10.0.26200), 8-core AMD Ryzen, 31~GB RAM,
CPython 3.9.13. The producer and consumer are native Windows processes; \kafka{}~4.1.1 and
\redis{}~7.2.4 run in Docker containers whose engine executes inside a WSL2 virtual machine,
reached through published ports. Two measured floors bound everything measured here. A requested
1~ms sleep takes a median of \textbf{15.6~ms}, because of the Windows timer tick, so event
emission is quantised to a roughly 15.6~ms grid. And opening a TCP connection to a published
container port costs a median of $5.6$~ms for \redis{} and $9.9$~ms for \kafka{}, far above a
genuine loopback path.

\emph{Testbed B (four machines).} Four Oracle Cloud \texttt{VM.Standard.E5.Flex} virtual machines
in the London region --- three brokers and one driver --- running Ubuntu 22.04 and CPython
3.10.12, communicating over the tenancy's private network. Client library versions are pinned
identically to Testbed~A. Both floors disappear: a requested 1~ms sleep takes $1.06$~ms, and a
round trip to the broker on an established connection is $0.22$~ms on the same host and
$0.24$~ms across machines. A genuine two-machine network is therefore \emph{faster} than
Testbed~A's ``co-located'' path, which is the sharpest statement we can offer of how much a
testbed can distort a benchmark.

Every result reported as a finding in Section~\ref{sec:results} comes from Testbed~B. Testbed~A
appears in Section~\ref{sec:firstanswer} because it produced the result set this paper is about.

\subsection{Replay and load generation}
\label{sec:replay}

Each match is preprocessed into a CSV \emph{replay plan} that records, for every event, the time
at which it should be emitted (\texttt{scripts/make\_replay\_plan.py}). A producer replays a plan
at a configurable speed-up factor; a consumer reads the events and records when they arrive.

In the concurrency experiment each of the $N$ feeds carries a \emph{distinct} real match at its
true event rate. This matters. An earlier design gave every feed the same merged ten-match plan,
which meant that raising $N$ raised the total event rate roughly tenfold per step: the
concurrency axis was covertly a throughput axis.

Replay speed is a treatment in this study rather than a convenience. Headline results are at true
real time, which is both the ecologically correct choice and, as Section~\ref{sec:audit} shows,
the only regime in which our instrument is sound. Where we report accelerated results we say so
and give their integrity status.

\subsection{What we measure}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary measure is \textbf{Time-to-Insight} (\tti{}): the interval from an event's scheduled
emission to its availability at the consumer. This is the end-to-end delay of the paper's title.
We decompose it into two parts that answer different questions
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}):

\[
\mathrm{TTI} = \underbrace{(t_{\mathrm{send}}-t_{\mathrm{sched}})}_{\text{scheduling lag}}
             + \underbrace{(t_{\mathrm{recv}}-t_{\mathrm{ack}})}_{\text{broker transport}}
             + \text{(residual)} .
\]

\emph{Scheduling lag} is the delay between when an event was due to be sent and when the producer
actually issued the send. It measures the client and the load generator. \emph{Broker transport}
is the delay between the broker acknowledging a message and the consumer receiving it. It
measures the broker. \tti{} is what a consumer experiences; transport is what distinguishes the
two products. The distinction is not cosmetic, and it is the reason this paper reaches a
different conclusion from its own first draft: for football feeds the two measures have opposite
answers, and we report both.

The acknowledgement time $t_{\mathrm{ack}}$ is recorded by the \kafka{} producer from its send
callback and by the \redis{} producer on return from \texttt{XADD}. It is therefore stamped in
the \emph{producer} process, while $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ is stamped in the \emph{consumer} process.
That is the subtraction that can go wrong.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{docs/results/figures/pipeline_schematic.pdf}
\caption{The replay pipeline, the four timestamps we record, and the intervals each measure
spans. Scheduling lag is measured entirely inside the producer process. Broker transport
subtracts a time recorded by the producer from a time recorded by the consumer, which is why it
can come out negative when either process is delayed --- the failure this paper is about.}

\subsection{The clock-integrity check}
\label{sec:gate}

Because transport subtracts a timestamp taken in one process from one taken in another, it admits
a check that no statistic can supply: the result cannot be negative. A message cannot arrive
before it is sent \cite{lamport1978time}.

We implement this as a stated rule in \texttt{scripts/clock\_integrity.py}, fixed before the final
campaign and applied to every run in the study, including runs whose results looked entirely
reasonable. A run is \textbf{rejected} if either:

\begin{enumerate}
\item more than $1\%$ of its events carry a negative value in any latency component, or
\item the median of any component is negative.
\end{enumerate}

A condition is reported as usable only if all of its runs survive. Where a condition is partly
rejected we report the surviving runs and state the retention rate, and where retention is low
enough to constitute selection we say so. The tool exits with a non-zero status when any run
fails, so a campaign script can stop on it.

\paragraph{On the choice of threshold.} The $1\%$ figure is a judgement, and we report its
consequences rather than defending it as obvious. Section~\ref{sec:threshold} gives the full
sensitivity analysis. In summary: the threshold materially changes how many individual runs are
rejected, but it does not change which conditions are usable or which conclusions survive, across
the whole range from zero tolerance to $20\%$.

\subsection{Statistical methods}
\label{sec:stat_methods}

\paragraph{Tests.} Latency distributions are non-normal, so difference tests are rank-based:
Mann--Whitney U \cite{mann1947test} per condition, Kruskal--Wallis \cite{kruskal1952use} across
concurrency levels, with rank-biserial effect sizes.

\paragraph{Estimator.} We report the Hodges--Lehmann shift \cite{hodges1963estimates} with a
percentile bootstrap interval \cite{efron1979bootstrap} as the primary estimate of the difference
between two systems, rather than a difference of means. This corrects our own earlier practice
and is forced by the data: the \kafka{} producer's scheduling lag is heavily skewed (median
$0.24$~ms but 95th percentile $88$~ms in the single-machine corpus), so a mean-based comparison
contrasts two differently contaminated estimators. The Hodges--Lehmann shift is also the estimator
consistent with the rank tests used elsewhere. Where the three procedures --- Welch, bootstrap and
Hodges--Lehmann --- disagree, we report the disagreement rather than choosing one.

\paragraph{Equivalence.} Several claims here are negative. Because failing to reject equality is
not evidence of equality, we state those claims using two one-sided tests
\cite{schuirmann1987tost,lakens2017equivalence}: the $90\%$ confidence interval of the difference
must lie entirely inside $(-\delta,+\delta)$ for the systems to be declared equivalent within
$\delta$.

\paragraph{Margins.} Margins are fixed before testing and chosen in proportion to the quantity
compared, which is a change from our earlier practice of applying one margin to every measure.
For \emph{broker transport}, a quantity of order one millisecond, $\delta = 1$~ms. For
\emph{end-to-end \tti{}}, $\delta = 40$~ms, one broadcast video frame at 25 frames per second, on
the grounds that a difference smaller than one frame cannot change what a coach, broadcaster or
model does. We report the margin, the observed difference and the interval in every case, and we
note explicitly where a stricter margin would not be met.

\paragraph{Multiple comparisons.} Holm's procedure \cite{holm1979simple} controls the family-wise
error rate at $\alpha = 0.05$ within each family. Families are declared over the design actually
executed: the four per-$N$ comparisons on transport form one family; the two Kruskal--Wallis
tests are single omnibus tests, one per system, and are not corrected; the network-delay arm forms
a separate family. Equivalence tests are pre-specified claims with their own margins and are not
members of any family.

\paragraph{Power.} Surviving samples range from 8 to 35 runs per cell. At eight runs per system
--- the $N{=}1$ cell, which the integrity check reduced most --- power is below $0.4$ even for
large effects \cite{cohen1988statistical}, and we treat that cell as descriptive and say so
wherever it appears. Cells with 19 or more runs reach power above $0.75$ for large effects.

\section{The Answer We Obtained First}
\label{sec:firstanswer}

This section reports the study as it stood before the integrity check existed. We present it in
full rather than describing it, for two reasons. It is the evidence for RQ5. And a reader cannot
judge how convincing an invalid result set can be from a summary of one.

\subsection{The result}
\label{sec:withdrawnresult}

On Testbed~A, replaying distinct real matches at ten times real speed across one, five and ten
concurrent feeds with 15 to 30 runs per cell and both producers pipelined, broker transport
separated the two systems cleanly (Table~\ref{tab:withdrawn}). \redis{} rose steadily from
$1.006$ to $1.346$~ms, an increase of $34\%$ across the range. \kafka{} was flat to within

overlap at all.

The finding was not merely significant, it was explicable, for the architectural reason set out in
Section~\ref{sec:lit_systems}. The measurement appeared to reproduce the textbook distinction
between a single-threaded server and a partitioned log, at the scale one would predict and in the
direction one would predict.

\subsection{The checks it had already passed}
\label{sec:defects}

The corpus behind Table~\ref{tab:withdrawn} was not naive. It was the product of five earlier
corrections, each of which had reversed the apparent winner, and each of which we found by the
ordinary means of investigating a result that looked wrong.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A process-relative clock.} Stamping cross-process events with Python's
\texttt{perf\_counter\_ns}, which measures from an arbitrary origin per process, injected each
run's consumer start-up offset (about two seconds) into every measurement. Fixed by using
\texttt{time.time\_ns}, a shared wall-clock epoch; transport then fell to about one millisecond.
\item \emph{A saturating replay speed.} At 120 times real speed the producer could not keep pace,
inflating scheduling lag to tens of seconds. Reduced to a nominally non-saturating ten times.
\item \emph{An asymmetric load generator.} The \kafka{} producer ran synchronously, blocking on a
broker round trip for every event, while the \redis{} producer dispatched asynchronously through
a worker pool. At one feed, median \tti{} fell from $1{,}644$~ms to $16$~ms once the \kafka{}
producer was pipelined. Both producers are now configured comparably.
\item \emph{A concurrency axis that was covertly a throughput axis.} Giving every feed the same
merged ten-match plan raised the aggregate event rate roughly tenfold per concurrency step. Fixed
by giving each feed a distinct match at its true rate.
\item \emph{A heavy tail contaminating every mean.} \kafka{}'s end-to-end 95th percentile sat at
$99$--$105$~ms at \emph{every} concurrency level while its median moved only from $1.8$ to
$7.2$~ms. A 95th percentile that ignores load is not system behaviour. Decomposition located it in
the producer's scheduling lag rather than in transport. We addressed it by moving to the
Hodges--Lehmann estimator rather than by excluding a warm-up period, which we tested and rejected
because only about a third of the slow events fall in the first $5\%$ of a run.
\end{enumerate}

We also verified that the comparison was not biased by message size or protocol: payloads are
near-identical across systems (median about 170 bytes) and serialisation costs are negligible and
comparable (about 2.3 and 2.1 microseconds for \kafka{}, 2.4 and 2.2 for \redis{}).

A sixth problem belongs here because it shaped our later protocol. Our first run of the
acknowledgement-batching experiment of Section~\ref{sec:netdelay} reported no improvement at all
--- a clean, publishable-looking refutation of our own hypothesis. In fact the treatment had never
reached the consumer: the orchestration script accepted the option but, because of a failed edit,
never passed it on. We detected this only by deliberately supplying an invalid option and
observing that the consumer did not reject it. A null result produced by a treatment that was
never applied is indistinguishable, in the data, from a genuine one. Every experiment reported
here now carries a manipulation check that stops the campaign if the treatment is not accepted,
and the consumer logs its effective configuration for each run.

The point of this list is not the corrections themselves. It is that a corpus which had survived
six of them, a fairness audit, and a full battery of rank tests, effect sizes and multiplicity
correction, was still entirely invalid --- and nothing in its output said so.

\subsection{Why it fails, and why it looked fine}

The cause is visible in \emph{which} conditions fail. Every rejected condition in the concurrency
corpus was replayed at ten times real speed; every condition replayed at true real time passes.

The mechanism follows from Section~\ref{sec:lit_clocks}. The acknowledgement time is recorded by
the producer's callback thread. When accelerated replay saturates the driver machine's CPU, that
thread is descheduled, and by the time it runs and reads the clock the consumer has already
recorded receiving the message. The result is a negative transport value. It is a timestamping
race under load, not a broken or unsynchronised clock, which is why medians stay positive at
moderate load and invert wholesale only when the machine saturates. At one hundred connections in
our connection sweep the failure becomes gross enough to see without any check at all: \kafka{}'s
median transport is reported as $-6.4$~ms.

The reason the corpus looked healthy is arithmetic. In the conditions behind
Table~\ref{tab:withdrawn}, between $5$ and $14\%$ of individual events invert. That is enough to
displace a median by a few tenths of a millisecond --- in a measurement whose entire effect is
$0.34$~ms --- and far too little to make any aggregate look wrong. Every median stays positive.
Every confidence interval stays narrow. The bias is also \emph{asymmetric} between the two
systems, because their clients record the acknowledgement differently: \kafka{} from an
asynchronous callback, \redis{} on return from a blocking command. An asymmetric bias between two
arms of a comparison manufactures a difference where none exists. The result was a large,
significant, theory-confirming effect assembled almost entirely out of instrument failure.

\subsection{How much the threshold matters}
\label{sec:threshold}

We had initially expected the distribution of inversion rates to be clearly bimodal, with sound
runs at zero and broken runs at obviously high rates, which would have made the $1\%$ threshold
an uncontroversial choice. It is not (Figure~\ref{fig:audit}a). Only $104$ of $1{,}382$ runs are
completely clean, and the remainder spread over three orders of magnitude with no natural gap.
The threshold is therefore a real decision, and we report its consequences
(Table~\ref{tab:threshold}, Figure~\ref{fig:audit}b).

Two things follow, and they point in different directions. The threshold strongly affects
\emph{how many runs} are rejected: from $92.5\%$ at zero tolerance to $19.2\%$ at a very
permissive $20\%$. But it does not affect \emph{which conditions are usable}, and therefore does
not affect any conclusion in this paper. A condition is usable only if all of its runs survive,
and even at a $5\%$ threshold the accelerated concurrency arms retain only $32$ to $77\%$ of their
runs, so no such condition qualifies at any threshold we considered. The withdrawal of
Table~\ref{tab:withdrawn} therefore holds across the entire range.

We prefer to report this than to claim a robustness the data does not show. A reader who thinks
$1\%$ too strict or too lenient can read the consequence directly from
Table~\ref{tab:threshold}.

\subsection{The property that makes this general}
\label{sec:gateproperty}

One feature of the audit deserves emphasis because it transfers beyond this study. The check is a
test against zero, so it rejects a measurement in proportion to how close that measurement sits to
zero. Our network-delay arm, where \redis{} transport is tens of \emph{seconds}, passes the check
almost perfectly --- 15 runs out of 15 at every injected delay --- on the same hardware, in the
same campaign, minutes apart from conditions that fail outright. A few milliseconds of
timestamping race is invisible against a 31-second effect and fatal against a
0.34-millisecond one.

The check therefore bites hardest exactly where the scientific question is most delicate. This
inverts a common intuition: that a large, clean, highly significant effect is the trustworthy
kind. In cross-process delay measurement, an effect of the same order as the instrument's own
noise floor is the one most likely to have been manufactured by it, and statistical significance
offers no protection, because the artefact is systematic rather than random.

We recommend that streaming benchmarks report an integrity audit alongside their results as a
matter of course, in the way a survey reports its response rate.

\subsection{What we therefore withdraw}
\label{sec:withdrawn}

We withdraw the entire Testbed~A arm: the concurrency comparison, the throughput sweep, the
synthetic network-delay arm, the acknowledgement experiment conducted there, and the
decision-staleness aggregates computed from them. On Testbed~B we withdraw the accelerated
concurrency sweep, the connection sweep above ten connections, and the three-node cluster arm,
which fails on every run in both systems and therefore supports nothing.

We report this at length rather than quietly rebuilding the paper around the surviving data,
because the withdrawn result is this study's most transferable finding. It was more publishable
than the result that replaced it.

\subsection{Where the difference actually comes from (RQ3)}

\tti{} and transport give opposite answers, and the decomposition explains why
(Figure~\ref{fig:e1}b). Of \kafka{}'s $105.5$~ms, \textbf{$102.9$~ms is producer scheduling lag},
against \redis{}'s $1.8$~ms. Broker transport is $0.84$~ms and $0.80$~ms respectively. The entire
twentyfold difference occurs before either broker is involved.

Three properties of this offset are measured, and together they rule out the obvious
explanations:

\begin{itemize}
\item It is \emph{constant}. The 50th and 95th percentiles of \tti{} differ by under $0.05$~ms
within a run, so every event pays it. It is not a tail effect.
\item It is \emph{unaffected by concurrency}: $103.0$, $103.0$, $102.8$ and $102.9$~ms at one,
nine, ten and twelve feeds. It is not contention.
\item It \emph{depends on the arrival rate}. Replaying the identical data, code and machines at
ten times real speed reduces \kafka{}'s scheduling lag from $103$~ms to between $0.19$ and
$1.01$~ms, and its \tti{} to between $1.8$ and $7.2$~ms.
\end{itemize}

The explanation most consistent with all three is that the \kafka{} client's background sender
thread must wake from idle to dispatch a record and pays a fixed cost of order 100~ms when the
producer queue has been empty --- which, at football's $0.415$ events per second, is the case
before essentially every event. Under accelerated replay events arrive closely enough that the
sender is never idle, and the cost disappears. We flag that this explanation is inferred from the
three measured properties rather than from instrumentation of the client's internals. We state it
as the leading hypothesis rather than an established mechanism; separating it from a
metadata-refresh or retry-backoff timer would require client-level tracing we did not perform.

What does not depend on the mechanism is the consequence, and it is this paper's sharpest
methodological result after the integrity check itself. \textbf{The difference between these two
systems on a football feed exists only at football's arrival rate.} A benchmark that drives them
with a dense synthetic publisher --- which is to say every benchmark in
Section~\ref{sec:lit_bench} --- measures the regime in which the difference is absent, and
reports parity. The sparse, bursty, mostly-idle pattern described in Section~\ref{sec:burstiness}
is not a nicety of ecological validity; it is the condition under which the effect exists at all.

\subsection{The condition that reverses the ordering (RQ4)}
\label{sec:netdelay}

Every result so far places consumer and broker on separate machines but on a fast private
network. We therefore injected one-way delay at both brokers using \texttt{tc netem}, at 5, 20 and
50~ms applied identically to each, and repeated the five-feed condition. The \redis{} arm passes
the integrity check in full at every delay, for the reason given in
Section~\ref{sec:gateproperty}: the effect is four orders of magnitude larger than the
instrument's noise floor.

The ordering reverses completely (Table~\ref{tab:netem}, Figure~\ref{fig:netem}). \kafka{} tracks
the injected delay almost additively, its transport rising from $5.6$ to $20.8$ to $44.9$~ms
against injected delays of 5, 20 and 50~ms. \redis{} collapses: $4.6$~seconds at 5~ms of delay,
$31.3$~seconds at 20~ms and $102.5$~seconds at 50~ms, roughly $900$, $1{,}560$ and $2{,}050$ times
the delay actually injected. No run was truncated, so this is amplification rather than loss.
Against Table~\ref{tab:latency_requirements}, \kafka{} at 20~ms of delay still meets every budget
listed; \redis{} misses all of them by four orders of magnitude.

\paragraph{The explanation, and an experiment that tests it.} Numbers of this size cannot be a
per-event cost: $102$ seconds cannot be assembled from $50$~ms applied once per event. The account
we test is a \emph{round-trip-bound consumption pattern}. Our \redis{} consumer acknowledges each
entry with \texttt{XACK} and waits for the reply before continuing --- the idiomatic pattern in
client tutorials --- so a read returning two hundred entries is followed by two hundred sequential
round trips. On a fast network this is free: a round trip on an established connection costs
$0.22$~ms, a ceiling of about $4{,}200$ exchanges per second against a demand of well under one
per second. At 20~ms it has no headroom at all. The queueing statement is elementary
\cite{kleinrock1975queueing}: once the service rate falls below the arrival rate the queue grows
without bound, and the delay is then set by the accumulated backlog rather than by the network.
\kafka{}'s consumer serves \texttt{poll()} from a prefetched buffer and commits its position on a
timer, so it has no per-record round trip to expose.

This is testable by intervention rather than observation, and we stated the criterion in advance:
if amortising the acknowledgements does not help, the account is wrong. At one feed under true
real-time replay --- the realistic football condition --- batching acknowledgements in groups of
two hundred takes median \tti{} from $4{,}138$~ms to $103$~ms, a factor of \textbf{40.2},
replicated four times with a spread of $4$~ms in the unbatched arm and $0.4$~ms in the batched one
(Table~\ref{tab:ack}).

Instrumenting the consumer's read loop confirms the mechanism and corrects its shape. We had
supposed the consumer was limited to about one message per round trip. It is not: under delay each
\texttt{XREADGROUP} returns a \emph{full} batch, a median of 106 messages, in about $32$~ms. The
constraint is entirely in the acknowledgement path. With per-message acknowledgement the consumer
spends roughly $200 \times 20$~ms $= 4$~s acknowledging each batch, issuing no reads at all
meanwhile: across a whole match it completes 13 reads, of which 4 return data, each finding a
large accumulated backlog. With acknowledgements amortised the same consumer performs 37 reads
returning a median of 16 messages each, and keeps pace.

\paragraph{An open question we do not resolve.} At five feeds under accelerated replay the same
change does nothing: $68{,}960 \rightarrow 73{,}598$~ms at 20~ms of delay and
$87{,}624 \rightarrow 92{,}386$~ms at 50~ms, both marginally \emph{worse} when batched. We report
this prominently because the obvious explanations do not survive inspection. The treatment was
applied: the campaign script runs a manipulation check that stops if the option is not accepted,
and the consumer logs its effective batch size for every run. The measurement is sound: the
\redis{} arm passes the integrity check on all 15 runs in each of the four cells. The machine is
not saturated in general: \kafka{} in the very same runs sits at $78$~ms. An earlier version of
this paper explained the null away as an integrity failure. That explanation is false and we
withdraw it.

The account we find most plausible is that at five feeds and ten times real speed the consumer is
far outside the stable regime in both arms, so the binding constraint has moved somewhere the
acknowledgement path no longer dominates. But we did not instrument the read loop in that arm and
cannot demonstrate it. We therefore claim the round-trip-bound mechanism \emph{at realistic
football load}, where we have both the intervention and the read-loop evidence, and we state
plainly that its behaviour at five times that load is unexplained. It is the most important open
question this study leaves.

\subsection{Connections and clustering}
\label{sec:conncluster}

Holding the per-feed rate at real time and varying only the number of concurrent connections,
transport stays near one millisecond for both systems at ten connections (\kafka{} $1.01$~ms,
10 of 10 runs retained; \redis{} $0.84$~ms, 7 of 10), and end-to-end delay preserves the ordering
of Section~\ref{sec:e1} ($105.4$ against $5.3$~ms). Above ten connections the check rejects both
systems, and at one hundred the failure is gross: \kafka{}'s median transport is reported as
$-6.4$~ms. We therefore report no result above ten connections for this arm.

This is a real limitation, because it is the arm that would speak to a multi-league feed platform
(Section~\ref{sec:realconcurrency}). It is also the clearest instance in this study of the check
removing an answer we wanted rather than one we disliked.

The three-node cluster arm fails on every run in both systems and supports no claim. We report its
existence and nothing from it.

\subsection{Durability}
\label{sec:durability}

Stronger durability costs delay in both systems, as PACELC predicts \cite{abadi2012pacelc}:
\kafka{}'s \texttt{acks=all} raises median \tti{} relative to \texttt{acks=1}, and \redis{}'s
\texttt{appendfsync always} raises it substantially relative to \texttt{everysec}, reflecting a
disk synchronisation on every write. The quantitative comparison was measured only on Testbed~A
and is therefore withdrawn. We report the direction, which the surviving data does not
contradict, and note that a durability replication on Testbed~B is the most obvious extension of
this work.

\subsection{Putting the numbers on a decision scale}
\label{sec:budget}

A benchmark measures transport. A decision-maker experiences everything between the action on the
pitch and the model's ability to act on it:
\[
  \text{staleness} = \underbrace{\text{annotation}}_{\text{human coding}}
  + \underbrace{\text{delivery}}_{\text{measured here}}
  + \underbrace{\text{inference}}_{\text{model}} .
\]

As set out in Section~\ref{sec:lit_football}, no peer-reviewed measurement of annotation delay
exists, so we sweep it from 1 to 30 seconds rather than adopting a vendor point estimate, and we
set inference to zero, which flatters the delivery term rather than the reverse.

At a vendor-typical 15 seconds of annotation, \kafka{}'s measured $105.5$~ms of delivery is
$0.70\%$ of end-to-end staleness and \redis{}'s $5.2$~ms is $0.03\%$; the choice between them is
worth $100.2$~ms, or $\mathbf{0.66\%}$. Inverting the relation is more informative: for delivery
to own even one percent of what a decision-maker experiences, annotation would have to complete
within $10.4$~s, and for ten percent, within $0.95$~s. The first is inside the range vendors
already claim; the second is what automated event detection from tracking data would have to
achieve.

The comparison looks different again if one asks about the brokers rather than the whole pipeline.
Broker transport of $0.84$~ms is $0.006\%$ of the same budget, and the difference between the two
brokers is $0.04$~ms, or $0.0003\%$ --- four orders of magnitude below visibility. This is the
honest form of the claim that infrastructure does not matter for football: \emph{broker} choice is
structurally irrelevant, \emph{client} configuration is worth two thirds of a percent, and a
misconfigured consumer behind a network hop is worth more than the entire annotation budget.

Expressed on a decision scale, an event delivered with delay $L$ leaves the consumer's forecast
wrong by the probability mass that event moved, for $L$ seconds \cite{kaul2012realtime}. The
largest forecast move in our corpus is a 95th-minute equaliser that shifts the win/draw/loss
distribution by $0.955$ in total variation. At \redis{}'s $5.2$~ms that event costs $0.005$
probability-seconds; at \kafka{}'s $105.5$~ms, $0.101$; and at the $31.4$~s a round-trip-bound
consumer suffers behind a 20~ms hop, about $30$ probability-seconds. The first two are
decision-irrelevant and the third is not.

We note two limits on this conversion. It changes units rather than adding inferential power:
both systems are scored against the identical model, so the ordering under it is the ordering
under delay. And the win-probability model behind it is a calibrated stand-in for the unreleased
state of the art \cite{robberechts2021bayesian} --- its expected calibration error is $0.054$
over 28{,}240 game states, but its skill over a lookup table conditioned on nothing but the
current goal difference is only $+0.026$. It should be read as a calibrated yardstick, not as a
model of in-play win probability in any stronger sense.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Which system, under which conditions}
\label{sec:which}

The question the project set out to answer has a conditional answer, and the conditions can be
known in advance (Table~\ref{tab:decision}).

\emph{For a deployment on one network at football's event rates --- the common case --- \redis{}
Streams is the better choice on delay}, by a factor of twenty end to end. The caveat is that this
is a property of the client rather than the broker, so a \kafka{} deployment that tunes its
producer out of the idle-wakeup regime should close most of the gap. We did not achieve that, and
a reader who does would find the two systems equivalent, since the brokers themselves are
equivalent to within one millisecond.

\emph{For any deployment where a consumer sits across a meaningful network, \kafka{} is the safer
choice} --- not because its broker is faster, but because its client already amortises what
\redis{}'s idiomatic consumer does per message. A \redis{} deployment can match it, and the fix is
one line, but the failure if you do not apply it is severe and invisible on a local testbed.

\emph{Neither system is under strain from football.} Concurrency up to the twelve simultaneous
matches the sport produces changes nothing for either, and both meet every published latency
budget in every configuration we could measure soundly.

\subsection{How hard each system is to configure}
\label{sec:config}

A latency comparison that ignores configuration effort misstates the engineering choice, so we
report what it actually took. The finding is symmetric, and we think it is the most practically
useful result in the paper after the integrity check.

\textbf{Each system has exactly one client setting that moves delay by one to two orders of
magnitude, and in each case the introductory example in the documentation uses the slow value.}
For \kafka{} it is producer pipelining: with one outstanding request per connection, which is what
a synchronous send example gives you, median \tti{} at one feed was $1{,}644$~ms; with
\texttt{max.in.flight} set to 64 it was $16$~ms, a factor of 103. For \redis{} it is
acknowledgement batching: the per-message \texttt{XACK} that every tutorial shows costs
$4{,}138$~ms behind a 20~ms hop, and batching two hundred at a time costs $103$~ms, a factor of
40.2.

Both share a property that makes them hard to catch: they are \emph{free on a local testbed}.
Pipelining does not matter when the broker round trip is $0.2$~ms, and neither does
acknowledgement batching. A benchmark conducted on loopback prices both at zero and will therefore
certify as equivalent two clients that differ by two orders of magnitude in deployment. This is
the practical corollary of Section~\ref{sec:gateproperty}: the conditions that make a testbed
convenient are the conditions that make it least informative.

Beyond these, the two systems differ in the \emph{shape} of their configuration surface rather
than its size. \kafka{}'s delay-relevant settings (\texttt{acks}, \texttt{linger.ms},
\texttt{max.in.flight}, \texttt{batch.size}, and the consumer poll timeout) are numerous,
documented together, and mostly safe to leave alone once pipelining is enabled. \redis{}'s are
fewer (\texttt{COUNT}, \texttt{BLOCK}, \texttt{appendfsync}, and acknowledgement granularity) but
the consequential one is not a setting at all. It is a property of how the application's read loop
is written, which no configuration audit will surface.

We also note one operational asymmetry we hit directly. Our own \redis{} cluster client had
loopback addresses hard-coded for cluster node discovery, which made a genuinely distributed
cluster impossible to test until we fixed the client. That is a defect in our code rather than in
\redis{}, but it is exactly the kind of defect a co-located testbed never reveals.

\subsection{Methodological implications}
\label{sec:methodimplications}

Three recommendations follow, in decreasing order of confidence.

First, \textbf{report a physical-consistency audit}. Any delay computed as a difference of
timestamps taken in different processes admits a sign check, and that check costs nothing. We
rejected $58\%$ of our own runs with it, and every rejected run had passed every conventional
check we knew to apply. We would encourage reviewers to ask for the audit, and authors to report
the retention rate the way a survey reports its response rate.

Second, \textbf{drive the benchmark at the domain's arrival rate, not a convenient one}. Our
central explanatory result exists only at football's $0.415$ events per second and vanishes at ten
times that. This is the opposite of the usual concern. An unrealistically \emph{slow} workload is
normally thought to risk failing to stress the system; here the realistic rate exposed a
difference that the accelerated rate concealed. Sparse, bursty, mostly-idle workloads are their
own regime.

Third, \textbf{measure at a realistic round-trip time, and treat interventions as interventions}.
The two configuration defects of Section~\ref{sec:config} are both invisible on loopback, and the
one experiment we ran without a manipulation check produced a clean false null. Both problems are
cheap to prevent and expensive to discover.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\emph{The surviving evidence is narrower than the design.} The integrity check removed the
throughput sweep, the connection sweep above ten connections, the cluster arm, the durability
quantification and the whole of Testbed~A. Two consequences are substantive. We cannot characterise
behaviour at multi-league concurrency, which is the regime a commercial feed platform occupies.
And our durability comparison is qualitative.

\emph{The $103$~ms \kafka{} offset is attributed, not proven.} We measure that it is constant,
unaffected by concurrency and dependent on arrival rate, which rules out contention and tail
effects and is consistent with an idle-sender wakeup, but we did not instrument the client
internals. If it is a property of the Python \kafka{} client rather than of \kafka{} itself, then
our end-to-end result is a statement about that client, and we say so in
Section~\ref{sec:attribution}. It reproduced across both testbeds, four concurrency levels and 164
runs, so it is not an accident of one machine; it may still be an accident of one library.

\emph{The five-feed acknowledgement null is unexplained}, as set out in
Section~\ref{sec:netdelay}. We prefer to leave it visible rather than resolve it by assumption.

\emph{The concurrency ceiling is a lower bound.} StatsBomb open data samples matches rather than
enumerating league-seasons, and the structural bound is per league. Our result to twelve feeds
describes a consumer of a single competition.

\emph{The decision-scale conversion is a lens, not a second measurement.} It changes units, adds
no inferential power, and the win-probability model behind it is calibrated but modest.

\emph{Replay is not live capture.} Feeds replay recorded matches, and the upstream annotation
pipeline is not modelled --- deliberately, since it is the dominant term and no peer-reviewed
measurement of it exists. Our figures bound the delivery contribution to staleness, not end-to-end
feed freshness.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to benchmark \redis{} Streams against Apache \kafka{} for real-time football feeds, on
open data, at concurrency levels derived from the sport rather than chosen by hand. We obtained a
complete answer, and it was physically impossible.

The audit that discovered this is the paper's principal contribution. A negative broker transport
--- a message received before it was sent --- is proof of instrument failure, and checking for it
rejected $1{,}321$ of our $2{,}266$ runs, including every condition behind a large, significant,
theory-confirming result about \redis{} slowing down under concurrency. The rejected corpus had
passed every conventional check: plausible medians, narrow intervals, large effect sizes, the
architecturally expected direction, and six prior rounds of correction. It failed only against
arithmetic. We report the check as a short, open, reusable script, and we note the property that
makes it general: because it is a test against zero, it bites hardest exactly where the measured
effect is smallest, which is to say on the delicate comparisons that benchmark papers exist to
make.

Inside the check, the original question has an answer. At every concurrency level football
produces, \redis{} Streams delivers a median end-to-end delay of $5.2$~ms against \kafka{}'s
$105.5$~ms, and neither system slows down as matches are added. But the brokers themselves are
statistically equivalent to within one millisecond. The twentyfold difference lies entirely in the
client send path, and it exists only at football's sparse arrival rate: replay the same data ten
times faster and it disappears. The ordering reverses behind a network hop, where \kafka{} tracks
the injected delay while a round-trip-bound \redis{} consumer turns $20$~ms into $31$~seconds ---
until its acknowledgements are batched, which recovers a factor of $40.2$ at realistic load and,
unexplainedly, nothing at five times that load.

The practical message is therefore about configuration rather than product. Each system has one
client setting worth one to two orders of magnitude in delay; in each case the introductory
example uses the slow value; and both are free on a local testbed, which is where such settings
are usually evaluated. Choose \redis{} Streams for a football feed on one network, choose \kafka{}
if a consumer sits across a network you do not control, and in either case measure at the
domain's real arrival rate, at a realistic round-trip time, and with a check that your timestamps
obey causality.
