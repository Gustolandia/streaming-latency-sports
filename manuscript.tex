\begin{abstract}
This study presents a comprehensive, reproducible benchmark suite that empirically evaluates how streaming architecture choices impact the timeliness and reliability of live football analytics. Using the publicly available StatsBomb open dataset (2003-2023), we simulate realistic match concurrency scenarios with open-source load-generation scripts to compare end-to-end lag between Apache \kafka{} and \redis{} Streams.

Unlike traditional comparisons, we frame the investigation around sports-specific decision-making requirements: How quickly can coaching staff, broadcasters, and betting platforms act on live event data? Our methodology introduces the Time-to-Insight (\tti{}) metric, which measures the interval from event occurrence to analytic output availability.

Using 40,660 real football events across 11 matches, and after correcting two instrumentation defects in earlier measurements (a process-relative cross-process clock and a saturating load generator), our corrected multi-broker matrix shows Apache \kafka{} achieving significantly lower median \tti{} than \redis{} Streams in both single-broker and clustered deployments (Holm--Bonferroni-corrected Mann--Whitney U, Cohen's $d=-1.18$ overall, large effect). Single-broker deployments outperform clustered ones for both systems, and stronger durability settings (\kafka{} \texttt{acks=all}; \redis{} \texttt{appendfsync always}) measurably increase latency. These findings reverse the conclusion of the initial, contaminated analysis and underscore the importance of measurement validity in cross-process streaming benchmarks.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The rapid digitization of sports has created an insatiable demand for real-time analytics. Coaching staff require instant tactical insights, broadcasters demand immediate highlight generation, and betting platforms need sub-second odds updates. At the heart of these systems lies the streaming infrastructure that transports event data from source to analytics pipeline.

Two dominant technologies have emerged: Apache \kafka{} (originally developed at LinkedIn) and \redis{} Streams (introduced in Redis 5.0). \kafka{} is a distributed event streaming platform designed for high-throughput, fault-tolerant data pipelines. \redis{} Streams provides a lightweight, in-memory streaming solution with persistent storage capabilities.

While numerous studies have compared these technologies, the literature lacks sports-specific evaluations. Sports data presents distinct challenges: high-frequency bursts during active play, ordered event delivery requirements, and strict latency constraints.


\subsection{Sports-Specific Latency Requirements}
\label{sec:sports_requirements}

Real-time sports analytics imposes domain-specific latency constraints that vary by use case and stakeholder. As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:latency_requirements}, different applications demand fundamentally different performance characteristics. Betting platforms require sub-500ms latency for in-play odds updates, as even a 2-second delay can erode user trust and cost millions in missed wagers \cite{v2solutions2025,ververica2025}. Broadcasting applications can tolerate up to 3 seconds for live video synchronization \cite{dolby2025,promwad2025}, while coaching decision systems need $<$500ms to enable real-time tactical adjustments \cite{pappas2020real,opta2023}.

The concept of an ``actionability window'' formalizes this requirement: the maximum latency within which insights can still influence decisions. Using an exponential decay model ($Value = V_0 \times e^{-\lambda \times latency}$), we estimate that at 500ms latency, only \textasciitilde60\% of coaching insight value remains, dropping to \textasciitilde13\% at 2000ms. This motivates our focus on minimizing Time-to-Insight (TTI) below sports-specific thresholds.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

This study addresses four primary research questions that bridge the gap between streaming systems theory and sports domain requirements:

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1:] How does streaming architecture choice (Apache \kafka{} vs \redis{} Streams) impact Time-to-Insight (TTI) for real-time sports data processing, and what are the underlying mechanisms driving this difference?
\item[RQ2:] How does concurrency level (N=5, 10, 20 concurrent feeds) affect TTI, throughput, and resource utilization for each streaming architecture under realistic sports workloads, and what are the scalability limits?
\item[RQ3:] What is the trade-off between latency (TTI), data consistency (match rate, ordering guarantees), and throughput across different streaming architectures, configurations, and persistence settings?
\item[RQ4:] How do streaming system performance characteristics (TTI, throughput, resource usage) vary across different sports event scenarios (S1--S5), and what are the underlying sport-specific factors driving these differences?
\end{description}

These questions are motivated by fundamental gaps in the literature. While industry benchmarks suggest \redis{} offers 2--5$\times$ lower latency than \kafka{} \cite{medium_benchmark_2025,github_benchmark_2025}, there is no sports-specific empirical validation. The CAP theorem \cite{brewer2012cap} and its extension PACELC \cite{abadi2012pacelc} predict inherent trade-offs between consistency and latency, but these have not been quantified in the streaming context. Furthermore, the unique characteristics of sports data---variable event frequencies, burst patterns, and strict actionability windows---demand domain-specific investigation.

From a theoretical perspective, RQ1 tests the PACELC theorem's ``Else'' clause: when no network partitions exist, systems must trade latency for consistency. Technically, RQ2 examines the scalability of both architectures under realistic sports workloads. Economically, RQ3 quantifies the value of consistency versus the cost of latency. From the sports domain perspective, RQ4 determines which architecture is optimal for which sport.


\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

For each research question, we formulate testable hypotheses grounded in theoretical frameworks and industry observations.

\paragraph{RQ1: Architecture Impact}
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{01}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_Kafka} = \mu_{TTI\_Redis}$ (No difference in median TTI between architectures)
\item[$\mathbf{H_{11}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_Kafka} > \mu_{TTI\_Redis}$ (Redis has significantly lower median TTI)
\item[$\mathbf{H_{21}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_Kafka} < \mu_{TTI\_Redis}$ (Kafka has lower median TTI)
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric, data violates normality)  
\noindent\textbf{Rationale:} Industry benchmarks \cite{medium_benchmark_2025,github_benchmark_2025} and our S2 results show \redis{} achieving 40--55\% lower TTI. The distributed log architecture of \kafka{} (disk-based, replication) theoretically incurs higher latency than \redis{}'s in-memory design. PACELC theorem predicts latency advantage for AP systems (\redis{}) over CP systems (\kafka{}) when partitions are absent.  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} $\mathbf{H_{11}}$ (Redis significantly outperforms Kafka on TTI)  
\noindent\textbf{Effect Size:} Large (Cohen's $d > 0.8$ based on S2 data)  
\noindent\textbf{Power:} $>0.99$ at $\alpha=0.05$ with current sample size (40--50 runs per group)


\paragraph{RQ2: Concurrency Scaling}
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{02}}$:] TTI is independent of concurrency level N
\item[$\mathbf{H_{12}}$:] TTI increases monotonically with concurrency level N
\item[$\mathbf{H_{22}}$:] TTI remains constant across N=5, 10, 20 (excellent scaling)
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} Kruskal-Wallis test (non-parametric one-way ANOVA)  
\noindent\textbf{Rationale:} Both \kafka{} and \redis{} are designed for horizontal scaling. Our S2 results (250 runs) showed stable TTI across N=5, 10, 20. Little's Law ($L = \lambda \times W$) suggests that with proper resource provisioning, latency should remain stable as concurrency increases.  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} $\mathbf{H_{22}}$ (Excellent scaling with constant TTI)  
\noindent\textbf{Effect Size:} Small ($d < 0.2$ expected)  
\noindent\textbf{Power:} $\approx0.30$ for detecting $d=0.2$ (sample size may need expansion)


\paragraph{RQ3: Latency-Consistency Trade-off}
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{03}}$:] Match rate = 100\% for all configurations
\item[$\mathbf{H_{13}}$:] Match rate $> 99.9\%$ for all configurations
\item[$\mathbf{H_{23}}$:] Match rate varies by configuration
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test  
\noindent\textbf{Rationale:} Our S2 results achieved 100\% match rate across all 250 runs. \kafka{} with acks=all provides exactly-once semantics, while \redis{} with consumer groups provides at-least-once. Both should achieve near-perfect data delivery.  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} $\mathbf{H_{13}}$ (All configurations achieve $>99.9\%$ match rate)

\noindent Additionally, we test the consistency-latency trade-off:
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{31}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_acks=all} > \mu_{TTI\_acks=1}$ (Kafka: strong consistency costs latency)
\item[$\mathbf{H_{32}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_AOF=always} > \mu_{TTI\_AOF=1s}$ (Redis: durability costs latency)
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} Paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} Both true (stronger guarantees = higher latency)


\paragraph{RQ4: Sports-Specific Performance}
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{04}}$:] TTI distribution is the same across all scenarios
\item[$\mathbf{H_{14}}$:] TTI distribution differs by scenario
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} Kolmogorov-Smirnov test  
\noindent\textbf{Rationale:} Scenarios S1--S5 have different event frequencies and burst patterns. Queueing theory predicts that systems with higher arrival rate variance (burstier scenarios) will experience higher latency variance. Sport-specific characteristics should manifest in measurable performance differences.  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} $\mathbf{H_{14}}$ (TTI differs by scenario)

\noindent Additionally, we test scenario-specific hypotheses:
\begin{description}
\item[$\mathbf{H_{41}}$:] $\mu_{TTI\_S5} > \mu_{TTI\_S1}$ (Higher event frequency $\rightarrow$ higher latency)
\item[$\mathbf{H_{42}}$:] $\sigma_{TTI\_S5} > \sigma_{TTI\_S1}$ (Higher burstiness $\rightarrow$ higher variance)
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Test:} One-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test  
\noindent\textbf{Expected:} Both true (scenario characteristics affect performance)


\paragraph{Statistical Framework}
All hypotheses will be tested using the framework summarized in Section~\ref{sec:stat_methods}. We apply Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (controlling Family-Wise Error Rate at $\alpha=0.05$), calculate Cohen's $d$ for effect sizes, report 95\% confidence intervals, and conduct power analysis for sample size justification. Non-parametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis) will be used when normality or equal variance assumptions are violated.


\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}

Real-time data processing is essential across domains from finance to IoT. Sports analytics represents a demanding application due to high event frequency, low latency requirements, and temporal consistency needs.

The CAP theorem establishes that distributed systems cannot simultaneously guarantee consistency, availability, and partition tolerance. \kafka{} prioritizes availability and partition tolerance through its distributed log architecture, achieving high throughput via batching. \redis{} Streams leverages in-memory structures to minimize latency, trading throughput for reduced delay.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{System Architecture}
Our benchmark system comprises four components:
\begin{itemize}
\item Replay Plan: Pre-processed StatsBomb event data with scheduled timestamps
\item Producer: Python script sending events to message broker
\item Broker: \kafka{} or \redis{} Streams handling message distribution
\item Consumer: Python script receiving events and logging timestamps
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dataset}
StatsBomb open dataset (2003-2023): 40,660 real football events across 11 matches.

\subsection{Metrics}
Primary metric: Time-to-Insight (TTI). Reported at percentiles: p50, p95, p99, max.

\subsection{Statistical Methods}
\label{sec:stat_methods}

We employ a rigorous statistical framework to test all hypotheses and ensure valid inferences.

\paragraph{Multiple Comparisons Correction}
With approximately 50 hypothesis tests across all research questions, we apply the \textbf{Holm-Bonferroni method} \cite{holm1979simple} to control the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) at $\alpha = 0.05$. This step-down procedure is more powerful than the standard Bonferroni correction while maintaining FWER control.

\paragraph{Effect Size Calculation}
For all significant findings, we report \textbf{Cohen's $d$} for comparing two means:
\begin{equation*}
Cohen's\; d = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_2}{s_{pooled}},\quad s_{pooled} = \sqrt{\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2 + (n_2-1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}}
\end{equation*}
Interpretation: $d = 0.2$ (small), $d = 0.5$ (medium), $d = 0.8$ (large) \cite{cohen1988statistical}.

\paragraph{Confidence Intervals}
We report 95\% confidence intervals for all mean differences using the t-distribution:
\begin{equation*}
CI = \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 \pm t_{\alpha/2, df} \times \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Power Analysis}
We conduct both \textit{a priori} and \textit{post hoc} power analysis. With our current sample of 40--50 runs per group, we can detect effect size $d = 0.58$ at $\alpha = 0.05$ with power = 0.8. For smaller effects ($d = 0.5$), power is approximately 0.72; for $d = 0.4$, power drops to 0.54.

\paragraph{Assumption Verification}
We verify statistical assumptions for all tests:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Normality:} Shapiro-Wilk test; Q-Q plots for visual inspection
\item \textbf{Equal Variance:} Levene's test; F-test for two groups
\item \textbf{Non-parametric Alternatives:} Mann-Whitney U (t-test), Kruskal-Wallis (ANOVA), Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired t-test)
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Measurement validity note.} An initial analysis was invalidated by two
instrumentation defects, since corrected: (i) the producer and consumer timestamped events
with a process-relative monotonic clock, so cross-process latencies were inflated by each
run's process-launch offset ($\sim$2~s); and (ii) a synchronous producer saturated at the
$120\times$ replay speed, inflating scheduling lag to tens of seconds. After switching to a
shared wall-clock (\texttt{time.time\_ns}) and a non-blocking producer, all runs were
regenerated; the results below reflect the corrected measurements.

$2\times2$ backend/configuration matrix (5 scenarios, 3 replications per cell, single feed).
Contrary to widely-cited industry comparisons, \kafka{} achieves significantly lower TTI
than \redis{} in every configuration.

\subsection{Consistency--Latency Trade-off (H31, H32)}

Stronger durability guarantees cost latency, as hypothesised. For \kafka{},
\texttt{acks=all} raises median TTI to 464~ms versus 346~ms for \texttt{acks=1}. For
\redis{}, synchronising the append-only file on every write (\texttt{appendfsync always})
raises median TTI to $\sim$14{,}600~ms versus 1{,}290~ms for \texttt{everysec} --- a far
larger penalty, reflecting per-write disk synchronisation.

\subsection{Scenario Sensitivity (RQ4)}

TTI varies significantly across scenarios for \kafka{} (Kruskal--Wallis
$p_{\text{adj}}=0.017$) but not significantly for \redis{} ($p_{\text{adj}}=0.080$).

\subsection{Concurrency Scaling (RQ2)}

Under true multi-feed concurrency on a single broker ($N$ parallel feeds replaying the
s2sf12 scenario), median TTI increases monotonically with $N$ for both backends, rejecting
the hypothesis of concurrency-independent latency: \kafka{} rises from 8.3~s ($N{=}5$) to
13.1~s ($N{=}10$) to 32.9~s ($N{=}20$), and \redis{} from 4.2~s to 8.3~s to 16.7~s.
Crucially, this high-concurrency regime \emph{reverses} the single-feed ordering: \redis{}
sustains roughly half the TTI of \kafka{} as $N$ grows, even though single-feed \kafka{} is
faster (Section~\ref{sec:results}). The optimal backend is therefore regime-dependent ---
\kafka{} for isolated low-latency feeds, \redis{} for many concurrent feeds on shared
infrastructure.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The corrected measurements reverse the conclusion suggested by the initial,
instrumentation-contaminated analysis. With a shared wall-clock and a non-saturating
producer, \kafka{} delivers lower end-to-end latency than \redis{} Streams for this
workload, particularly in the clustered configuration where \redis{} Cluster incurs
substantial cross-node overhead. This single-feed advantage, however, \emph{reverses} under
high concurrency (RQ2): with many parallel feeds on a shared broker, \redis{} sustains lower
TTI than \kafka{}, so the practical recommendation is workload-dependent. The episode also
underscores a methodological lesson: cross-process latency benchmarks must use a clock that
shares an epoch across processes, and the load generator must not itself become the
bottleneck.

For sports actionability, single-broker \kafka{} (median TTI 426~ms) approaches the
$<$500~ms coaching threshold, whereas the clustered and \redis{} configurations exceed it
under this $120\times$ compressed-replay load. Durability settings impose a measurable
latency cost (H31, H32), most acutely for \redis{} \texttt{appendfsync always}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Each run is a single feed; true multi-feed concurrency (RQ2) and
the S3 correction-propagation phase are being regenerated with the corrected
instrumentation. Replay at $120\times$ stresses the pipeline beyond real-time event rates,
so absolute latencies should be read in that light. A post-hoc power analysis indicates the
study is adequately powered for the large effects observed but underpowered for small
effects.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Once a cross-process clock defect and a producer-saturation defect are corrected, Apache
\kafka{} provides lower Time-to-Insight than \redis{} Streams for \emph{isolated} real-time
sports feeds, in both single-broker and clustered deployments, with single-broker fastest.
Under \emph{heavy multi-feed concurrency}, however, the ordering reverses in favour of
\redis{}, and TTI grows with the number of feeds for both systems. Stronger durability
settings increase latency for both. These corrected results overturn the project's initial
finding, show that the optimal backend is workload-dependent, and highlight the importance
of measurement validity in streaming benchmarks.
