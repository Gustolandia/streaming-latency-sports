\begin{abstract}
Engineers choose message brokers, which carry data between programs, partly by latency
benchmarks that now report latencies below a millisecond. At that scale, when a program reads the
clock matters. After a broker writes a message, it acknowledges it to the producer and delivers it
to the consumer. On one machine and one clock, the two readings should coincide. Measured, their
difference spreads over milliseconds, and part of it falls below zero. The cause is scheduling. A
thread must wait for a processor core before it can read the clock, and the thread that reads the
acknowledgment can wait longer than the message takes to reach the consumer. How often this
happens obeys laws we derive and test by manipulation. It rises with load, depends on how the load
is placed, and falls when the clock-reading threads run at real-time priority and as the timed
path outgrows the scheduler's slice, which the processor count sets. An industry-wide audit finds
such values discarded uncounted or never measured, and a filter on millisecond clocks that
deletes the fastest messages too. We give checks, and measure what most of them buy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{any} online services move their data through a message broker, a service
that takes each message from the program that publishes it, the \emph{producer}, and delivers it
to the program that receives it, the \emph{consumer} (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}a). Engineers choose
among brokers partly by latency benchmarks~\cite{dobbelaere2017kafka}, which time each message
from producer to consumer and now report latencies below a millisecond~\cite{indexdev2026brokers}. At that scale, a
thread's wait for a processor core can exceed the latency itself. A team that ranks brokers or
promises a response time on such a number may be acting on how the number was measured, not on
the broker.

We study one difference that, in the ideal case, is zero. Once the broker has written a message,
it returns an acknowledgment to the producer and delivers the message to the consumer. Both
leave after the same write. When the producer and the consumer run on one machine and read its
one clock, the producer's reading of the acknowledgment, $t_{\mathrm{ack}}$, and the consumer's
reading of the message, $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$, should coincide. We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The distribution} (Section~\ref{sec:finding}). On one clock, $S = t_{\mathrm{recv}}
- t_{\mathrm{ack}}$ is spread over milliseconds, and $\spanNegAckPct\%$ of it falls below zero.
Nothing arrived early: no latency timed from the \emph{publish call}, the moment the producer
hands the message over, falls below zero.
\item \textbf{The mechanism, and its laws} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). A thread must wait for
a core before it can read the clock, and the thread that reads the acknowledgment can wait longer
than the message takes to reach the consumer. How often that happens rises with load and depends
on where the load is placed, falls as the timed path lengthens, and has the timescale of the
scheduler's slice, which the kernel sets from the CPU count. \emph{Real-time priority}, which runs
the threads that read the clock ahead of every thread at the default \emph{normal priority}, cut
it $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$ on \kafka{} and $\rtRedisFactorLow$--$\rtRedisFactorHigh\times$ on \redis{} at unchanged load.
\item \textbf{The industry-wide audit} (Section~\ref{sec:external}). \harnessSilentWordCap{} of
the \harnessAuditedWord{} tools we read at source dispose of such values without counting them.
\toolsOneClockWordCap{} of the \toolsRunWord{} we ran take both timestamps in one process, so
they never measure a \emph{one-way latency}, the time from one process to another. On a
millisecond clock, a filter that keeps only latencies above zero, a \emph{positivity filter}, also
deletes the fastest messages, and no public report we audited says how many samples it kept.
\item \textbf{What to do} (Section~\ref{sec:practice}). Checks for benchmark authors and
readers, each with what it buys where we measured it. Better clock synchronization cures neither
problem.
\end{enumerate}

\section{How a Message Is Timed}
\label{sec:sysmodel}
\label{sec:method}

A producer process publishes messages to a broker at a paced rate, and a consumer process reads
them back. Each message crosses two legs (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}a). Leg~1 carries it to the
broker, which writes it to its log and returns an acknowledgment along leg~1, and leg~2 carries
it on to the consumer. Three timestamps bracket its path: $t_{\mathrm{pub}}$, read immediately
before the publish call; $t_{\mathrm{ack}}$, read when the producer learns that the broker has
accepted the message; and $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$, read when the consumer holds it. We call any
difference of two timestamps a \emph{span}. Three spans follow:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{pub}}, \qquad
A \equiv t_{\mathrm{ack}} - t_{\mathrm{pub}}, \\
S &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{ack}} \;=\; D - A .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
Broker benchmarks call $D$ the \emph{end-to-end latency}, the message's time from the publish
call to the consumer, and $A$ the \emph{publish latency}, the producer's wait to hear that the
broker has it~\cite{openmessaging2018}. $S$ compares the acknowledgment's return along leg~1 with
the message's trip along leg~2. A benchmark that starts its clock at the acknowledgment, as our
own first one did, reports $S$ as the message's latency.

Unless the text says otherwise, the measurements come from four Oracle Cloud virtual machines on
a private network. Three run the brokers, \kafka{} and \redis{} Streams. The fourth runs the
producer and the consumer as separate CPython processes that read one clock, so nothing here
depends on synchronizing clocks. Its kernel is Linux~6.8, whose scheduler is EEVDF (Earliest
Eligible Virtual Deadline First)~\cite{stoica1996proportional}. The producer replays StatsBomb's open football event
data~\cite{statsbomb2023} at a chosen publish rate, and nothing below depends on its content
beyond message size and rate. Each message is a JSON record of an event's identifiers and
timestamps, $\workloadBytesLo$--$\workloadBytesHi$ bytes, which one experiment pads by up to
$\workloadPadMaxKB$~KB. A producer publishes $\workloadRateLo$--$\workloadRateHi$ messages per
second in nine runs out of ten, and up to $\workloadFeedsMax$ producers run at once.
\emph{Utilization}\label{def:rho} $\rho$ is the fraction of
processor time busy on the host over a run, and a \emph{background load}, processor work run
beside the clients, sets it.\label{def:vocabulary} A \emph{condition} is one point of the
experimental design: a broker, a publish rate, a message size, a background load and how often
the consumer confirms what it has read. A \emph{run} executes a condition once, a
\emph{replicate} is a further run of it, and a \emph{campaign} is a set of runs designed
together. Where an experiment changes one variable and holds the rest, each value it gives that
variable is a \emph{configuration}. A later campaign ran on three pairs of Microsoft Azure machines, two
x86 and one Neoverse~N2. It was \emph{registered}: its predictions were committed in public
before the runs that test them~\cite{nosek2018preregistration}. Supplement~S1.4 lists which runs
each number rests on.


\section{The Distribution of $S$}
\label{sec:finding}

In the ideal case $S$ is close to zero, because its two readings follow one write and are taken
on one machine and one clock. Measured, it is spread over milliseconds, and part of it falls below
zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoways}). Over the $\spanEvents$ messages of the $\spanRuns$ cloud runs that
produced matched records, $\spanNegAck$ values of $S$, $\spanNegAckPct\%$, fall below zero
(excluding $\backlogLate$ messages delayed by a testbed fault; Supplement~S1.5). We call this
share the \emph{negative-span rate}. It pools runs at every load we ran, each weighted
by its messages. Per run it goes from zero, in $\spanRunsNoNegative$ runs, to
$\spanRunRateMaxPct\%$, with a median of $\spanRunRateMedianPct\%$, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}
shows what moves it. Both brokers show it, \kafka{} on
$\spanKafkaNegAckPct\%$ of its $\spanKafkaEvents$ messages and \redis{} on
$\spanRedisNegAckPct\%$ of its $\spanRedisEvents$. The registered campaign found it again in
\kafka{}'s own Java client, at a lower rate, and on Neoverse~N2 processors as on x86
(Supplement~S6.2).

and consumer on one node, was told that the clocks were skewed between
nodes~\cite{omb_issue216}. On one clock there is no skew to blame.

\section{The Mechanism and Its Laws}
\label{sec:mechanism}

A thread must wait for a core before it can read the clock. That wait is its \emph{scheduling
delay}\label{def:scheddelay}, the time a thread that is ready to run spends queued before a core
runs it, which real-time Linux measures as scheduling latency~\cite{bristot2025timerlat}. We show by \emph{manipulation}, moving one variable with the rest held fixed, that this
wait puts $S$ below zero, and state the laws that govern it: when $S$ falls below zero, how
often, and on what timescale.

\subsection{When $S$ falls below zero}
\label{sec:proxy}
\label{sec:model}
$D$ and $A$ are causal chains. Each starts at the publish call, which precedes the broker's
write, which precedes both the acknowledgment and the delivery. $S$ is not a chain: its two
readings are two branches of one cause, the write, and neither has to come first. Since
Equation~\ref{eq:model} holds for every message, whatever the signs,
\begin{equation}
S < 0 \quad\text{if and only if}\quad A > D .
\label{eq:negspan}
\end{equation}
If neither thread waited, $S$ would equal the \emph{margin}\label{def:margin} $m$, the time the
message still needs to reach the consumer once the acknowledgment has reached the producer.
Each reading is late by its thread's wait for a core (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}b), so $S$ falls below
zero when the acknowledging thread's wait, $\delta_{\mathrm{ack}}$, outlasts the margin and the
consumer's own wait, $\delta_{\mathrm{recv}}$, together.


\subsection{How often, and what moves the rate}
\label{sec:twostate}
\label{sec:mixture}
Each thread that reads the clock is either running, in which case its reading is late by a narrow
jitter, or waiting for a core. From an idle host to the heaviest load before its processors are
fully busy, the negative-span rate grows $\invGrowth\times$ but the jitter's width only
$\coreGrowth\times$, so load mostly changes how often the thread is waiting. Write $p$ for that
probability, the thread's \emph{waiting probability}\label{def:occupancy}, and $G$ for the chance
that the rest of its wait, the \emph{residual wait}, outlasts a given time. To leading order,
when the consumer's own wait is short,
\begin{equation}
\Pr\bigl[\,S<0 \mid m\,\bigr] \;\approx\; p\,G(m) ,
\label{eq:occupancy}
\end{equation}
the waiting probability times the chance that a residual wait outlasts the margin. Load, its
placement and priority move $p$, and the path's length moves the margin.

\textbf{Priority.} Real-time priority for the threads that read the clock should cut their
waiting probability while utilization stays fixed. Every manipulation ran both brokers, $25$ runs
a configuration on each (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). Across \rtPairsWord{} matched pairs from
$\rtRhoLow$ to $\rtRhoHigh\%$ utilization, real-time priority cut the rate $\rtFactorLow$- to
$\rtFactorHigh$-fold on \kafka{} and $\rtRedisFactorLow$- to $\rtRedisFactorHigh$-fold on
\redis{}. Every pair had disjoint intervals, and achieved utilization matched to within
$\manipWorst$ (Supplement~S3.1). That refutes any account in which utilization alone sets the
rate, including the queueing form we had ourselves adopted. In the registered campaign, on other
machines, real-time priority removed the values below zero on \kafka{} in every run, and load
raised the rate in all five \kafka{} campaigns that tested it (Supplement~S6.2).

\textbf{Placement.} With the background load concentrated on six of the \testbedCpusWord{} cores
or spread over all of them, two configurations reached the same utilization, $\rho =
\mechGeomRho$, and on both brokers they gave different rates (Supplement~S3.2). On \kafka{} the
spread load gave the higher rate, $\GeomOrigFactor$-fold [bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\placeKafkaOrigCI$], and
$\GeomReplFactor$-fold [bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\placeKafkaReplCI$] in a full replication.
Spreading the load leaves no core free, so a woken thread waits more often. On \redis{} it was the concentrated
load that gave more values below zero, so where the load is placed matters on both brokers, in
opposite directions; we have not tested why.

\textbf{Path length.} A wait of a given size overlaps a short path almost entirely and a long one
hardly at all. Padding the payload lengthens the path without touching the scheduler. On \kafka{}
it raised the median of $S$ $\payloadTransportFactorRound$-fold while utilization varied by
$\payloadRhoSpread$, and the rate fell $\payloadRateFall$-fold; on \redis{} the rate fell
$\pathRedisOrig$-fold. That is the direction $G$ predicts, and the opposite of what stress on the
host would cause (Supplement~S3.3).

\textbf{An independent check.} A kernel trace of the scheduler's wakeup and switch
events~\cite{gregg2016runqlat} times the wait directly and shares no probe or data with the
application's timestamps. It counts the threads of both brokers' runs, so it is compared with
both brokers' rate. With nothing fitted, the share of traced wakeups that waited longer than
$0.5$~ms, our estimate of the margin in those runs, stands to that rate
at ratios of \tracedPooledRatios{} in the \tracedPooledWord{} normal-priority configurations traced
(Supplement~S3.5). The registered campaign timed each acknowledgment's own wait. Of the
readings below zero, $\recMostlyWaitingLo$--$\recMostlyWaitingHi\%$ had spent most of the time
between the reply's arrival and the timestamp waiting for a core. The share of acknowledgments
whose wait outlasted the margin stood to the rate at medians of
$\recOwnWaitRatioLo$--$\recOwnWaitRatioHi$ (Supplement~S6.2).

\subsection{The timescale the slice sets}
\label{sec:tail}
A thread woken while another holds the core waits out what is left of that thread's
\emph{slice}, the time the scheduler lets a thread run at a turn, up to a whole one. The kernel
sets the base slice $s$ from the number of CPUs $n$, with a cap:
\begin{equation}
s \;=\; s_0\times\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2\min(n,8)\rfloor\bigr),
\label{eq:slice}
\end{equation}
where $s_0$ is $0.75$~ms in Linux~6.8 and $\sliceConstantMs$~ms after a change of
2025~\cite{zhou2025slice}, which our distribution's kernels carry. That gives $\sliceFactor
\times \sliceConstantMs$~ms $= \baseSliceMs$~ms on our hosts' \testbedCpusWord{} CPUs. The traced waits carry that timescale. Over $\tracedEvents$ wakeups, their
counts have \tracedModesWord{} local maxima, which survive a $\stallCoarsenFactor\times$ widening
of the buckets. The last sits in the $\tracedModeLo$--$\tracedModeHi$~ms bucket that contains the
slice, with $\tracedModeShare\%$ of all wakeups, $\tracedModeRatio\times$ the bucket below it, and
above it the counts collapse (Supplement~S3.7). The trace counts every wait, including those that
begin when a thread is preempted, which last about a whole slice. The thread that reads the
acknowledgment is instead woken by the reply. The registered campaign timed that thread's own
wait for every acknowledgment, and a reading made after its verdicts found it to be the rest of a
slice (Fig.~\ref{fig:residual}).

The CPU count moves the slice. In the registered campaign the slice read back at 2, 4 and 8 CPUs
as Equation~\ref{eq:slice} gives with $s_0 = \sliceConstantMs$~ms. On \kafka{}, at the one CPU
count whose slice is at least its shortest end-to-end latency, the end-to-end latency at which the
rate falls moved with the slice (Supplement~S6.2).

A whole slice is $\sliceOverDeliveryLo$--$\sliceOverDeliveryHi$ times the per-condition median
end-to-end latencies we measured, $\condDeliveryLoMs$--$\condDeliveryHiMs$~ms, and the margin is
shorter than the end-to-end latency, so the slice lands on the scale of the paths now
benchmarked. Read together, Equations~\ref{eq:negspan}, \ref{eq:occupancy} and~\ref{eq:slice}
say what the rate depends on. It rises with the waiting probability $p$, which load raises, the
placement of the load moves, and real-time priority lowers. It falls as the margin outgrows the
residual wait, whose scale the slice sets and the CPU count moves. On our kernel, any host with at
least as many CPUs as ours has a slice of $\baseSliceMs$~ms, so a single wait can outlast any
margin shorter than that.

\subsection{Tools read at source}
\label{sec:tools}
We read \harnessAuditedWord{} measurement tools at source, across \harnessLanguagesWord{}
languages and \harnessVendorsWord{} vendors, and classified them with one program whose
verdicts, each with its source line, are in the supplement's tool registry (Supplement~S5).
\harnessSilentWordCap{} dispose of a value at or below zero without counting it, in
\harnessDisposalClassesWord{} ways: a filter drops it, the tool's own library refuses it, or
another value replaces it. Replacement hides best, because nothing looks missing: \texttt{fio}
and \texttt{btt} substitute zero and one nanosecond, and RabbitMQ PerfTest takes the absolute
value~\cite{rabbitmq_perftest}. Until June 2026 PerfTest also kept only values above zero, which in
its millisecond mode deleted every trip shorter than a millisecond. Apache \kafka{}'s own
end-to-end tool rounds each latency down to whole milliseconds, so on a path faster than a
millisecond every percentile it reports is zero~\cite{kafka_tools}. Only \texttt{emqtt-bench},
whose histogram keeps every sample~\cite{emqtt_bench}, and Rezolus, whose scheduler sampler has
counted its discards since August 2026~\cite{rezolus}, keep the evidence.

\subsection{Tools run}
\label{sec:toolsrun}
Reading a tool shows what it does with a value below zero, not whether it ever meets one. The
registered campaign therefore ran \toolsRunWord{} benchmarking tools against a broker on a second
machine, \toolsRoundsWord{} rounds each, with predictions fixed from their source before any run
(Supplement~S5). A delay added to the path in steps moved every tool's figure
$\toolsSlopeLo$--$\toolsSlopeHi$~ms per millisecond added, for each time the interval it times
crosses the delay, so every tool measures the path. Moving back the clock a tool reads, by more
than the interval it times, separated the tools that read one clock from those that read two. The
figures of \toolsOneClockWord{} tools did not move in $\toolsOneClockRuns$ of their
$\toolsOneClockOf$ runs, the rest undecided, because each takes both timestamps in one process,
timing a round trip or a message it publishes and receives itself. Such a tool never meets a value
below zero, and for the same reason it cannot measure the one-way latency of the delivery a broker
exists to provide. The eleventh, librdkafka's \texttt{rdkafka\_performance}, timestamps in the
producer and subtracts in the consumer, so its figures moved with the clock and add the clocks'
offset to the path's latency. Its consumer drops every latency of zero or less and prints a line
for each, though none of its own values came near zero in our runs (Supplement~S5).

\subsection{A positivity filter on a millisecond clock}
\label{sec:extmethod}
\label{sec:extphase}
\label{sec:extcomp}
The positivity filter is one of the dispositions above, and the one that deletes more than the
values below zero. In statistical
terms it truncates the distribution at zero: what it removes is not counted, as it would be if
censored. We measured it in
depth in a broker benchmark that many public comparisons run, the OpenMessaging
Benchmark~\cite{openmessaging2018}. Its filter was added in 2018 because its end-to-end latency
``is calculated based on time difference of \texttt{System.currentTimeMillis()} on two different
machines'' and ``can be negative''~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}, and its histogram rejects values
below zero outright. For clocks that disagree, the filter is a sound local fix.

It has two side effects. Its clock counts whole milliseconds (whole seconds in its RabbitMQ driver;
Supplement~S5), so its \emph{timestamp resolution}
$\tau$, the smallest step a timestamp can take, is a millisecond. A message that arrives before
the millisecond digit changes therefore computes to zero and is deleted (Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}a).
And nothing counts what the filter deletes. A sample survives only if a millisecond boundary falls between its two
timestamps. With publish instants placed uniformly within the millisecond, that happens with
probability $T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$ on a path shorter than $\tau$, where
$T_{\mathrm{true}}$\label{def:ttrue}, the \emph{true end-to-end latency}, is the one a perfect
clock read without delay would give. So the share of samples kept, the \emph{retention}, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
\text{retention} \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}}}{\tau}\right) .
\end{equation}
Every sample kept on such a path reads exactly one step, so two brokers whose messages all
arrive within a millisecond print the same median and differ only in their retention, which is
never printed.

We added counters that record, by sign, every sample the filter discards, and changed nothing
else. Every run was in the benchmark's embedded mode, which runs it whole in one Java virtual
machine. Of the $\ombMedianCells$ settings whose own summary we captured,
$\ombGridMedianCells$ printed a median of exactly $\ombGridPrintedLo$ or $\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms,
computed from $\ombGridRetentionMin$--$\ombGridRetentionMax\%$ of the samples taken
(Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}b). Two
replicates of one setting kept $\ombKeptLo$ and $\ombKeptHi$ samples, $\ombRetentionFold$-fold
apart, and printed the same median, with nothing in the output to tell them apart. The filter can
also inflate a median. At $\ombAboveGridPartialKB$~KB, the \ombAboveGridPartialWord{} settings
kept only $\ombAboveGridPartialRetLo$--$\ombAboveGridPartialRetHi\%$ of their samples, and every
sample they deleted computed to zero, so most of their messages arrived within a millisecond. Yet
they printed medians of $\ombAboveGridPartialMedLo$ and $\ombAboveGridPartialMedHi$~ms, those of
the slow remainder.

Equation~\ref{eq:retention} holds where it can be tested. It predicts one retention for every
configuration whose publish interval bears no ratio of small whole numbers to $\tau$. The
\spreadIncommensurateWord{} such configurations all keep
$\spreadIncommensurateLo$--$\spreadIncommensurateHi\%$. That puts $T_{\mathrm{true}}$ at
$\spreadIncommensurateTrueMs$~ms, read as a mean over messages with any path longer than $\tau$
counted as $\tau$. Where the ratio is one of small whole numbers, the periodic
schedule phase-locks with the clock: the publish instants visit only a few positions within the
millisecond, and retention takes only a few values. A registered manipulation of message size moved one configuration onto one of those
values and off it (Supplement~S4.3). The arithmetic is in Hewlett-Packard's 1970 counter
note~\cite{hp1970tia}, but the deletion is not: the counter keeps its zero readings and estimates
the interval from the share that are not zero.

Signed counters show the filter hiding two different failures. On \kafka{}, the
$\ombKafkaDiscarded$ samples it discarded over $\ombKafkaRuns$ runs hold $\ombKafkaNegatives$
values below zero, so every discard is a loss of resolution. On \redis{}, the benchmark subtracts
a millisecond timestamp the broker assigns from the consumer's millisecond clock on another host.
All $\ombRedisNegatives$ values below zero over its $\ombRedisRuns$ runs are exactly $-\tau$, the
only value below zero that two floored clocks less than a millisecond apart can produce
(Supplement~S4.4). An unsigned counter cannot tell the two failures apart.

\subsection{Public reports}
\label{sec:reports}
We registered an audit of the benchmark's public reports before reading any
(Supplement~S5). Its signature is a printed end-to-end median of exactly 1~ms, the smallest
value the benchmark can print and the value every kept sample takes on a path faster than a
millisecond. Of $\reachReports$ public reports that print the benchmark's end-to-end percentiles,
across $\reachConfigs$ configurations, \reachPrimaryConfigsWord{} of the $\reachMedianConfigs$
medians they print carry it, in \reachPrimaryReportsWord{} reports. None of the reports states how
many samples it kept. The signature is rare partly because $\reachFinerReports$ of the reports come
from copies of the benchmark whose maintainers changed how the span is timed, and
\reachSubMsReportsWord{} of them print medians below a millisecond, which the original
benchmark's clock cannot produce. Those copies still take the publish timestamp in whole
milliseconds for \kafka{} and Pulsar, and keep the positivity filter (Supplement~S5).

\textbf{Sign-check every run.}\label{sec:gate}\label{sec:audit} The \emph{sign check} rejects a
run when more than one percent of its messages carry a value below zero in any latency component
it records ($S$, the publish delay or the processing-time latency), or when the median of any of
them is below zero. It rejected
$\auditRejectedCloud$ of the $\auditRunsCloud$ cloud runs behind our results
($\auditPctCloud\%$; Supplement~S2). It sees only the worst cases. The publish latency exceeds half the
end-to-end latency on $\diseaseOverHalf\%$ of messages, but the check sees only the
$\diseaseOverWhole\%$ on which it exceeds the whole, and a latency that load inflates but leaves
positive passes by construction. The check therefore needs the repair that follows.

\textbf{Time from the publish call, or add the publish latency back.}\label{sec:cost} The publish
latency does not grow because a message travels further, so on one path, at a fixed publish
latency, the relative error of $S$ as a latency, $A/D$, falls as $1/D$. At our median publish
latency, $A = \ackLagMedianUs\,\mu$s, the error is $\exposureErrOne\%$ at an end-to-end latency of
$1$~ms and $\exposureErrTen\%$ at $10$~ms, and below $\exposureCrossover$~ms the median publish
latency exceeds the end-to-end latency itself (Fig.~\ref{fig:exposure}). Across the
band of $A$ in the figure, the error at $10$~ms runs from $\exposureErrTenLo$ to
$\exposureErrTenHi\%$. From one condition to the next, though, the publish latency grows with the
end-to-end latency, so our conditions sit above the curve. Grouping each condition's runs by the
sign check's verdict gives $\spanRatioConditions$ groups from $\spanRatioBaseConditions$
conditions, over which $S$ understates the end-to-end latency $\understateFactor\times$ at the
median, with an interquartile range of $\understateIQRLo$--$\understateIQRHi\times$. Because the two brokers' clients read the
acknowledgment differently, $S$ also distorts the gap between them, a property of the clients
that a reader would credit to the brokers. Over $\gapDistortionPairs$ matched workloads it
inflates the gap $\gapDistortionPaired\times$ at the median, with an interquartile range of
$\gapDistortionPairedIQR\times$, and in $\gapDistortionDeflates\%$ of them it narrows the gap
instead.

The repair is Equation~\ref{eq:model}. $D = S + A$ holds for every message, and $A$ is read on the
producer's side of the same clock, so a benchmark that records its publish latency recovers,
message by message, the end-to-end latency it failed to measure. A benchmark that keeps only
medians can add only those, and the median of $S$ plus the median of $A$ need not be the median
of $D$. That recovery's median error is $\recoveryErrPass\%$ on the runs the sign check passes,
in $\recoveryPassN$ conditions, and $\recoveryErrFail\%$ on the runs it rejects, in
$\recoveryFailN$, with upper quartiles of $\recoveryErrPassHi\%$ and $\recoveryErrFailHi\%$. Timing from the publish call removes the values
below zero, but not the consumer's own wait for a core, which lengthens every latency and which no
check sees. Measured three ways in $\recvWaitRuns$ later runs on the registered campaign's
machines, that wait added $\recvWaitAddedLo$--$\recvWaitAddedHi$~ms to the mean end-to-end latency
at $\recvLowLoadPct$ and $\recvHighLoadPct\%$ background load, while the median message waited
$\recvWaitMedianLoUs$--$\recvWaitMedianHiUs\,\mu$s (Supplement~S3.8).

\textbf{Give the threads that read the clock a core.} Real-time priority cut the negative-span
rate $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$ at unchanged background load on \kafka{}, and
$\rtRedisFactorLow$--$\rtRedisFactorHigh\times$ on \redis{} (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}). A busy-polling thread spins on a core of its own instead of
sleeping, and never waits to be woken. Busy-polling a dedicated core should buy the
same for the price of that core, though we did not measure it. Since Linux~6.12 a thread can
also ask for a shorter slice of its own, so that it runs sooner when woken, without real-time
priority~\cite{corbet2024eevdf}; we did not measure that either. At real-time priority the rate
settles at $\rtResidualMin$--$\rtResidualMax$ on \kafka{} and $\rtRedisResidualMin$--$\rtRedisResidualMax$ on \redis{}, not at zero, so the check stays, and a real-time or
polling thread changes the system it measures.

\textbf{Count what the filter deletes.} The deletion leaves nothing in a printed percentile, so
the remedy is to make the tool print it: record at the clock's native resolution, and print
beside every latency the retention and the discards, counted by sign. A discard at zero means the
clock's step was too coarse for the path, and one below zero means two clocks disagreed
(Section~\ref{sec:extmethod}). A schedule paced at a simple fraction of the clock's step can
phase-lock with it, and randomizing the publish instants breaks the lock.

A better-synchronized clock repairs neither the late reading nor the deletion: the values of $S$
below zero arise on one clock, and the deletion from the clock's step, not its offset. A finer
clock confines the deletion to faster paths but leaves the filter and every value of $S$ below
zero in place. The remedies are therefore ordered: record at native resolution, count what is
discarded, randomize the publish instants, and only then does synchronization quality matter.


\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Scheduling delay.} That a ready thread can wait for a core is well
known~\cite{gregg2016runqlat}. On servers, a thread kept waiting behind another's time slice adds
milliseconds to the tail latency, and real-time priority removes that wait~\cite{li2014tales};
co-located work lengthens it~\cite{leverich2014reconciling}, and real-time Linux measures the
noise it adds~\cite{bristot2023osnoise}. Scheduler studies find the wait roughly proportional to
the time slice~\cite{jia2024skyloft}, and the limits of the timers that benchmarks read are
known too~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. Load testers of microsecond-scale services correct for the
\emph{client bias} their own queueing and scheduling add to the latencies they
report~\cite{zhang2016treadmill,kogias2019lancet}, Lancet by busy-polling. Chandrasekar and
Kramberger model that bias with a single-parameter queueing law~\cite{chandrasekar2026bias} that
our placement contrast refutes. Villain et al.\ found that an outgoing socket timestamp ``does
not respect causality'' under every stress pattern, including none, and traced host latency to interrupts,
scheduling and context switching~\cite{villain2012probing}. None gives the consequence for a
difference read by two processes: that it falls below zero at a rate set by the waiting
probability and the margin, on the timescale of the slice.

\textbf{Clocks.} Elsewhere, values below zero are read as clocks that disagree. Sharma et al.\
count negative timing spans in distributed inference systems and read them as clock skew, holding
that ``queueing alone cannot produce negative timing spans''~\cite{sharma2026causality}. For the
backlog they model we agree, but a timestamping thread waits in a second queue, the kernel's run
queue. Van~Dongen and Van~den~Poel, who saw latencies below zero on up to a tenth of their events
when timestamps came from different brokers, took both from one
broker~\cite{vandongen2020evaluation}, the one-clock rule reached by another route. Better
measurement systems synchronize clocks~\cite{ieee1588}, calibrate counters across
nodes~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler} or timestamp in the network interface~\cite{kogias2019lancet}
or the switch~\cite{kundel2024p4sta}, and the checks of Section~\ref{sec:practice} apply to
whatever they measure.

\textbf{Resolution and discarded samples.} The retention arithmetic and its cure, phase jitter,
are in a 1970 counter note~\cite{hp1970tia}, and a clock coarser than the intervals it times can
still estimate their mean from many readings, though not any one of them~\cite{danzig1990highres}. IP
measurement advises randomizing probe times~\cite{rfc2330,rfc3432}: probes at random instants see
the time average, which a periodic schedule can miss~\cite{baccelli2006pasta}. Coordinated omission~\cite{tene2015latency} is the deletion's mirror
image: it fails to record samples that would have been slow, while a positivity filter deletes
samples that were fast. Network measurement reports the share of traces it
rejects~\cite{paxson1998calibrating} and keeps a packet timestamped in the
future~\cite{rfc4656}. The literature on rigorous benchmark
reporting~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong,georges2007rigorous,hoefler2015benchmarking} asks, in Hoefler
and Belli's words, that removed outliers be counted. The tools we audited remove samples before
any analyst sees them and count none, a case that neither that literature nor the principles for
reproducible performance evaluation in the cloud~\cite{papadopoulos2021methodological} address. Research benchmarks of stream processing compute
latency from two broker-assigned millisecond timestamps~\cite{henning2024shufflebench}, so the
retention arithmetic applies wherever their latencies fall below a millisecond. What we add is
these checks, applied where a benchmark reports a latency.


\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}
\label{sec:limitations}

Attributing the values of $S$ below zero to scheduling is an inference. One clock excludes clock
skew, the manipulations exclude utilization, timer noise and stress, and the kernel trace observes
the wait. Clocks that disagree across virtual CPUs~\cite{kvmclock} are excluded too. A clock read
in $\clockIncrementNs$~ns can only be \clockAdmitted{}, both of which the kernel uses only where the CPUs'
clocks agree, and an offset large enough for the deepest value of $S$ would have driven $D$ below
zero, while the smallest $D$ observed caps any offset $\spanOffsetMargin$-fold lower. Both testbeds
are virtual, so \emph{steal time}, the time a virtual processor is ready but waits while the
hypervisor runs another machine, is bounded, not excluded: real-time priority, a setting inside
our virtual machine, cannot shorten it, yet it cut the rate. Our CPython timestamps also wait on
the interpreter lock, but \kafka{}'s Java client, which has none, shows the same values below
zero. One rival is not excluded. A broker that delivered the message to the consumer before it
acknowledged the producer would put $S$ below zero with no wait at all. \kafka{} runs with
\texttt{acks=all}, so both replies follow the same commit, but we did not capture the order in
which they left (Supplement~S3.6). Behind most readings below zero, the thread had spent most
of its time waiting for a core (Section~\ref{sec:twostate}), and real-time priority, which cannot
reorder the broker's replies, cut the rate in every pair, so this rival could explain only the
rest.

The laws hold to leading order, and Equation~\ref{eq:occupancy} describes how the rate moves
between conditions, not single messages. Read with the measured end-to-end latency in place of
the margin, it would have the rate fall as $D$ grows inside a condition, yet the rate rises in
$\rbdUp$ of $\rbdTestable$ testable conditions, because a long delivery comes with a long
publish latency (Supplement~S3.4). A registered prediction that priority would cut the waiting
probability at least tenfold failed, scheduler counters seeing it fall $\eaSevenOccFallLo$- to
$\eaSevenOccFallHi$-fold, so the factorization of Equation~\ref{eq:occupancy} is untested. The
registered campaign also predicted the rate from recorded waits, to within a factor of $1.5$
either way, and none of its three predictions was confirmed. Predictions from every thread's
waits ran $\pSixRatioLo$--$\pSixRatioHi$ times the measured rate, those from the time-weighted
waits $\recTimeWeightedRatioLo$--$\recTimeWeightedRatioHi$ times, and those from each
acknowledgment's own wait met the rule on \recOwnWaitPartsInWord{} of \recOwnWaitPartsWord{}
combinations of machine pair and broker. The share of readings below zero whose thread had
mostly been waiting reached the registered $90\%$ on \recMostlyWaitingHoldsWord{} of \recMostlyWaitingPartsWord{}
combinations of pair, broker and load (Supplement~S6.2). The tracer is not free: under it
\kafka{}'s real-time runs read no value below zero, where untraced they read
$\untracedRtNegatives$ in $\untracedRtEvents$, though its normal-priority rate did not move
($\untracedRate$ untraced, $\tracedRate$ traced). The registered campaign's recording raised the rate it recorded $\recRaisedLo$- to
$\recRaisedHi$-fold. Each prediction is therefore held against the traced run's own rate. The base slice on the Oracle hosts is
derived, not read off them; the Azure hosts read it back. In the registered campaign, the
predictions of where the rate falls with the end-to-end latency, and of how quickly, were not
confirmed. Python's rate there was $\pyOverJavaX$ and $\pyOverJavaArm$ times Java's on the x86
and Neoverse~N2 pairs, above the registered limit of $1.5$ (Supplement~S6). The constants are
those of one EEVDF-era kernel. Designs that pin a busy-polling thread to a dedicated core, or
timestamp in hardware, remove the waiting state, and we make no claim about them.

The deletion is demonstrated in one benchmark, in its embedded mode, and reproduced in one
independent program of our own; the benchmark's distributed mode failed on every attempt
(Supplement~S4.5). One registered detail failed: the $32$~KB configuration pins at
$\flipPinThirtyTwo\%$ retention, not the predicted $\flipVertexTwoThirds\%$. The audit found the
deletion's signature in few public results, so we claim it for those and our own runs only. We
classified the \harnessAuditedWord{} tools by reading them, and ran the \toolsRunWord{} on one
pair of machines, one kernel and one publish rate. How much a deployment loses depends on its
end-to-end latency and publish rate, so we recommend reporting retention rather than calling any
reported number wrong.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A latency benchmark measures the system only while its readings are taken faster than the latency
it measures. On one clock, the producer's reading of an acknowledgment and the consumer's reading
of the message, which should coincide, differ by milliseconds and in either order, because a
thread waits for a core, for up to a scheduler's slice, before it reads the clock. Across the
industry such values are deleted, replaced or never measured, a positivity filter on a
millisecond clock deletes the fastest messages too, and no report we audited says how many
samples it kept. Before acting on a broker latency below a few milliseconds, ask which timestamp
it was timed from, how many samples the tool kept, and how many it discarded, by sign.
