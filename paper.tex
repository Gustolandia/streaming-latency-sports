\begin{abstract}
A message broker carries messages between distributed services. Published latency benchmarks
guide the choice of a message broker. They now report sub-millisecond latencies, finer than some
of their clocks resolve and shorter than a thread's wait for a core on a busy host. We timed
widely used brokers on one clock. Timed from the broker's
confirmation of receipt, many messages came out negative, as if delivered before being sent.
The cause is not clock synchronization but scheduling. The thread that records the confirmation
waits for a core longer than the message takes. Giving it priority at unchanged load removes most
negatives. We did a wide audit of the most used tools across the industry: most of them discard
such values uncounted or never measure a one-way delivery. A positivity filter on a millisecond
clock also deletes the fastest messages. We derive the fraction kept, and a test registered in
advance confirms it. In practice a sub-millisecond median
can describe the clock, not the broker. A void result also looks sound: a sign check rejected
most of our runs. We give benchmark authors checks that cost nothing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{O}{ur} first harness timed each message from the moment the producer learned
that the broker had accepted it. By its arithmetic, $\spanNegAck$ of the $\spanEvents$
messages we timed arrived before they were sent. By the clock, none did. Timed from the send
on the same clock, not one of them is negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoways}a). That is the finding
this paper starts from. A latency timed from the broker's acknowledgment can come out negative
on a single clock, with no disagreement between clocks to blame, and a run in which that
happens often cannot report a latency at all. A per-run sign check rejected $\auditRejected$ of
our $\auditRuns$ runs and left nothing of our own first result usable, although that result had
passed every conventional check we knew (Section~\ref{sec:finding}).

It matters because engineers choose a message broker, the service that carries an application's
messages, by its published latency benchmarks, which time each message from producer to
consumer. Those benchmarks now report latencies below a millisecond~\cite{indexdev2026brokers}. On such a
path both the clock and the scheduler are slower than the message. Several of the tools that
produce such comparisons time a delivery in whole milliseconds (Section~\ref{sec:tools}), and
on a busy host the thread that reads a clock can wait milliseconds for a core
(Section~\ref{sec:tail}). The numbers still print, and they look like any other latency.

The arithmetic had subtracted two timestamps written by two different threads, and a thread
must wait for a core before it can read the clock. That wait is the \emph{scheduling
delay}\label{def:scheddelay}, the time a thread that is ready to run spends queued before a
core runs it. When the thread that records the acknowledgment waits longer than the message
takes to arrive, the difference comes out negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). Nothing arrived
early. One reading was taken late.

Three small laws govern it (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). We call the time a message takes to
reach the consumer its \emph{delivery time}, and the time the producer waits to hear that the
broker has it its \emph{acknowledgment lag}. The difference is
negative exactly when the acknowledgment lag exceeds the delivery time. To leading order, its
rate is the chance that the timestamping thread is waiting for a core times the chance that this
wait lasts longer than the delivery time. These waits have a mode in the histogram bucket that holds the
scheduler's \emph{slice}, the time it lets a task run at a turn. A slice is a few milliseconds,
the scale of the paths now benchmarked. Moving the scheduling while holding the load fixed moves
the rate. Real-time priority on the threads that take the timestamps cut it $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$.

The industry meets such values and mostly discards them. \harnessSilentWordCap{} of the
\harnessAuditedWord{} measurement tools we read at source dispose of a latency at or below zero
without counting it. Of \toolsRunWord{} benchmarking tools we ran, \toolsOneClockWord{} never meet
a negative, because they time on one clock, which also keeps them from measuring a one-way
delivery at all. Apache \kafka{}'s own end-to-end tool reports zero at every percentile on a
sub-millisecond path~\cite{kafka_tools}, and none of the $\reachReports$ published reports we
audited states how many samples it kept (Section~\ref{sec:external}).

What discarding costs is clearest in one of these tools, a broker benchmark whose users were
told that negative latencies were clock skew between nodes~\cite{omb_issue216}. It discards
every latency that is not positive~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}, a rule we call a \emph{positivity
filter}. For clocks that disagree, the rule is sensible. But its
end-to-end clock counts whole milliseconds, and to that clock a message that arrives before the
digit changes took no time at all, which is faster than light too. The same rule therefore
discards every such message, about half of them on the path we measured and nearly all at some
send rates (Section~\ref{sec:extcomp}). In $\ombGridMedianCells$ of the $\ombMedianCells$
embedded-mode settings whose summary we captured, with the whole benchmark in one process, it
printed a median of exactly $\ombGridPrintedLo$ or $\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms. Those medians were
computed from as little as $\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of the samples taken, and nothing in the
output said how few. Applied to the latencies our first harness reported, the same rule would
remove $\msGuardDeletedPct\%$ of them uncounted, negatives and zeros alike
(Fig.~\ref{fig:twoways}b).

Clock resolution and scheduling delay are each well understood. What was not known is what they do to the sub-millisecond comparisons that
broker benchmarks now publish, because the tools that produce those comparisons discard the
evidence that would show it. We call the negative latency Failure~1 and the deletion Failure~2.

Neither failure announces itself, and that is the practical problem. On a path faster than a
millisecond every sample such a filter keeps reads exactly one millisecond, so the tool prints
the same median whatever the broker. It does not print what differs between two brokers, the
share of samples kept. A median computed from $\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of the samples is
typographically identical to one computed from all of them, and more stable from run to run for
having its variation deleted. The tool passes the reproducibility a reader checks because it
fails the verifiability nobody does.

Where fast and slow messages mix, the deletion inflates instead. At $\ombAboveGridPartialKB$~KB,
\ombAboveGridPartialWord{} settings printed medians of
$\ombAboveGridPartialMedLo$ and $\ombAboveGridPartialMedHi$~ms, yet most of their messages had
arrived within a millisecond. A latency timed from the acknowledgment understates the delivery
time it stands for and inflates the gap between two brokers, a property of the clients that a
reader would credit to the brokers (Section~\ref{sec:cost}). A better-synchronized clock repairs
none of this, and the checks that expose it cost nothing (Section~\ref{sec:practice}).

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
The paper has three parts and draws their consequences in a fourth. Both failures are
established by manipulation rather than by observation alone. We call a prediction, campaign or
audit \emph{registered} when it was committed in public before the data that test it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The finding} (Section~\ref{sec:finding}). A latency timed from an acknowledgment
timestamp comes out negative on one clock under both brokers we measured, while none timed from
the send ever does, and the same negatives appear in \kafka{}'s own Java client and on a second

\item \textbf{The mechanism} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). Such a latency comes out negative when

the same load at identical utilization differed twofold, so utilization alone does not set the

\item \textbf{The industry} (Section~\ref{sec:external}). With a millisecond clock and a
positivity filter, the share of samples a benchmark keeps, its \emph{retention}, is set by the ratio of the delivery
time to the clock's resolution and by the phase of its send schedule. A registered manipulation
of message size moved a setting across the boundary this predicts. \harnessSilentWordCap{} of
the \harnessAuditedWord{} tools read at source give no count of what they discard
(Section~\ref{sec:tools}). Of \toolsRunWord{} tools run under registered predictions,

(Section~\ref{sec:toolsrun}). A registered audit of $\reachReports$ published reports found the
signature in \reachPrimaryConfigsWord{} of the $\reachMedianConfigs$ medians they print and a
stated retention in none (Section~\ref{sec:reports}).
\item \textbf{Practical implications} (Section~\ref{sec:practice}). What a printed latency can
and cannot tell its reader, what timing from the acknowledgment costs and how to add it back,
and checks that cost nothing: a per-run sign check, and retention published beside every latency.
\end{enumerate}

\section{How a Benchmark Times a Message}
\label{sec:sysmodel}
\label{sec:metrics}
\label{sec:method}

A producer process sends recorded events, one message each, at a paced rate to a broker, and a
consumer process reads them back. Four timestamps bracket a message's path. The first, $t_{\mathrm{sched}}$,
is when the event was due to be sent, and $t_{\mathrm{send}}$ is taken immediately before the
send call. Then $t_{\mathrm{ack}}$ is when the producer learns that the broker accepted the
message, and $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ is when the consumer holds the record. A \emph{span} is any difference
of two of these timestamps, and a \emph{negative span} is one whose difference is below zero.
Three spans follow:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{send}}, \qquad
A \equiv t_{\mathrm{ack}} - t_{\mathrm{send}}, \\
S &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{ack}} \;=\; D - A .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}

$D$ is the \emph{delivery time}, from the send call to the consumer holding the record. $A$ is
the \emph{acknowledgment lag}, from the send call to the producer observing the broker's
acknowledgment. $S$ is what a harness reports when it times a message from the acknowledgment
rather than from the send, as our own first harness did. We call it the \emph{transport
proxy}, because it stands in for the broker's transport time without measuring it. The end-to-end
latency runs from the event falling due to
the consumer having handled it, at $t_{\mathrm{out}}$:
\begin{equation}
E = t_{\mathrm{out}} - t_{\mathrm{sched}}
  = \underbrace{(t_{\mathrm{send}}-t_{\mathrm{sched}})}_{\text{send lag}} + A + S
  + \underbrace{(t_{\mathrm{out}}-t_{\mathrm{recv}})}_{\text{handling}} .
\label{eq:tti}
\end{equation}
The \emph{send lag} is how late the send call ran after the event was due, and the
\emph{handling span} is the consumer's own work on the record after $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$.

Write $\tau$ for the \emph{timestamp resolution}, one millisecond in several of
the tools of Section~\ref{sec:external}. Write $T_{\mathrm{true}}$\label{def:ttrue} for the
\emph{true delivery time} of a message, the interval $D$ would report if its timestamps were
arbitrarily fine. \emph{Retention} is the fraction of the samples a measurement tool takes
that survive into what it reports.

\subsection{Terms}
\label{def:vocabulary}
A \emph{condition} is one point of the experimental design: a broker, a send rate, a message
size, a background load and a consumer pattern. A \emph{cell} is a condition together with a
workload. Where the workload is fixed, the two coincide. A \emph{run} is one execution of a

\subsection{The sign check}
\label{sec:gate}
\label{sec:stats}
A run is rejected if more than $1\%$ of its events carry a negative value in any latency
component, or if the median of any component is negative. A condition is usable only if all of
its runs survive. We fixed the rule before the final campaign, after earlier campaigns had run,
and apply it to every run, including those whose results looked reasonable.

The one-percent line is a convention, and it decides nothing we report. Even swept from $0$ to
$20\%$, it makes none of our first result's rejected conditions usable (Supplement~S2.1). The
rule does not assume a negative is impossible, and for the transport proxy it is not. A run
whose reference timestamp fails on more than one event in a hundred cannot report a latency,
whatever the cause.

Every proportion below is a count over a stated denominator. Rates carry Wilson score $95\%$
intervals and ratios Katz intervals, Holm-corrected within a named family. The supplement's
tables print the corrected values. Every table is generated from the committed
data at build time.

\label{sec:blindspot}
The sign check sees only the worst cases. The acknowledgment lag exceeds half the delivery time on
$\diseaseOverHalf\%$ of events and a tenth of it on $\diseaseOverTenth\%$, but the check fires
only on the $\diseaseOverWhole\%$ where it exceeds the whole. The two delays are correlated
within a run (median correlation $\spanRhoWithinMedian$ over $\spanRhoConditions$ conditions),
one scheduler causing both. Treating
the two as independent within a run would overpredict the negative-span rate
$\indepWithinFloored$-fold, the median of the $\indepWithinFlooredN$ conditions above
$\indepFloorPct\%$. A delivery time longer than the acknowledgment lag is therefore not clean,
only quiet. Load-induced inflation that stays positive passes the check by construction.

\subsection{Beyond our clients and our processors}
\label{sec:beyond}
The negatives are not peculiar to our Python clients or to one processor family. A later
registered campaign on three further pairs of machines found them in \kafka{}'s own Java
client, at a third to a quarter of the Python client's rate, and on Neoverse~N2 processors as on
x86 (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Our timestamp is a CPython call and also waits on the
interpreter lock. The Java client, which has none, shows the same failure at a lower rate.

\section{The Mechanism: A Timestamp Taken Late}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The negatives in our corpus are produced by scheduling. We establish this by moving scheduling
while holding everything else fixed. Three small laws carry the argument: an identity that says
when the span inverts, a two-state model that says how often, and the scheduler's rule for its
slice, which sets the timescale.

\subsection{The transport proxy is not a causal chain}
\label{sec:proxy}
\label{sec:model}
$D$ and $E$ are chains of events, each preceding the next. $S$ is not. The broker's append
precedes both the producer's receipt of the acknowledgment and the consumer's receipt of the
record, but neither of them precedes the other. They are two branches of one cause. Since
Equation~\ref{eq:model} holds per event, whatever the signs,
\begin{equation}
S < 0 \quad\text{if and only if}\quad A > D .
\label{eq:negspan}
\end{equation}

Every timestamp is written some time after the event it marks, because the thread that reads
the clock must first be scheduled (Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). A negative $S$ therefore shows only that the acknowledgment cannot
serve as the origin of the delivery time for that event, which is the use an
acknowledgment-referenced harness puts it to. The same holds wherever the origin
timestamp is written by a thread that must first be woken, such as a delivery-report poll or
a completion reaper (Supplement~S5). The identity is checked on every build. Both sides count
$\spanNegAck$ negatives over our $\diseaseEvents$ events, and the build fails if they part.

A fixed distribution of $A$ overlaps a small $D$ almost
entirely and a large one hardly at all. That is why a path whose delivery time runs to tens of
seconds passes every run, while the same hardware measuring one millisecond fails wholesale.

\subsection{The cause, by manipulation}
\label{sec:mixture}
\label{sec:twostate}
Each timestamping thread is either running, in which case its timestamp is late by a narrow
jitter, or preempted and waiting for a core. From idle to the knee of the load sweep the rate
grows $\invGrowth\times$ but the jitter's width only $\coreGrowth\times$, so load mostly changes
how often the thread is waiting for a core. Write $p$ for the probability of the second state,
the thread's \emph{occupancy}\label{def:occupancy}. Call the part of that wait still to come the
\emph{residual stall}, and write $G$ for its survival function, the chance that it outlasts a
given time. Conditioning
Equation~\ref{eq:negspan} on the true delivery time gives, to leading order,
\begin{equation}
\Pr\bigl[\,S<0 \mid T_{\mathrm{true}}\,\bigr] \;\approx\; p\,G(T_{\mathrm{true}}) ,
\label{eq:occupancy}
\end{equation}
the occupancy times the chance that a residual stall outlasts the delivery time. The highest
rate near saturation, $\invCeiling$, is the largest observed rather than a bound.

The core count $k$\label{def:kcores} is the number of cores the background load occupies. Two
settings that reach the same utilization with the load on different numbers of cores differ in
\emph{load geometry}\label{def:geometry}: \emph{concentrated} on few cores or \emph{spread} over
many. Load changes $p$ but
does not determine it (Table~\ref{tab:mechanism}, bottom), so we write $p$, not $p(\rho)$, and
the model predicts how the rate falls as the path lengthens, not how it rises with load.

\subsection{Where the timescale comes from}
\label{sec:tail}
The scheduling delay behind these negatives is not a single heavy tail. Over the
$\tracedEvents$ traced wakeups the counts have \tracedModesWord{} local maxima, which a power
law, descending monotonically, cannot have. The last, in the
$\tracedModeLo$--$\tracedModeHi$~ms bucket, holds $\tracedModeShare\%$ of all wakeups and
stands $\tracedModeRatio\times$ its lower neighbor. Above it the counts collapse. They fall
$\tracedModeFallA\times$, $\tracedModeFallB\times$ and $\tracedModeFallC\times$ over the next
\tracedModeFallOctaves{} octaves and then $\tracedLastBucketFall\times$, each octave a doubling of the wait, whereas a power law
would fall by the same factor in every octave (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}).

Gregg reported the distribution as bimodal in the post
introducing \texttt{runqlat}~\cite{gregg2016runqlat}, and the \texttt{timerlat} tracer
separates its interrupt and thread components~\cite{bristot2025timerlat}. What we add is the
upper mode's position, consistent with the scheduler's base slice. The kernel's published
rule~\cite{zhou2025slice} gives the base slice $s$ by scaling a constant by the number of CPUs
$n$, with a cap:
\begin{equation}
s \;=\; 0.75\,\mathrm{ms}\times\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2\min(n,8)\rfloor\bigr),
\label{eq:slice}
\end{equation}
which gives $\sliceFactor \times 0.75\,\mathrm{ms} = \baseSliceMs$~ms on the host's
\testbedCpusWord{} CPUs. That slice is derived rather than measured. With run-to-parity, which lets
a running task finish its slice before a woken task preempts it, a task stays within one slice
plus one tick of its fair share~\cite{guittot2025slice}.

A thread woken while another task holds the core waits out what is left of that task's
slice, up to a whole one. A whole slice is $\sliceOverDeliveryLo$--$\sliceOverDeliveryHi$ times
the per-condition delivery times this corpus measures, $\condDeliveryLoMs$--$\condDeliveryHiMs$~ms.
The registered campaign timed that wait afterwards, acknowledgment by acknowledgment, and found
the rest of a slice, in a reading made after its verdicts (Supplement~S6.5). The slice lands on
the scale of the interval being measured, which is why the check fires on a substantial
minority of events.

Read together, Equations~\ref{eq:negspan}, \ref{eq:occupancy} and~\ref{eq:slice} say what the
rate depends on. It rises with the occupancy $p$, which load raises and real-time priority
lowers. It falls as the delivery time outgrows the residual stall, whose scale the slice sets.
On our kernel, any host with at least as many CPUs as ours has a slice of $\baseSliceMs$~ms, so
every path faster than that is within reach of a single stall. And it owes nothing to how well
two clocks agree, because one clock reads both timestamps.

\section{The Industry's Remedy: Deletion}
\label{sec:external}

We assessed how the industry handles a latency at or below zero in four ways, and they agree.
The value is deleted, replaced or never measured, and almost never counted. Of the
\harnessAuditedWord{} measurement tools we read at source, \harnessSilentWord{} dispose of such a

\subsection{Ten tools, read at source}
\label{sec:tools}
The disposal is a pattern across the tooling. We read
\harnessAuditedWord{} tools at source, across \harnessLanguagesWord{} languages and
\harnessVendorsWord{} vendors, and classified each with one program, the same for every tool
(Table~\ref{tab:tools}). \harnessSilentWordCap{} dispose of the sample without counting it, in
\harnessDisposalClassesWord{} classes: filtering, refusal by the tool's own library, and substitution.

Substitution is the worst, because it leaves nothing missing. The tools \texttt{fio} and
\texttt{btt} substitute zero and one nanosecond, and RabbitMQ PerfTest takes the absolute value, so a
negative latency enters its distribution as a positive one of the same
size~\cite{rabbitmq_perftest}. In each, a value never measured enters the distribution. Apache
Pulsar's performance client admits zero~\cite{pulsar_perf}. It drops negatives and keeps the
zero samples a strict positivity filter deletes, which then pile up visibly at zero. Apache \kafka{}'s own
end-to-end tool keeps every sample and loses it anyway. It fills the array its percentiles come
from by integer division, so on a sub-millisecond path every percentile it reports is
zero~\cite{kafka_tools}.

In November 2025 \kafka{}'s coordinator metrics adopted the same disposal, clamping negative
durations to zero with no counter~\cite{kafka19888}. \kafka{}'s record timestamp is itself a
millisecond field on the wire, so patching one benchmark does not reach the broker's own
metadata (Supplement~S4.7). The failure is not universal. \texttt{emqtt-bench}'s histogram
keeps every sample, as RFC~7679 asks~\cite{emqtt_bench}, and \harnessCountingWord{}, Rezolus, counts
its discards in a metric that names the cause~\cite{rezolus}. The NATS command-line tool keeps a
negative in the minimum, median and maximum it prints, while its percentile table, recorded into
a library that refuses one, leaves it out with nothing to say so~\cite{nats_cli}. What survives is
the narrower claim: the tools that quantize, filter, refuse or substitute do so without counting.

\subsection{A positivity filter on a millisecond clock}
\label{sec:extmethod}
A filter that admits only positive latencies is one of the ways the tools of
Table~\ref{tab:tools} dispose of an impossible value, and the one whose cost reaches beyond the
negatives. Its origin is documented and its reasoning is sound. In the broker benchmark we
measured in depth, the OpenMessaging Benchmark~\cite{openmessaging2018}, which many published
comparisons run, the filter was added in 2018 because end-to-end latency ``is calculated based
on time difference of \texttt{System.currentTimeMillis()} on two different machines'' and ``can
be negative''~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}. Its recorder is an \texttt{HdrHistogram}
\texttt{Recorder}, which rejects negative values outright, so filtering for positives is the
reasonable local fix, defensive rather than evasive. A tool that times a message faster than
light has two choices: announce the discovery, or delete the message. This one deletes it.

The fix is sound locally, and it has two non-local consequences. First, \texttt{> 0} also
deletes every sample that computes to exactly zero. With a millisecond clock, a delivery time
shorter than a millisecond computes to zero unless it crosses the \emph{grid}, the instants, one
millisecond apart, at which the timestamp changes value. Second, the one observation that would
show the measurement had failed is the exact observation the filter removes, and nothing counts
it. Over our campaign that silence covers $\ombDiscarded$ samples, and Section~\ref{sec:tools}
found the same uncounted disposal in \harnessSilentIndependentWord{} independent tools.

The coarse clock and the filter sit together on
the \ombGuardedPath{} span, timed in \ombGuardedClock{}s, while the same harness times its
\ombUnguardedPath{} span in \ombUnguardedClock{}s and records every sample. It computes its
reported latency distribution from a subset of the samples it collects, does not record how
large that subset was, and lets the send rate decide. We \emph{instrumented} it with counters that
record, by sign, every sample its filter discards. Everything measured on it here is in embedded
mode, where the whole benchmark runs in one JVM. Its distributed mode failed on every attempt
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{What the filter did}
\label{sec:extphase}
Of the $\ombMedianCells$ instrumented settings whose own summary we captured,
$\ombGridMedianCells$ report a median of $\ombGridPrintedLo$ or $\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms. Those
medians were computed from between $\ombGridRetentionMin$ and $\ombGridRetentionMax\%$ of the
samples the benchmark took (Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}b). Two replicates of one setting, $200$~B at
$500$~msg/s on one host,
kept $\ombKeptLo$ and $\ombKeptHi$ samples, a $\ombRetentionFold$-fold difference. Both reported
a median of $\ombGridPrintedLo$~ms, with nothing in the output to distinguish them.

Three uninstrumented runs printed p50, p95, p99 and max all equal to $\ombGridPrintedLo$. Like a
stopped clock, the printed median is exactly right whenever the true one happens to be a
millisecond. Over all $\ombRuns$ instrumented runs retention falls as low as
$\ombRetentionMinExact\%$, about four samples in ninety thousand.

The \ombEscapeCellsWord{} settings that print above the grid carry the largest payloads. The
\ombAboveGridWholeWord{} at $\ombAboveGridWholeKB$~KB keep every sample. The
\ombAboveGridPartialWord{} at $\ombAboveGridPartialKB$~KB keep only
$\ombAboveGridPartialRetLo$--$\ombAboveGridPartialRetHi\%$. Every sample they delete computes
to zero, so most of their messages arrived within a millisecond, and the medians they print,
$\ombAboveGridPartialMedLo$ and $\ombAboveGridPartialMedHi$~ms, describe only the slow
remainder.

\subsection{The retention law, and a registered confirmation}
\label{sec:extcomp}
Retention moves with where the producer's send instants fall against the grid. Publish
latency across these settings takes only two values, $\ombPubLatLo$ and $\ombPubLatHi$~ms, while
retention varies $\ombRetentionFold$-fold. A sample survives when its delivery time crosses a grid instant,
which for $T_{\mathrm{true}} < \tau$ happens with probability $T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}a):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
\text{retention} \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}}}{\tau}\right)
\qquad\text{(uniform phases).}
\end{equation}

All \spreadIncommensurateWord{} configurations whose send interval is incommensurate with
$\tau$, with no ratio of small whole numbers between the two, sit near $50\%$, with medians from $\spreadIncommensurateLo$ to $\spreadIncommensurateHi$. That
implies $T_{\mathrm{true}} = \spreadIncommensurateTrueMs$~ms at $\tau = 1$~ms. Where the send
interval is commensurate with $\tau$, retention is confined to a few values, so
moving $T_{\mathrm{true}}$ moves a configuration between them (Supplement~S4.3 tests every
configuration). That prediction was registered before its runs, and it came true. Replicate
spread collapses to $\flipSpreadThirtyTwo$ points at $32$~KB and re-opens to
$\flipSpreadSixtyFour$ at $64$~KB, a boundary crossed in both directions by one manipulated
variable. One registered detail failed. The $32$~KB configuration pins at
$\flipPinThirtyTwo\%$ retention, not at the predicted $\flipVertexTwoThirds\%$.

The arithmetic is not ours. Hewlett-Packard's Application Note 162-1~\cite{hp1970tia} carries
the retention identity, the ceiling on resolution gain, the symptom and the cure. What is new is
what the harness does with it. The counter keeps both readings, and the retained fraction is its
estimator. A harness released in 2018 throws away the quantity a 1970 counter measured with.

\subsection{Our own harness, and the sign channel}
\label{sec:generality}
To show the deletion is not an artifact of one JVM, we reproduced the filter in an independent
Python harness sharing no code with the benchmark. On one clock it
recorded zero negative differences across $\harnessOneClockSamples$ samples, as a causal chain
requires, and the grid behavior reappeared intact. Counting the discards separately by sign, which
we call the \emph{sign channel}, is what makes the filter's cost legible. Across the
$\ombKafkaRuns$ runs of the benchmark on \kafka{}, its $\ombKafkaDiscarded$ discarded samples
contain not one negative. The most negative value at any load, size or rate is $0\,\mu$s. Those discards are resolution
failures, not clock failures, a distinction an unsigned counter cannot make.

The same channel caught the real thing in the $\ombRedisRuns$ runs of the replication on
\redis{}, which logged $\ombRedisNegatives$ negatives, every one exactly $-1000\,\mu$s. That is the smallest value a
one-millisecond timestamp can express, and it is no coincidence. On \redis{} the benchmark's span
is a difference between two millisecond-floored clocks on two hosts. A sub-millisecond offset between two
floored clocks can produce exactly one negative value, $-1000\,\mu$s, and no other. Clock
discipline was clean at every logged minute, so this failure needs no misbehaving clock, only two of
them and a floor (Supplement~S4.5).

\subsection{What a printed latency can and cannot say}
\label{sec:reading}
A broker latency printed below a few milliseconds cannot be taken at face value unless its
output says how it was timed and what was kept. For two brokers whose messages all arrive
within a millisecond, a tool that filters for positive values on a millisecond clock prints
identical output (Section~\ref{sec:extcomp}). By
Equation~\ref{eq:retention} the two differ only in retention, $T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$ for
uniform send phases, and retention is never printed. A
sub-millisecond ranking read from that output ranks the clock. The error also reaches
large messages, where it inflates the median. At $\ombAboveGridPartialKB$~KB the printed median
described only the slow remainder of a path whose median message took under a millisecond
(Section~\ref{sec:extphase}).

No output carries a warning, and none of the $\reachReports$
published reports we audited says what it kept (Section~\ref{sec:reports}). Most tools avoid the
negative instead of reporting it. \toolsOneClockWordCap{} of the \toolsRunWord{} we ran take both
timestamps from one clock, so their figures are not one-way delivery times, and a comparison
that sets them beside one-way latencies compares different intervals
(Section~\ref{sec:toolsrun}).

Timed from the acknowledgment, a latency understates the delivery time, the more so the
shorter the path. A run of such latencies can be void outright. Our own first result had survived six corrections and a battery of rank tests before the sign check
left none of its conditions usable (Section~\ref{sec:audit}). Section~\ref{sec:cost} sizes the
error and repairs it.

The exposure is not confined to benchmarks. \kafka{}'s coordinator metrics
now clamp negative durations to zero without a counter (Section~\ref{sec:tools}), and any
harness that times from a delivery report or a completion reaper starts its clock at a
timestamp that may be written late (Section~\ref{sec:proxy}).

\subsection{The cost of timing from the acknowledgment, and the repair}
\label{sec:cost}
The acknowledgment lag $A$ is a scheduling quantity. It does not grow because a message traveled
further, so the relative error of the transport proxy is $A/D$ and falls as $1/D$. At our
median acknowledgment lag, $A = \ackLagMedianUs\,\mu$s, the error is $\exposureErrOne\%$ at a
delivery time of $1$~ms, $\exposureErrTen\%$ at $10$~ms and $\exposureErrHundred\%$ at
$100$~ms. Below $\exposureCrossover$~ms the median lag exceeds the delivery time itself
(Fig.~\ref{fig:exposure}).

For a harness that reports $S$, two consequences follow on our corpus. The reported span
is $\reportedFraction\%$ of the delivery time it stands for, understating it
$\understateFactor\times$.
That is a median of per-condition ratios over $\spanRatioConditions$ conditions, with an
interquartile range of $\understateIQRLo$--$\understateIQRHi\times$. And because the two
brokers' clients timestamp differently, the proxy inflates the gap between two systems
$\gapDistortionPaired\times$ over $\gapDistortionPairs$ matched workloads, a property of the
clients that a reader would attribute to the brokers.

The acknowledgment lag can be added back. Equation~\ref{eq:model} holds per event
and $A$ is producer-side on one clock. A harness that records its own acknowledgment lag
therefore recovers the delivery time it failed to measure. Its median error is
$\recoveryErrPass\%$ on the $\recoveryPassN$ conditions the check passes and
$\recoveryErrFail\%$ on the $\recoveryFailN$ it rejects, with upper quartiles of
$\recoveryErrPassHi\%$ and $\recoveryErrFailHi\%$, two populations that the data cannot tell
apart. The repair sits beside the check of Section~\ref{sec:gate}. The check
declines to publish the proxy as a latency, and the repair reports the delivery time in its
place. Timing from the send removes the negative values but not the receipt's own wait for a
core, which inflates every latency where no check can see it. Measured three ways in
$\recvWaitRuns$ later runs, that wait added $\recvWaitAddedLo$--$\recvWaitAddedHi$~ms to the mean
latency on both brokers at $\recvLowLoadPct$ and $\recvHighLoadPct\%$ load, while the median
message waited $\recvWaitMedianLoUs$--$\recvWaitMedianHiUs\,\mu$s (Supplement~S3.8
and~S3.10).

\subsection{Checks that cost nothing}
\label{sec:checks}
Both failures are cheap to detect, and Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists the checks. SPEC and TPC
judge a benchmark on five criteria~\cite[Sec.~3]{kistowski2015build}. In their terms, the
tool of Section~\ref{sec:intro} passes the half of \emph{reproducibility} a reader checks,
run-to-run consistency at a fixed configuration, because it fails \emph{verifiability}. A
second tester who picks a different send rate measures a different sample. Better measurement systems
exist~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler,kogias2019lancet,kundel2024p4sta}, and the checks apply to
whatever they measure.

Every check in the table restores verifiability, and every one has a
precedent. Network measurement has published the share of
traces it rejected for nearly thirty years~\cite{paxson1998calibrating,paxson2004sound}. OWAMP
records a packet timestamped in the future unaltered rather than discarding it~\cite{rfc4656}.
Randomizing probe times against periodic phenomena is standing advice~\cite{rfc2330,rfc3432}.
What we add is the conjunction, applied where a benchmark reports a latency, with the rejection
rate published as a headline quantity.

One mitigation our measurements support directly costs something. Giving the timestamping
threads real-time priority cut the negative-span rate $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$ at
unchanged background load. Busy-polling a dedicated core should buy the same for the price of
that core, though we did not measure it. The floor is not zero, so the check stays, and a
real-time or polling timestamping thread changes the system it measures. Where that is
unacceptable, report retention instead.

\label{sec:betterclock}
A better-synchronized clock fixes neither failure. Hardware-timestamped PTP~\cite{ieee1588} would
take two hosts from the $\interHostOffsetUs\,\mu$s offset \texttt{chrony} reported between ours to
under a microsecond. Behind a one-millisecond timestamp the two produce byte-identical records. The filter
deletes the same samples, and the retention arithmetic is unchanged. A finer clock does fix the
second failure on these paths. It retires Equation~\ref{eq:retention}'s deletions and moves the
grid to faster paths, but it leaves the filter and every genuine negative in place. The
remedies are therefore ordered: record at native resolution, count what is
discarded, dither the send instant, and only then does synchronization quality matter.

\subsection{What is left of the broker comparison}
\label{sec:brokers}
Once the failed runs are removed, the broker comparison that motivated the work is small. On the
end-to-end latency $E$, a causal chain, the two brokers are equivalent against a
$\ttiTostMargin$~ms margin (Supplement~S7), and on the transport proxy they sit within a millisecond
(TOST, two one-sided tests against a $\tostMargin$~ms margin, $p < 0.001$, across \tostLevelsWord{} levels). So it is
not a purchasing argument. A consumer that acknowledges each message and waits for the reply
reverses their order, and a co-located testbed hides the effect. Our own measurement treats the
two brokers differently. \kafka{}'s client
deserializes the payload before $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ and \redis{}'s after it, which is worth
$\spanHandlingWorstUs\,\mu$s or $\spanHandlingSharePct\%$ of \redis{}'s median delivery time.
$E$ contains both parses and is free of the asymmetry.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
That measurement error can dominate a benchmark is established. What prior work does not supply
is a check applied to every run, and a count of what the check removes.

\subsection{Broker benchmarks}
\label{sec:related_bench}
Broker comparisons appear continuously in peer-reviewed venues and in the gray literature. Apache
\kafka{} and \redis{} Streams are the pair they most often name, and many of them run the
OpenMessaging Benchmark~\cite{openmessaging2018}. That experimental setup can dominate a
result is established~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}, and how to report one is a settled
literature~\cite{georges2007rigorous,hoefler2015benchmarking}. What that literature does not do is

% referee advised against swapping unprompted: Georges is the better-known citation and every

check what its tool discards.

Karimov et al.~\cite{karimov2018benchmarking} separate event-time
from processing-time latency. Van~Dongen and Van~den~Poel~\cite{vandongen2020evaluation} use
single-broker append timestamps ``to ensure correct latency measurements,'' which is our one-clock
rule, reached earlier and by another route. Neither asks what a millisecond timestamp does to a sub-millisecond
path, nor which samples the tool keeps. Where a comparison reports a median above the tool's
one-millisecond timestamp resolution, the deletion arithmetic of Section~\ref{sec:external} does
not bind. Some give no number to place. A 2026 comparison names a percentile for one broker and
gives the other two only ``sub-millisecond''~\cite{indexdev2026brokers}, so its ranking cannot be
placed against the resolution at all.

\subsection{Cross-process time}
\label{sec:related_time}
Lamport~\cite{lamport1978time} established that a distributed system has no single physical time,
only a causal partial order, and that order decides this paper's central span. Measurement
systems defeat clock disagreement~\cite{ieee1588} by calibrating cross-node
counters~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler}, timestamping in the network
interface~\cite{kogias2019lancet} or the switch dataplane~\cite{kundel2024p4sta}, and measuring
the timer's own accuracy~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. Villain et al.~\cite{villain2012probing},
profiling FreeBSD against a hardware reference, found that the outgoing socket timestamp ``does
not respect causality'' under every stress pattern, including none. They attributed host latency
to interrupt processing, scheduling and context switching. That is the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, measured more precisely than we measure it. What it does not give is
the consequence for a two-process measurement, that the negative-span rate falls as the delivery time being
measured grows. The manipulations that establish it separate scheduling from its rival
explanations (Section~\ref{sec:twostate}).

Paxson~\cite{paxson1998calibrating} treated physically
inconsistent transit times as measurement failure and published the share of traces affected.
Lancet~\cite[Sec.~4.3]{kogias2019lancet} reports an inconclusive verdict when a run's samples fail
its stability test. Neither reports the share of runs a campaign lost.

Production tracing reached a related answer without a mechanism. Skew adjusters that rewrite a span's timestamps are called
harmful and have defaulted to off since 2020~\cite{jaeger_harmful}. Sharma et
al.~\cite{sharma2026causality} count negative timing spans in distributed inference systems. They
hold that ``queueing alone cannot produce negative timing spans'' and read the negatives as clock
skew. For the queue they model, we agree. A second queue decides ours, the run queue the
timestamping thread waits in.

\subsection{Resolution and discarded samples}
\label{sec:related_resolution}
That a timer coarser than the interval it measures invalidates the measurement is
textbook~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. Metrology carries both failures in one uncertainty budget.
The NIST stopwatch guide budgets reaction time beside display resolution, and notes that at short
intervals the resolution becomes significant against the stopwatch's rated
accuracy~\cite{nist2009stopwatch}. That periodic sampling
commensurate with a periodic phenomenon sees only part of it is the IPPM framework~\cite{rfc2330},
and RFC~3432 makes a randomized start mandatory for periodic streams~\cite{rfc3432}.

Coordinated
omission~\cite{tene2015latency} is the mirror image. There the measurement fails to record samples
that would have been slow. Here it deletes samples that were fast. Millsap and Holt argue that
coarse timing survives in aggregate because the two signed errors
cancel~\cite[Sec.~7.7]{millsap2003oracle}. A filter admitting only positive samples removes one
sign, and with it the cancellation that defense depends on.

\paragraph{Sources of scheduling delay}
\label{sec:related_tail}
A runnable thread does not always run. Work-conservation violations are documented for CFS-era
schedulers~\cite{lozi2016wastedcores}, ours being EEVDF-era. Chandrasekar and
Kramberger~\cite{chandrasekar2026bias} apply a single-parameter queueing law to benchmark bias and
find, independently, that the harness's own scheduling inflates the latencies it reports. The
load-geometry contrast of Section~\ref{sec:twostate} refutes only that single-parameter form.

\section{Threats and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}
\label{sec:limitations}
Attributing the negatives to scheduling is an inference. One clock excludes clock skew, the
manipulations of Section~\ref{sec:twostate} exclude utilization, timer noise and stress, and
the kernel trace observes the wait directly. The interpreter lock is bounded twice. The kernel-only
estimator, which cannot see the lock, already brackets the observed rate, and \kafka{}'s Java
client shows the same failure. Both testbeds are virtual, so stolen processor time is bounded,
not excluded. Real-time priority, a control inside the guest, still cut the rate.

Disagreeing
clocks on different virtual CPUs~\cite{kvmclock} cannot explain the negatives either. A clock
that reads in $\clockIncrementNs$~ns, as ours did, can only be \clockAdmitted{}, both of which
the kernel uses only where the CPUs' clocks are guaranteed to agree. An offset large enough to produce
the deepest negative span would also have driven the delivery time $D$ below zero. Its floor bounds
any offset $\spanOffsetMargin$-fold below that. Across hosts
\texttt{chrony} bounds its own error at $\chronyHostBoundLo$--$\chronyHostBoundHi$~ms per host, so
we claim no cross-host bound in general, and a skew threshold such as Sharma et
al.'s~\cite{sharma2026causality} does not transfer to a loaded machine (Supplement~S3.6
and~S9.4).

The mechanism requires a timestamping path that can be preempted. Designs that pin a thread to a
dedicated core and busy-poll, or that timestamp in hardware, remove the preempted state, and we
make no claim about them. The mechanism's constants are those of one EEVDF-era kernel (6.8), and
CFS-era schedulers may shift them.

A registered campaign tested the mechanism's law on three
further pairs of machines. One prediction held on every campaign that tested it, all five on
\kafka{}, that load raises the negative-span rate. On \kafka{}, real-time priority removed the
negatives in every run, and at the one core count whose slice \kafka{}'s shortest delivery time
reaches, the rate's fall with the delivery time moved with that slice. Its predictions of where the fall sits and how wide it
is failed, and Python's rate exceeded Java's by more than the registered factor of $1.5$
(Supplement~S6).

The deletion arithmetic is demonstrated on one benchmark and reproduced in one
independent harness. The registered audit of Section~\ref{sec:reports} found its signature in few
published results, so we claim the regime for those and for our own runs only. We read the
\harnessAuditedWord{} tools of Section~\ref{sec:tools} at source and did not run them to
classify them. The \toolsRunWord{} of Section~\ref{sec:toolsrun} ran on one pair of machines,
one processor family and one kernel, at one message rate. The distributed mode of the benchmark
we measured in depth failed on every attempt, so its cross-host exposure
rests on source reading and our own two-host harness (Supplement~S4.6). Exposure depends on a deployment's path latency and producer rate, which is why
we recommend publishing retention rather than calling any published number wrong.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A latency benchmark measures the system only while its timestamps are taken faster than the
delivery time it measures. Once the message is the faster of the two, the arithmetic reports it
faster than light, and the tool deletes it. Two timestamps written by threads that each
wait for a core come out in the wrong order whenever one thread's wait exceeds the other's by
more than the time between the events they mark. A timestamp quantized to a millisecond and
filtered for positivity deletes every message that arrives before the millisecond digit
changes, about half on the path we measured and nearly all at some send rates. It reports the
survivors as exactly one millisecond, by arithmetic the operator never sees.

Neither failure announces itself. A finer clock cures only the second, and neither is expensive
to detect. Most of the tools we ran sidestep the first failure without curing it. Timing on one
clock, they never produce a negative, and they never time a one-way delivery either.

The practical rule is short. Before acting on a broker latency below a few milliseconds, ask
which timestamp it was timed from, how many samples the tool kept, and how many it discarded,
by sign. None of the $\reachReports$ published reports we audited answers the second. Check the
spans whose endpoints are timestamped in different places, count what your tool throws away,
and publish retention beside the number.
