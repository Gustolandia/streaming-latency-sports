\begin{abstract}
Message-broker benchmarks now compare systems on sub-millisecond paths, where the time being
measured is shorter than the clock's resolution and than a busy thread's wait for a
processor. There a benchmark can misreport in two ways its output does not announce. First, a
latency timed from the broker's acknowledgment turns negative whenever the thread recording it
is scheduled late by more than the delivery. Timed on one clock, $\spanNegAck$ of our
$\spanEvents$ messages were negative this way and none from the send, so the cause is
not clock synchronization; real-time priority for the timestamping threads, at unchanged utilization,
cut the rate $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$. A zero-cost sign check rejected
$\auditRejected$ of our $\auditRuns$ runs, including every run behind our first result.
Second, the OpenMessaging Benchmark timestamps in whole milliseconds and silently discards
every latency that is not positive: $\ombGridMedianCells$ of $\ombMedianCells$
embedded-mode settings whose summary we captured printed a median of exactly
$\ombGridPrintedLo$ or $\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms, computed from as little as
$\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of the samples. We derive the retained fraction from the ratio of
delivery to resolution, confirm a registered prediction of it, find uncounted disposal in
\harnessSilentWord{} of \harnessAuditedWord{} tools, and give benchmark authors checks that
cost nothing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{O}{ur} first harness timed each message from the moment the producer learned
that the broker had accepted it. By its arithmetic, $\spanNegAck$ of the $\spanEvents$
messages we timed arrived before they were sent. By the clock, none did: timed from the send
on the same clock, not one of them is negative (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoways}a). The arithmetic had
subtracted two timestamps written by two different threads, and a thread must wait for a
processor before it can read the clock. That wait is the \emph{scheduling
delay}\label{def:scheddelay}: the time a thread that is ready to run spends queued before a
core runs it. When the thread that records the acknowledgment waits longer than the message
takes to arrive, the difference comes out negative. Nothing arrived early; one reading was
taken late. Users of the benchmark discussed next have met the same ordering, a publish
latency above the end-to-end latency on a single node. They were told it was clock skew
between nodes~\cite{omb_issue216}.

That benchmark, the OpenMessaging Benchmark~\cite{openmessaging2018}, is the one broker
comparisons share. It has a sensible rule for such faster-than-light samples, which it
expects from machines whose clocks disagree: it discards every latency that is not
positive~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}. But its end-to-end clock counts whole milliseconds, and to
that clock a message that arrives before the digit changes took no time at all, which is
faster than light too. The same rule therefore discards every such message: on the
benchmark's own path about half of them, and at some send rates nearly all
(Section~\ref{sec:extcomp}). Applied to our own corpus, it would delete
$\msGuardDeletedPct\%$ of the spans without a count (Fig.~\ref{fig:twoways}b).
In $\ombGridMedianCells$ of the $\ombMedianCells$ settings whose own summary we captured, it
printed a median of exactly $\ombGridPrintedLo$ or
$\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms. The median came from as little as $\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of the
samples taken, and nothing in the output said how few.

Neither failure announces itself. The second leaves a signature that nobody reads: every
end-to-end percentile the benchmark prints sits on the millisecond grid. It also makes the
benchmark look better. A median computed from $\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of the samples is
typographically identical to one computed from all of them, and more stable from run to run for
having its variation deleted. The tool passes the
reproducibility a reader checks because it fails the verifiability nobody does. Nor is this
one project's defect. The tools that time broker paths are few and shared, and Apache
\kafka{}'s own end-to-end tool reports zero at every percentile on such a
path~\cite{kafka_tools}. Clock resolution and scheduling delay are each well understood.
What was not known is what they do to the sub-millisecond comparisons that broker benchmarks
now publish, because the tools that produce those comparisons discard the evidence that would
show it.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
We make three contributions, each established by manipulation rather than by observation
alone.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The first failure, and its cause} (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). A latency
referenced to an acknowledgment timestamp turns negative when the thread that writes it is
scheduled late by more than the delivery. Real-time priority for the timestamping threads cut
the rate $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$ at unchanged utilization. Two placements of the
same load at identical utilization differed twofold, so utilization alone does not set the
rate. The same mechanism appears in \kafka{}'s own Java client and on a second processor
family.
\item \textbf{The second failure, derived and confirmed} (Section~\ref{sec:external}). With a
millisecond clock and a positivity filter, the share of samples a benchmark keeps is set by
the ratio of the delivery to the clock's resolution and by the phase of its send schedule. A
registered manipulation of message size moved a setting across the boundary this predicts.
\harnessSilentWordCap{} of \harnessAuditedWord{} tools we read at source dispose of such
samples without counting them, and a registered audit of $\reachReports$ published reports
found the signature in \reachPrimaryConfigsWord{} configurations and a stated retention in none
(Section~\ref{sec:tools}).
\item \textbf{Checks that cost nothing} (Section~\ref{sec:authors}). A per-run sign check
rejected $\auditRejected$ of our $\auditRuns$ runs, every run behind our own first result
among them. All had passed every conventional check we knew. Publishing the retained fraction
beside every latency exposes the second failure the same way.
\end{enumerate}
Both processes read one clock unless the text says otherwise, so nothing here depends on clock
synchronization.

\section{How a Benchmark Times a Message}
\label{sec:sysmodel}
\label{sec:metrics}
\label{sec:method}

A producer process sends recorded events at a paced rate to a broker, and a consumer process
reads them back. Four timestamps bracket a message's path. The first, $t_{\mathrm{sched}}$,
is when the event was due to be sent, and $t_{\mathrm{send}}$ is taken immediately before the
send call. Then $t_{\mathrm{ack}}$ is when the producer learns that the broker accepted the
message, and $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ is when the consumer holds the record. Three quantities
follow:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
D &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{send}}, \qquad
A \equiv t_{\mathrm{ack}} - t_{\mathrm{send}}, \\
S &\equiv t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{ack}} \;=\; D - A .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
$D$ is the \emph{delivery time}, from the send call to the consumer holding the record. $A$ is
the \emph{acknowledgment lag}, from the send call to the producer observing the broker's
acknowledgment. $S$ is what a harness reports when it times a message from the acknowledgment
rather than from the send, as our own first harness did. We call it the \emph{transport
proxy}, for the reason given below. The end-to-end latency runs from the event falling due to
the consumer holding it:
\begin{equation}
E = t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{sched}}
  = \underbrace{(t_{\mathrm{send}}-t_{\mathrm{sched}})}_{\text{send lag}} + A + S .
\label{eq:tti}
\end{equation}
The \emph{send lag} is how late the send call ran after the event was due. A \emph{span} is
any difference of two of these timestamps, and a \emph{negative span} is one whose difference
is below zero. Write $\tau$ for the \emph{timestamp resolution}, one millisecond in the
benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:external}. Write $T_{\mathrm{true}}$\label{def:ttrue} for the
\emph{true delivery time} of a message, the interval $D$ would report if its timestamps were
arbitrarily fine. \emph{Retention} is the fraction of the samples a measurement tool takes
that survive into what it reports.

\subsection{The transport proxy is not a causal chain}
\label{sec:proxy}
\label{sec:model}
$D$ and $E$ are chains of events, each preceding the next. $S$ is not. The broker's append
precedes both the producer's receipt of the acknowledgment and the consumer's receipt of the
record, but neither of them precedes the other. They are two branches of one cause. Since
Equation~\ref{eq:model} holds per event, whatever the signs,
\begin{equation}
S < 0 \quad\text{if and only if}\quad A > D .
\label{eq:negspan}
\end{equation}
The identity is checked on every build: both sides count $\spanNegAck$ negatives over our
$\diseaseEvents$ events, and the build fails if they part. Every timestamp is written some time
after the event it marks, because the thread that reads the clock must first be scheduled
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model}). That delay is not the cost of the clock call, which is small and
separately measurable~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. It is the wait before the call runs at all.
A negative $S$ therefore does not show that either timestamp is wrong. It shows that the
acknowledgment cannot serve as the origin of a delivery latency for that event, which is the
use an acknowledgment-referenced harness puts it to. The same holds wherever the origin
timestamp is written by a thread that must first be woken, such as a delivery-report poll or
a completion reaper (Supplement~S30). A fixed distribution of $A$ overlaps a small $D$ almost
entirely and a large one hardly at all. That is why a path whose transport runs to tens of
seconds passes every run, while the same hardware measuring one millisecond fails wholesale.

\subsection{Testbeds, clocks and workload}
\label{sec:testbeds}
\label{sec:clocks}
\label{sec:workload}
The measured findings of Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism} and~\ref{sec:external} come from four
Oracle Cloud \brk{VM.Standard.E5.Flex} instances, three brokers and one driver. They run
Ubuntu 22.04 on kernel \brk{6.8.0-1057-oracle} (EEVDF scheduler) and CPython 3.10 on a
private network, with \texttt{chrony}-disciplined clocks logged every minute. The producer and
the consumer run as separate processes on one host and read one clock
(\brk{time.time_ns()}). A workstation running the brokers under containers enters only
through the audit of Section~\ref{sec:audit}. One early phase perturbed the measurement enough
to be unusable and is excluded from every result here; the campaigns that remain measure
utilization with no core pinning. A later registered campaign ran on three pairs
of Microsoft Azure machines, two x86 and one Neoverse~N2. The workload is
StatsBomb's open football event data~\cite{statsbomb2023}: \replayedMatchesWord{} of its
$\corpusMatches$ matches, replayed in order at a chosen send rate. It stands for small,
frequent, ordered messages, and nothing below depends on its content beyond message size and
rate. The achieved send rate is recorded per run against elapsed wall time,
and pacer jitter is $\pacerJitterLo$--$\pacerJitterHi\,\mu$s at p90 over $\pacerJitterRuns$
runs.

\subsection{Terms}
\label{def:vocabulary}
A \emph{condition} is one point of the experimental design: a broker, a send rate, a message
size, a background load and a consumer pattern. A \emph{cell} is a condition together with a
workload; where the workload is fixed the two coincide. A \emph{run} is one execution of a
cell, and a \emph{replicate} is a further run of the same cell. Where a manipulation compares
settings that differ in one variable, each setting is a \emph{configuration} of that
manipulation. Utilization\label{def:rho} $\rho$ is the fraction of processor time busy on the
host, as measured over the run. The core count $k$\label{def:kcores} is
the number of cores the background load occupies. Two settings that reach the same
utilization with the load on different numbers of cores differ in \emph{load
geometry}\label{def:geometry}: \emph{concentrated} on few cores or \emph{spread} over many.

\subsection{The sign check}
\label{sec:gate}
\label{sec:stats}
A run is rejected if more than $1\%$ of its events carry a negative value in any latency
component, or if the median of any component is negative. A condition is usable only if all of
its runs survive. We fixed the rule before the final campaign, after earlier campaigns had run,
and apply it to every run, including those whose results looked reasonable. The one-per-cent
line is a convention, and it decides nothing we report: swept from $0$ to $20\%$, it makes none
of our first result's rejected conditions usable (Supplement~S18). The reason is not that a negative is impossible, since
for the transport proxy it is not: a run whose reference timestamp fails on more than one event
in a hundred cannot report a latency, whatever the cause. A run that passes has cleared the
cheapest available bar and nothing more. Every proportion below is a count over a stated
denominator. The rates of Table~\ref{tab:mechanism} carry Wilson score $95\%$ intervals and
each of its ratios a Katz interval, Holm-corrected within a named family; the corrected value
is the one the supplement's tables display. Every table
is generated from the committed data at build time.

\section{Failure 1: Late Timestamps Invert the Acknowledgment-Referenced Span}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The negatives in our corpus are produced by scheduling. We establish this by moving scheduling
while holding everything else fixed.

\subsection{What the sign check finds}
\label{sec:audit}
The check rejects $\auditRejectedWorkstation$ of $\auditRunsWorkstation$ runs on the
workstation testbed ($\auditPctWorkstation\%$) and $\auditRejectedCloud$ of $\auditRunsCloud$
on the cloud testbed ($\auditPctCloud\%$). Every run behind our first result is among them: on
that first testbed $\auditUsableConditionsWorkstation$ of $\auditConditionsWorkstation$
conditions survive. Every rejected run had passed every conventional check we knew. Our first
corpus had survived six corrections, a fairness audit, and a full battery of rank tests, effect

\label{sec:blindspot}
The sign sees only the worst cases. The acknowledgment lag exceeds half the delivery time on
$\diseaseOverHalf\%$ of events and a tenth of it on $\diseaseOverTenth\%$, but the check fires
only on the $\diseaseOverWhole\%$ where it exceeds the whole. The two delays are correlated
within a run (median correlation $\spanRhoWithinMedian$ over $\spanRhoConditions$ conditions),
one scheduler causing both. Treating
the two as independent within a run would overpredict them $\indepWithinFloored$-fold, the
median of the $\indepWithinFlooredN$ conditions above $\indepFloorPct\%$. A
delivery longer than the acknowledgment lag is therefore not clean, only quiet. Load-induced
inflation that stays positive passes the check by construction: the check is necessary, not
sufficient.

\subsection{The cause, by manipulation}
\label{sec:mixture}
\label{sec:twostate}
Each timestamping thread is either running, in which case its timestamp is late by a narrow
jitter, or preempted and off the processor. Write $p$ for the probability of the second state,
the thread's \emph{occupancy}\label{def:occupancy}. To leading order the negative-span rate is
the occupancy times the chance that a residual stall outlasts the delivery. Load changes how
often the thread that records the time is off the processor, not how wide the ordinary jitter
is. From idle to the knee the rate grows $\invGrowth\times$ and the jitter's width only
$\coreGrowth\times$; the highest rate near saturation, $\invCeiling$, is a maximum over those
conditions and not a bound (Supplement~S9). Load moves $p$ but does not fix it, as the
geometry rows below show,
so we write $p$ and not $p(\rho)$. The model predicts how the rate falls as the path lengthens, not how it rises with
load.

Real-time priority on the timestamping threads moves the occupancy while utilization stays
fixed. The rate falls by factors of $\rtLowFactor$ and $\rtHighFactor$

shares no probe or data with the application-level rate. It predicts that rate to within a
third across $\tracedRatioArms$ configurations, with nothing fitted: ratios $\tracedRatios$, no
consistent sign. The middle ratio comes from a configuration our own tracer check, fixed in
advance, withholds; the two it admits still straddle one. The probe is not free: the rate was $\untracedRate$ untraced against
$\tracedRate$ traced ($z = \observerZ$), so each prediction is compared with the traced run's
own rate.

The mechanism belongs neither to our Python clients nor to one processor family. A later
registered campaign on three further pairs of machines found the same fall with the delivery
in \kafka{}'s own Java client, and the same removal by real-time priority (Supplement Part~V).
The Java client's rate was a third to a quarter of the Python client's. The same
negative spans appeared on Neoverse~N2 processors as on x86. Our timestamp is a CPython call and
also waits on the interpreter lock; the Java client, which has none, shows the same failure at a
lower rate.

\subsection{Where the timescale comes from}
\label{sec:tail}
The scheduling delay behind these negatives is not a single heavy tail. Over the
$\tracedEvents$ traced wakeups the counts have $\tracedModes$ local maxima. The last, in the
$\tracedModeLo$--$\tracedModeHi$~ms bucket, holds $\tracedModeShare\%$ of all wakeups and
stands $\tracedModeRatio\times$ its lower neighbor. Above it the counts collapse: they fall
$\tracedModeFallA\times$, $\tracedModeFallB\times$ and $\tracedModeFallC\times$ over the next
\tracedModeFallOctaves{} octaves and then $\tracedLastBucketFall\times$, where a power law falls
by one factor every time (Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Gregg reported the distribution as bimodal in the post
introducing \texttt{runqlat}~\cite{gregg2016runqlat}, and the \texttt{timerlat} tracer
separates its interrupt and thread components~\cite{bristot2025timerlat}. What we add is the
upper mode's position. It is consistent with the scheduler's base slice: the kernel's
published rule~\cite{zhou2025slice} gives $\sliceFactor \times 0.75\,\mathrm{ms} =
\baseSliceMs$~ms on the $\testbedCpus$ cores the campaign's load recovers. That slice is
derived rather than measured. With run-to-parity, a task's lag is held within one slice plus
one tick~\cite{guittot2025slice}.

A thread woken while another task holds the processor waits out what is left of that task's
slice, up to a whole one. That is $\sliceOverDeliveryLo$--$\sliceOverDeliveryHi$ times the
per-condition deliveries this corpus measures, $\condDeliveryLoMs$--$\condDeliveryHiMs$~ms.
The registered campaign timed that wait afterwards, acknowledgment by acknowledgment, and found
the rest of a slice rather than a whole one. That reading was made after its verdicts
(Supplement~S16.10). The slice lands on the scale of the interval being measured, which is why
the check fires on a substantial minority of events. The
scheduler's tick is also $\tickMs$~ms here, by coincidence of configuration; it is not the
timestamp resolution $\tau$.

\subsection{What it costs, and the repair}
\label{sec:cost}
The acknowledgment lag $A$ is a scheduling quantity. It does not grow because a message travelled
further, so the relative error of the transport proxy is $A/D$ and falls as $1/D$. At our
median acknowledgment lag, $A = \ackLagMedianUs\,\mu$s, the error is $\exposureErrOne\%$ at a
$1$~ms delivery, $\exposureErrTen\%$ at $10$~ms and $\exposureErrHundred\%$ at $100$~ms. Below
$\exposureCrossover$~ms the lag exceeds the delivery it is a correction to. Across
conditions $A$ runs $\exposureLagLo$--$\exposureLagHi\,\mu$s between its tenth and ninetieth
percentiles. That puts the $10$~ms error at $\exposureErrTenLo$--$\exposureErrTenHi\%$ and the
crossover at $\exposureCrossoverLo$--$\exposureCrossoverHi$~ms (Fig.~\ref{fig:exposure}).

For a harness that reports $S$, two consequences follow on the gated corpus. The reported span
is $\reportedFraction\%$ of the delivery it stands for, understating it $\understateFactor\times$.
That is a median of per-condition ratios over $\spanRatioConditions$ conditions, with an
interquartile range of $\understateIQRLo$--$\understateIQRHi\times$. And because the two
brokers' clients timestamp differently, the proxy inflates the gap between two systems
$\gapDistortionPaired\times$ over $\gapDistortionPairs$ matched workloads, a property of the
clients that a reader would attribute to the brokers.

The acknowledgment lag can be added back. Equation~\ref{eq:model} holds per event
and $A$ is producer-side on one clock. A harness that records its own acknowledgment lag
therefore recovers the delivery it failed to measure. Its median error is $\recoveryErrPass\%$
on the $\recoveryPassN$ conditions the check passes and $\recoveryErrFail\%$ on the
$\recoveryFailN$ it rejects, with upper quartiles of $\recoveryErrPassHi\%$ and
$\recoveryErrFailHi\%$: two populations the data cannot tell apart (Supplement~S16.9).
This does not reopen the check of Section~\ref{sec:gate}: the check declines to publish the
proxy as a latency, and this reports the delivery instead.

\section{Failure 2: A Millisecond Clock and a Positivity Filter}
\label{sec:external}

The cross-broker benchmark we audit computes its reported latency distribution from a subset of
the samples it collects. It does not record how large that subset was, and it lets the send rate
decide. Everything measured below is the OpenMessaging Benchmark in embedded mode, where driver
and workers run in one JVM; its distributed mode failed on every attempt
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{The filter, and its origin}
\label{sec:extmethod}
In \brk{LocalWorker.java}:294 the end-to-end latency is \texttt{now - publishTimestamp}. Here
\texttt{now} is read in the consumer and \texttt{publishTimestamp} is \kafka{}'s producer-set
\texttt{CreateTime}, both in milliseconds. The difference is promoted to microseconds. The \emph{positivity filter} at \brk{WorkerStats.java}:95
then admits a sample to the histogram only when \texttt{endToEndLatencyMicros > 0}. The message
is counted as received a few lines earlier, but a non-positive latency is dropped and no counter
records how many. Over the campaign below that silence covers $\ombDiscarded$ samples.

The filter's origin is documented and its reasoning is sound. It was added in 2018 because
end-to-end latency ``is calculated based on time difference of
\texttt{System.currentTimeMillis()} on two different machines'' and ``can be
negative''~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}. The recorder is an \texttt{HdrHistogram}
\texttt{Recorder}, which rejects negative values outright, so filtering for positives is the
reasonable local fix, defensive rather than evasive. A benchmark that times a message faster
than light has two choices: announce the discovery, or delete the message. This one deletes it.
The fix is sound locally, and it has two non-local consequences. First, \texttt{> 0} also
deletes every sample that computes to exactly zero, and with a millisecond clock every delivery
shorter than a millisecond does. Second, the one observation that would show the measurement
had failed is the exact observation the filter removes, and Section~\ref{sec:tools} finds the same uncounted disposal in
\harnessSilentIndependentWord{} independent tools. The filter's scope is telling. It sits on the
\ombGuardedPath{} path, timestamped in \ombGuardedClock{}s, while the same harness times its
\ombUnguardedPath{} span in \ombUnguardedClock{}s and records every sample. The coarse clock and
the filter both landed on the one span that crosses processes.

\subsection{What it did}
\label{sec:extphase}
Of the $\ombMedianCells$ instrumented cells whose own summary we captured, $\ombGridMedianCells$
report a median of $\ombGridPrintedLo$ or $\ombGridPrintedHi$~ms. Those medians were computed
from between $\ombGridRetentionMin$ and $\ombGridRetentionMax\%$ of the samples the benchmark
took (Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}b). Two replicates of one cell, $200$~B at $500$~msg/s on one host,
kept $\ombKeptLo$ and $\ombKeptHi$ samples, a $\ombRetentionFold$-fold difference. Both reported
a median of $\ombGridPrintedLo$~ms, with nothing in the output to distinguish them. Three
uninstrumented runs printed p50, p95, p99 and max all equal to $\ombGridPrintedLo$. Like a
stopped clock, the printed median is exactly right whenever the true one happens to be a
millisecond. Over the full ledger of $\ombRuns$ instrumented runs the retained fraction falls as
low as $\ombRetentionMinExact\%$, about four samples in ninety thousand. The only cells that
escape are the \ombEscapeCellsWord{} whose payload is large enough that the resolution stops
binding.

\subsection{The retention law, and a registered confirmation}
\label{sec:extcomp}
Publish latency across these cells takes only two values, $\ombPubLatLo$ and $\ombPubLatHi$~ms,
while retention varies $\ombRetentionFold$-fold. What moves retention is not the path but where
the producer's send instants fall against the \emph{grid}: the instants, one millisecond apart,
at which the timestamp changes value. A sample survives when its delivery crosses a grid instant,
which for $T_{\mathrm{true}} < \tau$ happens with probability $T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:deletion}a):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:retention}
\text{retention} \;=\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{T_{\mathrm{true}}}{\tau}\right)
\qquad\text{(uniform phases).}
\end{equation}
All \spreadIncommensurateWord{} configurations whose send interval is incommensurate with $\tau$
sit near $50\%$, with medians from $\spreadIncommensurateLo$ to $\spreadIncommensurateHi$. That
implies $T_{\mathrm{true}} = \spreadIncommensurateTrueMs$~ms at $\tau = 1$~ms. Where the send
interval is commensurate with the tick, the retained fraction is confined to a few values, so
moving $T_{\mathrm{true}}$ moves a configuration between them (Supplement~S11 tests every
configuration). That prediction was registered before its runs, and it came true. Replicate
spread collapses to $\flipSpreadThirtyTwo$ points at $32$~KB and re-opens to
$\flipSpreadSixtyFour$ at $64$~KB, a boundary crossed in both directions by one manipulated
variable (Supplement~S10). One registered detail failed: the $32$~KB configuration pins at
$\flipPinThirtyTwo\%$ retention, not on the predicted vertex at $\flipVertexTwoThirds\%$.

The arithmetic is not ours. Hewlett-Packard's Application Note 162-1~\cite{hp1970tia} carries
the retention identity, the reduced fraction the grid turns on, the ceiling on resolution gain,
the symptom and the cure (Supplement~S17 sets them side by side). What is new is the sign of the
operation. The counter keeps both readings, and the retained fraction is its estimator. A harness
released in 2018 throws away the quantity a 1970 counter measured with.

\subsection{Our own harness, and the sign channel}
\label{sec:generality}
To show the deletion is not an artifact of one JVM, we reproduced the filter in an independent
Python harness sharing no code with the benchmark. On one clock it
recorded zero negative differences across $\harnessOneClockSamples$ samples, as a causal chain
requires, and the grid behavior reappeared intact. Separating the discards by sign is what makes
the filter's cost legible. Across the \kafka{}-driver corpus's $\ombKafkaRuns$ runs, its
$\ombKafkaDiscarded$ discarded samples contain not one negative: the most negative value at any
load, size or rate is $0\,\mu$s. Those discards are resolution
failures, not clock failures, a distinction an unsigned counter cannot make. The same channel
caught the real thing in the \redis{}-driver replication's $\ombRedisRuns$ runs:
$\ombRedisNegatives$ negatives, every one exactly $-1000\,\mu$s. That is the smallest value a
one-millisecond timestamp can express, and it is no coincidence. The \redis{} driver's span is a
difference between two millisecond-floored clocks on two hosts. A sub-tick offset between two
floored clocks can produce exactly one negative value, $-1000\,\mu$s, and no other. Clock
discipline was clean at every logged minute: this failure needs no misbehaving clock, only two of
them and a floor (Supplement~S14).

\section{How Widespread}
\label{sec:tools}
The disposal is a pattern across the tooling. We read
\harnessAuditedWord{} tools at source, across \harnessLanguagesWord{} languages and
\harnessVendorsWord{} vendors, and classified each with the code that classified the benchmark
(Table~\ref{tab:tools}). \harnessSilentWordCap{} dispose of the sample without counting it, in
\harnessDisposalClassesWord{} classes: filtering, refusal by the tool's own library, and
substitution.
Substitution is the worst because it leaves nothing missing: \texttt{fio} and \texttt{btt}
substitute zero and one nanosecond, and a value never measured enters the distribution. Apache
Pulsar's performance client admits zero~\cite{pulsar_perf}. It drops negatives and keeps the
samples the benchmark deletes, which then pile up visibly at zero. Apache \kafka{}'s own
end-to-end tool keeps every sample and loses it anyway. It fills the array its percentiles come
from by integer division, so on a sub-millisecond path every percentile it reports is
zero~\cite{kafka_tools}.

\section{What Benchmark Authors Should Do}
\label{sec:discussion}
\label{sec:authors}
Both failures are cheap to detect, and Table~\ref{tab:checks} lists the checks. SPEC and TPC
judge a benchmark on five criteria~\cite[Sec.~3]{kistowski2015build}. The tool we audit passes
the half of \emph{reproducibility} a reader checks, run-to-run consistency at a fixed
configuration, because it fails \emph{verifiability}. A median from $\ombGridRetentionMin\%$ of
the samples is the more stable for having its variation deleted, and a second tester who picks a
different send rate measures a different sample. Better measurement systems
exist~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler,kogias2019lancet,kundel2024p4sta}, and the checks apply to
whatever they measure. Every check in the table restores verifiability, and every one has a
precedent. Network measurement has published the share of
traces it rejected for nearly thirty years~\cite{paxson1998calibrating,paxson2004sound}. OWAMP
records a packet timestamped in the future unaltered rather than discarding it~\cite{rfc4656}.
Randomizing probe times against periodic phenomena is standing advice~\cite{rfc2330,rfc3432}.
What we add is the conjunction, applied where a benchmark reports a latency, with the rejection
rate published as a headline quantity.

The last check is the one mitigation our measurements support directly: real-time priority on
the timestamping threads cut the negative-span rate $\rtFactorLow$--$\rtFactorHigh\times$ at
unchanged background load. Busy-polling a dedicated core should buy the same for the price of
that core, though we did not measure it. The floor is not zero, so the check stays, and a
real-time or polling timestamping thread changes the system it measures; where that is
unacceptable, report retention instead.

\label{sec:betterclock}
A better-synchronized clock fixes neither failure. Hardware-timestamped PTP~\cite{ieee1588} would
take two hosts from our $\interHostOffsetUs\,\mu$s of \texttt{chrony} agreement to under a
microsecond. Behind a one-millisecond timestamp the two produce byte-identical records: the filter
deletes the same samples and the retention arithmetic is unchanged. A finer clock does fix the
second failure on these paths. It retires Equation~\ref{eq:retention}'s deletions and moves the
grid to faster paths, but it leaves the filter and every genuine negative in place. The
remedies are therefore ordered: record at native resolution, count what is
discarded, dither the send instant, and only then does synchronization quality matter.

\label{sec:brokers}
Once the failed runs are removed, the broker comparison that motivated the work is small. On the
end-to-end latency, which is a causal chain, the two brokers are equivalent against a wider
margin (Supplement~S13); on the transport proxy they sit within a millisecond (TOST against a
$\tostMargin$~ms margin, $p < 0.001$, across $\tostLevels$ levels). So it is not a purchasing
argument. One consumption pattern, which a co-located testbed hides, reverses their order. Our own
measurement is not symmetric across that comparison. \kafka{}'s client deserializes the payload
before $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ and \redis{}'s after it, which is worth $\spanHandlingWorstUs\,\mu$s or
$\spanHandlingSharePct\%$ of \redis{}'s median delivery. No reader could have
known that had we not said so, and the end-to-end latency is free of it.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
That measurement error can dominate a benchmark is established. What prior work does not supply
is a check applied to every run, and a count of what the check removes.

\subsection{Broker benchmarks}
\label{sec:related_bench}
Broker comparisons appear continuously in peer-reviewed venues and in the gray literature. Apache
\kafka{} and \redis{} Streams are the pair they most often name, and the OpenMessaging
Benchmark~\cite{openmessaging2018} is the tool they share. That experimental setup can dominate a
result is established~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}, and how to report one is a settled
literature~\cite{georges2007rigorous,hoefler2015benchmarking}. What that literature does not do is

% 64(5), 2015 -- bib key chen2015statcomparisons -- is prepared as the swap for
% georges2007rigorous if an editor asks for a venue citation on statistical reporting. The
% referee advised against swapping unprompted: Georges is the better-known citation and every

check what its tool discards. Karimov et al.~\cite{karimov2018benchmarking} separate event-time
from processing-time latency. Van~Dongen and Van~den~Poel~\cite{vandongen2020evaluation} use
single-broker append timestamps ``to ensure correct latency measurements'': our one-clock rule,
reached earlier and by another route. Neither asks what a millisecond timestamp does to a sub-millisecond
path, nor which samples the tool keeps. Where a comparison reports a median above the tool's
one-millisecond timestamp resolution, the deletion arithmetic of Section~\ref{sec:external} does
not bind. Not every comparison does: a 2026 comparison names a percentile for one broker and
gives the other two only ``sub-millisecond''~\cite{indexdev2026brokers}, so its ranking cannot be
placed against the resolution at all.

\subsection{Cross-process time}
\label{sec:related_time}
Lamport~\cite{lamport1978time} established that a distributed system has no single physical time,
only a causal partial order, and that order decides this paper's central span. Measurement
systems defeat clock disagreement~\cite{ieee1588} by calibrating cross-node
counters~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler}, timestamping in the network
interface~\cite{kogias2019lancet} or the switch dataplane~\cite{kundel2024p4sta}, and measuring
the timer's own accuracy~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. Villain et al.~\cite{villain2012probing},
profiling FreeBSD against a hardware reference, found that the outgoing socket timestamp ``does
not respect causality'' under every stress pattern, including none. They attributed host latency
to interrupt processing, scheduling and context switching: the mechanism of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, measured more precisely than we measure it. What it does not give is
the consequence for a two-process measurement: the negative-span rate falls as the delivery being
measured grows. The manipulations that establish it separate scheduling from its rival
explanations (Section~\ref{sec:twostate}). Paxson~\cite{paxson1998calibrating} treated physically
inconsistent transit times as measurement failure and published the share of traces affected.
Lancet~\cite[Sec.~4.3]{kogias2019lancet} reports an inconclusive verdict when a run's samples fail
its stability test. Neither reports the share of runs a campaign lost. Production tracing reached
a related answer without a mechanism: skew adjusters that rewrite a span's timestamps are called
harmful and have defaulted to off since 2020~\cite{jaeger_harmful}. Sharma et
al.~\cite{sharma2026causality} count negative timing spans in distributed inference systems. They
hold that ``queueing alone cannot produce negative timing spans'' and read the negatives as clock
skew. For the queue they model, we agree. A second queue decides ours: the run queue the
timestamping thread waits in.

\subsection{Resolution and discarded samples}
\label{sec:related_resolution}
That a timer coarser than the interval it measures invalidates the measurement is
textbook~\cite{kuperberg2011timers}. Metrology carries both failures in one uncertainty budget:
the NIST stopwatch guide budgets reaction time beside display resolution, and notes that at short
intervals the resolution becomes significant against the stopwatch's rated
accuracy~\cite{nist2009stopwatch}. That periodic sampling
commensurate with a periodic phenomenon sees only part of it is the IPPM framework~\cite{rfc2330},
and RFC~3432 makes a randomized start mandatory for periodic streams~\cite{rfc3432}. Coordinated
omission~\cite{tene2015latency} is the mirror image. There the measurement fails to record samples
that would have been slow; here it deletes samples that were fast. Millsap and Holt argue that
coarse timing survives in aggregate because the two signed errors
cancel~\cite[Sec.~7.7]{millsap2003oracle}. A filter admitting only positive samples removes one
sign, and with it the cancellation that defense depends on.

\paragraph{Sources of scheduling delay}
\label{sec:related_tail}
A runnable thread does not always run. Work-conservation violations are documented for CFS-era
schedulers~\cite{lozi2016wastedcores}, ours being EEVDF-era. Chandrasekar and
Kramberger~\cite{chandrasekar2026bias} apply a single-parameter queueing law to benchmark bias and
reach our inflation half independently. The load-geometry contrast of Section~\ref{sec:twostate}
refutes that single-parameter form, not queueing itself.

\section{Threats and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}
\label{sec:limitations}
Attributing the negatives to scheduling is an inference, separated from its rivals by
manipulation. One clock excludes clock skew, and identical $\rho$ at rates twofold apart excludes
utilization. \texttt{SCHED\_FIFO} excludes timer noise, which does not respect scheduling class. A
longer transport that lowers the rate excludes stress. A tracepoint histogram that predicts the
rate unfitted is direct observation. The interpreter lock is bounded twice: the kernel-only
estimator, which cannot see the lock, already brackets the observed rate, and
\kafka{}'s Java client shows the same failure (Section~\ref{sec:twostate}). Clock incoherence
across virtual CPUs~\cite{kvmclock} is closed twice as well. The probe's $\clockIncrementNs$~ns
increment admits only \clockAdmitted{}, both of which the kernel uses solely where cross-CPU
coherence is asserted, and the send-referenced span's floor is $\spanOffsetMargin\times$ too high to hide the deepest negative
(Supplement~S15). Across hosts \texttt{chrony} bounds its own error at
$\chronyHostBoundLo$--$\chronyHostBoundHi$~ms per host, so we claim no cross-host bound in
general, and the skew threshold of Section~\ref{sec:related_time} does not transfer to a loaded
machine (Supplement~S33).

The mechanism requires a timestamping path that can be preempted. Designs that pin a thread to a
dedicated core and busy-poll, or that timestamp in hardware, remove the preempted state, and we
make no claim about them. The mechanism's constants are those of one EEVDF-era kernel (6.8), and
CFS-era schedulers may shift them. A registered campaign tested the mechanism's law on three
further pairs of machines. One prediction held on every campaign: load raises the negative-span
rate. On \kafka{} real-time priority removed the negatives, and the number of online CPUs moved
the fall through the slice it sets. Its predictions of where the fall sits and how wide it is failed, and Python's rate
exceeded Java's by more than the registered factor of $1.5$ (Supplement Part~V). The deletion
arithmetic is demonstrated on one benchmark and reproduced in one independent harness, and the
registered audit of Section~\ref{sec:tools} found its signature in few published results, so we
claim the regime for those and for our own runs, not for the literature at large. The \harnessAuditedWord{} tools of Section~\ref{sec:tools} were read at source, not
deployed. The benchmark's distributed mode failed on every attempt, so its cross-host exposure
rests on source reading and our own two-host harness (Supplement~S24). Exposure depends on a deployment's path latency and producer rate, which is why
we recommend publishing a retention rate rather than calling any published number wrong.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A latency benchmark measures the system only while its timestamps are taken faster than the
delivery it measures. Once the message is the faster of the two, the arithmetic reports it
faster than light, and the benchmark deletes it. Two timestamps written by threads that each
wait for a core invert the delivery between them whenever those waits differ by more than it. A
timestamp quantized to a millisecond and filtered for positivity deletes most of a
sub-millisecond path, by arithmetic the operator never sees, and reports the survivors as
exactly one millisecond. Neither failure announces itself. A finer clock cures only the second,
and neither is expensive to detect. Check the spans whose endpoints are timestamped in different places, count
what your tool throws away, and publish the retention rate beside the number.
