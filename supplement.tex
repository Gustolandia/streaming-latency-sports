\subsection{S1.1. Testbeds, clocks and workload}
\label{s:setup-testbeds}

Two testbeds carry the evidence (Table~\ref{stab:testbeds}). On the cloud testbed the producer and
the consumer run as separate processes on one host with $\testbedCpus$ online CPUs, and read one
wall clock, \textsc{clock\_realtime}, through \brk{time.time_ns()}. A wall clock gives every host
one epoch, which the cross-host runs need, and on one host its readings came out in order: no
latency timed from the publish call falls below zero (\ref{s:setup-timestamps}). No scheduler tunable is
written anywhere in the archived campaign, which is strong evidence, short of proof, that the
kernel's defaults applied, among them the derived base slice of $\baseSliceMs$~ms
(Section~\ref{P-sec:tail}). The clocksource was not recorded, but a measured read cost narrows
down which one it was (\ref{s:rivals}). Across hosts, \texttt{chrony} reports an inter-host
offset of $\interHostOffsetMs$~ms but bounds its own error at
$\chronyHostBoundLo$--$\chronyHostBoundHi$~ms per host over the $\chronyHosts$ hosts captured, far
more than the offset, so we claim no cross-host bound.

Both testbeds start the brokers with one script, \brk{cloud/brokers.sh}. \kafka{} 4.1.1, from the
image \brk{apache/kafka:4.1.1}, runs as a single node that is also its own controller, the role
that keeps the cluster's metadata (KRaft mode). \redis{} 7, from the image \brk{redis:7}, runs
beside it on the same broker machine. On the cloud testbed both brokers ran on one of the three
broker machines. In a control of 50 runs for each broker, the \emph{co-located control}, both ran
on the client machine itself. The clients are
the Python libraries \texttt{kafka-python} and \texttt{redis-py}, and the registered campaign also
ran \kafka{}'s own Java client (\ref{s:law-design}). Table~\ref{stab:testbeds} gives each testbed's
machines and versions, and Table~\ref{stab:settings} the settings of the brokers and the clients.

The producer replays StatsBomb's open football event data~\cite{statsbomb2023}. On the cloud
testbed it replays \replayedMatchesWord{} of its $\corpusMatches$ matches, in order, at a chosen
publish rate. The registered campaign replays a steady 50 messages a second, except in its block
M0, which replays a match's bursts.

\subsection{S1.3. Where each timestamp is taken, and which spans fall below zero}
\label{s:setup-timestamps}

The publish timestamp is read on the calling thread just before the client is invoked.
The two clients read the acknowledgment timestamp in different places, but in both it is read
only after a thread that was not running has been woken and then given a core. Under \redis{} it
is read on the calling thread the moment the synchronous \texttt{xadd} returns, with no second
thread involved. Under \kafka{} it is read, by default, in the client's I/O thread when the
delivery callback fires. Our \kafka{} producer's \texttt{inline} mode, which requires one request
outstanding, instead timestamps on the calling thread once the publish future resolves.

The consumer reads $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ when it holds the record, and $t_{\mathrm{out}}$ once it
has handled it. Our \kafka{} consumer parses the payload inside \texttt{poll()}, before
$t_{\mathrm{recv}}$, and our \redis{} consumer parses it between the two. The processing-time
latency $t_{\mathrm{out}} - t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ is therefore $\spanKafkaHandlingNs$~ns under
\kafka{} and $\spanRedisHandlingNs$~ns under \redis{}, medians of run medians over $\spanRuns$
runs, and negative on none of the $\spanEvents$ messages. The parse thus falls inside $D$ for \kafka{} only, and \redis{}'s median
end-to-end latency of $\spanHandlingDeliveryUs\,\mu$s leaves out a parse $\spanHandlingSharePct\%$
as long. A longer $D$ puts fewer values of $S$ below zero, and \kafka{} already has the higher
rate, so including that parse would widen the gap between the brokers in
Table~\ref{P-tab:spans}.

Table~\ref{P-tab:spans} counts the values below zero in each span over every cloud run that
produced matched records, rejected runs included, because a rejected run shows which span falls
below zero as well as a clean one does. It leaves out the messages a consumer received late,
while it was still reading earlier runs' messages (\ref{s:consumerfaults}). Only $S$, the one span whose two timestamps have no causal
order (Section~\ref{P-sec:proxy}), ever does.

By condition the rate varies widely: over the $\spanConditions$ conditions behind the table, each
pooling its runs of both verdicts, its median is $\spanCondRateMedianPct\%$ (quartiles
$\spanCondRateLoPct$--$\spanCondRateHiPct\%$), $\spanKafkaCondRateMedianPct\%$ on \kafka{}
($\spanKafkaCondRateLoPct$--$\spanKafkaCondRateHiPct\%$) and $\spanRedisCondRateMedianPct\%$ on
\redis{} ($\spanRedisCondRateLoPct$--$\spanRedisCondRateHiPct\%$).

\subsection{S1.5. Two faults of the cloud testbed, and the messages left out}
\label{s:consumerfaults}

Two faults of the cloud testbed's consumers came to light after its runs, when the testbed was
gone and no run could be repeated. One lost messages. The other delayed them, and it decides which
messages every number from that testbed counts.

\paragraph{The consumer stopped during long pauses} A consumer stops after $15$~s without a
message. The producer replays a match in real time, and many matches pause for longer.
In \idleRuns{} runs the consumer stopped during such a pause, and the producer published the rest
of the match to a broker that acknowledged it and that no one read: \idleMessages{} of the
\idlePublished{} messages published, each among its run's last. A message no one received has no
end-to-end latency, so no number counts these.

\paragraph{The consumer first read earlier runs' messages} The runs reused topic and stream names
from one campaign to the next, and nothing cleared them. The \redis{} trial deleted its stream
with a command-line client the driver did not have, and discarded the error; the \kafka{} trial
cleared nothing. A run's consumer reads its topic or stream from the beginning and discards other
runs' messages. So it first read every earlier campaign's, a median of \backlogForeignMedian{}
messages on \redis{}, whose consumer logs each read, and meanwhile the run's own first messages
waited in the broker. A message that waited there was received long after its acknowledgment. It
cannot put $S$ below zero, so it dilutes the rate.

\paragraph{The rule} A message is late when its delivery took more than a second longer than the
run's fastest. The consumer caught up when it received the last such message. A message published
before then that arrived within a second of that instant came in the same burst, so it is late
too, although it waited less. Every message of a run is late when even its fastest delivery took
over a second, because its consumer never caught up. The second is a wide margin. A run's fastest
delivery took \backlogFastestMedianMs~ms at the median and under \backlogFastestHiMs~ms in
$99\%$ of runs, and over a second only in the \backlogNever{} runs whose consumer never caught up.

\paragraph{How the rule was checked} None of the \backlogLate{} messages it finds has a value of
$S$ below zero. In every one of the \backlogRuns{} runs that have any, the run's first published
message is one of them, so each backlog began with its run. Every \redis{} run with a late message
whose consumer logged its reads had read other runs' messages first. The late messages are
\backlogKafkaLatePct\% of \kafka{}'s matched messages and \backlogRedisLatePct\% of \redis{}'s.

\paragraph{What leaving them out changes} Every analysis of the cloud testbed leaves the late
messages out (\texttt{scripts/stale\_backlog.py}). The corpus of Table~\ref{P-tab:spans} falls
from \backlogJoined{} matched messages to \spanEvents{}, and its negative-span rate rises from
\backlogRateBeforePct\% to \spanNegAckPct\%. The campaigns whose consumers read the longest
backlogs move most. The padding repeat E-A10b had sat below E-A10 and now agrees with it at every
padding (Table~\ref{stab:payload}). The traced campaigns' rates rise to their untraced twin's,
which removes a finding: the tracer had seemed to lower \kafka{}'s rate at normal priority, and it
does not (\ref{s:tracer}). The cells of a table now hold different numbers of messages, and each
caption gives the range.

\paragraph{Shorter waits} A backlog the consumer read in under a second escapes the rule, which
needs a wait of a second to see one. Leaving out also each run's leading messages whose $S$
exceeds the run's median by more than $300$~ms, with those published before the last of them
arrived, takes out \backlogWideExtra{} more messages, none below zero. The corpus rate then reads
\backlogWideRatePct\%, so the margin's choice moves it by hundredths of a point.

\paragraph{What cannot be recomputed} The sign check's counts in S2 come from runs that are no
longer in the archive, and were made with the late messages in. A late message can only dilute its
run's values below zero, so the check's rejections are a lower bound. The registered campaign
deleted its streams and topics before each run. By the same rule \backlogAzureLatePct\% of its
messages are late, and only \backlogAzureStartRuns{} of its \backlogAzureRuns{} runs began with
a wait, so its readings stand as judged (\ref{s:law}).


\section{S2. The sign check, and the runs it covers}
\label{s:signcheck}

The rule is that of Section~\ref{P-sec:gate}, applied to every run. A run is rejected if more than
$1\%$ of its messages carry a negative value in $S$, in the publish delay $t_{\mathrm{pub}} -
t_{\mathrm{sched}}$ or in the processing-time latency $t_{\mathrm{out}} - t_{\mathrm{recv}}$, or if
the median of any of the three is negative. A condition is usable only if all of its runs
survive. The one-percent line is a convention, and we fixed the rule after earlier campaigns had
run, which is weaker than registration.

Over the $\auditRunsCloud$ cloud runs behind our results, the check rejects $\auditRejectedCloud$
($\auditPctCloud\%$), and $\auditUsableConditionsCloud$ of their $\auditConditionsCloud$
conditions keep every run. Each build recomputes these counts from the verdicts the check
committed, condition by condition, in \brk{docs/results/integrity_by_condition.csv}
(\brk{scripts/audit_ledger.py}); the runs themselves are no longer in the archive. The check was
made with the late messages of \ref{s:consumerfaults} in, so leaving those messages out could only
add rejections. That corpus is not the one of Table~\ref{P-tab:spans}, which counts
every cloud run that produced matched records, rejected or not, $\spanRuns$ of them. Of those,
$\spanRunsOverOnePct$ exceed the one-percent line on $S$.

\subsection{S2.1. The groups of runs behind the paper's Section VI}
\label{s:groups}

$\recoveryErrKafka\%$ over \kafka{}'s $\recoveryKafkaN$ groups, with an upper quartile of

\subsection{S3.1. Real-time priority cuts the rate in every matched pair}
\label{s:priority}

\subsection{S3.2. One utilization, placed two ways, gives two rates}
\label{s:geometry}

A concentrated load keeps $k$ of the $C=\testbedCpus$ cores fully busy and leaves the other $C-k$
free, while a spread load keeps every core busy part of the time and leaves none free. If values
of $S$ below zero come from a thread that cannot find a free core, the spread load should produce
more of them at equal utilization, and on \kafka{} it does (Table~\ref{stab:geometry}, \kafka{}'s
runs).

At $k=6$ both placements sit at $\rho=\mechGeomRho$, identical to four decimals, on one machine in
one hour, and their rates differ $\GeomOrigFactor$-fold ($z=\GeomOrigZ$ if messages were
independent; bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\placeKafkaOrigCI$). No function of $\rho$
can do that, since a function returns one value for one input. The replication E-A6b reproduces
the cell at the same $\rho$ and a factor of $\GeomReplFactor$ [bootstrap over runs, $95\%$:
$\placeKafkaReplCI$]. At $k=5$ both campaigns keep the direction once whole runs are resampled
[bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\geomFiveOrigRunCI$ in the original, $\geomFiveReplRunCI$ in the
replication]. At $k=7$ the difference is small, since one free core in eight is nearly as useless
as none, and neither run resolves it once whole runs are resampled: both intervals take in no
difference at all [bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\geomSevenOrigRunCI$ in the original,
$\geomSevenReplRunCI$ around the replication's $\geomSevenReplFactor$]. Multi-server queueing already expects placement to matter at fixed
$\rho$, so the contrast refutes not queueing itself but the single-parameter form, in which $\rho$
alone fixes the rate.

On \redis{}, in the same runs, the order reverses. At $k=6$ the spread load's rate is
$\placeRedisOrig$ times the concentrated load's [bootstrap over runs, $95\%$: $\placeRedisOrigCI$],
and $\placeRedisRepl$ times in the replication [bootstrap, $95\%$: $\placeRedisReplCI$]. One utilization still gives two rates, which is what refutes a
rate set by utilization alone, but the explanation above fits \kafka{} only. We have not tested
why \redis{}'s clients fare worse with two cores free. One candidate is its producer's pool of
worker threads (Table~\ref{stab:settings}), which may need more cores than a concentrated load
leaves.

\subsection{S3.4. Load mostly changes how often the thread waits}
\label{s:twostate}

A thread that reads the clock is either running, and reads it late by a narrow jitter, or waiting
for a core. From an idle host to the heaviest load before its processors are fully busy, E-A3's
negative-span rate grows $\invGrowth$-fold while the jitter's width grows only $\coreGrowth$-fold
(Table~\ref{stab:mixture}). Load therefore moves mostly the waiting probability $p$ of
Equation~\ref{P-eq:occupancy}, the chance the thread is waiting, and little the running
state's jitter. A scale family, in which load only widens one distribution, has one parameter and
cannot move the two this differently. The tails agree. If load moves only $p$, the tail
curves of two loads should shift vertically and stay parallel on a log scale, where a scale family
would rescale them horizontally. Over tail estimates from at least $20$ events, the log ratio
of two conditions' tail masses varies over thresholds by a median factor of $1.34$ across
conditions, while the scale-family collapse fails by $23\times$. That test was exploratory; the
registered test of $p$ is the priority manipulation of \ref{s:priority}.

A wait lasts an interval, so the messages inside one are read late together. A Wald--Wolfowitz
runs test on each run's sequence of signs of $S$ finds strong clustering at every load, even with
no background load. Coarse kernel timestamps would corrupt messages independently and give
$z\approx0$.

Equation~\ref{P-eq:occupancy} describes how the rate moves between conditions, not single
messages. Read with the measured $D$ in place of the margin, it would have the rate fall as $D$
grows inside a condition, and it does not. We counted each condition's messages in bins of $D$,
$\rbdBinsPerDecade$ to a decade with at least $\rbdMinCount$ messages a bin, a count fixed before
any bin was read. The rate rises from one bin to a longer one, with disjoint Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}, in $\rbdUp$ of $\rbdTestable$ testable conditions, and in
$\rbdUpLo$ to $\rbdUpHi$ over four, eight and twelve bins a decade. The rises sit in the
runs the sign check rejects ($\rbdFailUp$ of $\rbdFailTestable$ conditions), not in those it
passes ($\rbdPassUp$ of $\rbdPassTestable$), whose rates are low. Within a run $D$ and $A$
correlate, with a median coefficient of $\spanRhoWithinMedian$ over our groups, so a long delivery
comes with a long publish latency, and a message chosen for its long $D$ likely had its
acknowledgment read late too. These runs could not read the margin message by message; freeze 41 later did, as $S_k$, and
the rate did not fall from one fifth of it to the next (\ref{s:orderscope}). Between
conditions, where padding lengthens the margin at fixed load, the rate falls as predicted
(\ref{s:payload}).

\subsection{S3.5. A kernel trace agrees with the rate, unfitted, and changes it}

margin, the median of $S$ with no load, and the margin differs by broker: in E-A3's idle runs it is

\subsection{S3.6. Rival explanations: excluded, bounded, or real on Redis}
\label{s:rivals}

Five rivals fall to the design and the manipulations. One clock excludes clock skew between
hosts, the cause a concurrent study assigns to such values (postmortem, S33). One utilization at rates twofold apart excludes utilization (\ref{s:geometry}). Real-time priority
excludes the timer noise that reaches a thread whatever its scheduling class, such as interrupts
(\ref{s:priority}), and a longer path that lowers the rate excludes stress on the host
(\ref{s:payload}). Coarse kernel timestamps fall to the runs test of \ref{s:twostate}.

One kind of timer delay does depend on the class, so priority alone cannot exclude it. A thread
that sleeps with a timeout may wake later than the timeout by its \emph{timer slack}, the margin
the kernel allows itself in order to batch wakeups. In Linux~6.8 a real-time task gets no slack in
\texttt{select}, \texttt{poll}, \texttt{epoll\_wait} and \texttt{nanosleep}, while a normal task
gets at least $50\,\mu$s by default. But slack can delay only a wakeup that a timer makes. A thread
that reads the clock after arriving data is woken by the data, not by a timer, so slack cannot
delay it.
Five more rivals need an argument each. Two, the hypervisor and the interpreter lock, are
bounded rather than excluded, and a third, the broker's reply order, is excluded on \kafka{} and
real on \redis{}.

\paragraph{The second thread} \kafka{}'s client reads the clock for the acknowledgment on its I/O
thread, and \redis{}'s on the calling thread (Figure~\ref{P-fig:model};
\ref{s:setup-timestamps}). The hand-off is not the mechanism. \redis{}, which
has no second thread, has values of $S$ below zero on $\spanRedisNegAckPct\%$ of its messages
against \kafka{}'s $\spanKafkaNegAckPct\%$, and on more of its runs
($\spanRedisRunsAckInvertsPct\%$ against $\spanKafkaRunsAckInvertsPct\%$). Its calling thread,
returning from a blocking socket read, must also be woken and given a core before it reads the
clock.

\paragraph{The hypervisor} Both testbeds are virtual machines, so steal time, the time a virtual
processor is ready to run but waits while the hypervisor runs another machine, is bounded, not
excluded. Two observations disagree with steal time as the cause. Real-time priority is a setting
inside the virtual machine, which steal time would not yield to, yet it cuts the rate
$\rtFactorLow$- to $\rtFactorHigh$-fold on \kafka{} and $\rtRedisFactorLow$- to
$\rtRedisFactorHigh$-fold on \redis{} (\ref{s:priority}). And the upper mode of the traced waits shares its
bucket with the base slice that the virtual machine's own kernel derives (\ref{s:timescale}), not with any
quantity the hypervisor exposes. The Oracle runs did not record steal time. The registered
campaign recorded it from \texttt{/proc/stat} in $\stealRunsRead$ runs and found \stealFinding{},
but we did not establish whether the Azure hypervisor reports steal time to its machines, so that
reading does not exclude it either.

\paragraph{The interpreter lock} The threads that read the clock are CPython, which releases the
lock around blocking I/O and retakes it on return, so a timestamp waits for a core and then for
the lock. The kernel trace cannot see the second wait, and neither priority nor the size of the
waits tells the two apart. The direction of the trace's error bounds that second wait. A wait in
series with the run queue would make the trace underestimate the rate, but its ratios to the rate
of the runs it traced, \tracedPooledRatios{}, lie on both sides of one. The registered campaign
also found values of $S$ below zero in \kafka{}'s own Java client, which has no interpreter lock,
at a third to a quarter of the Python client's rate (\ref{s:law-verdicts}). So the effect needs no
interpreter lock. The Java result does not show that the lock adds nothing to the Python client's
rate. Outside Python too, another concurrent study splits the latency of a Linux host's network
stack into parts timed by the kernel. It finds that the main part of the tail is the wait from a
thread's wakeup by arriving data to its running~\cite{zuo2026hoststack}.

\paragraph{The clock} Clocks that disagree across virtual CPUs are excluded twice on the Oracle
machines, whose hypervisor is KVM~\cite{kvmclock}. First, the platform probe's smallest observed
increment of \texttt{clock\_gettime(CLOCK\_MONOTONIC)} was $\clockIncrementNs$~ns, an upper bound on the cost of
one read. That excludes \texttt{acpi\_pm} by arithmetic, since its tick is $\clockPmTickNs$~ns,
and \texttt{hpet} by its published read cost near $1.2\,\mu$s. What remains, \clockAdmitted{},
carries the coherence guarantee. A \texttt{kvm-clock} that fast is on the vDSO path, which
\texttt{kvmclock.c} enables only when the hypervisor sets \texttt{PVCLOCK\_TSC\_STABLE\_BIT}, the
flag that guarantees monotonic time across CPUs. A KVM guest selects \texttt{tsc} only when the kernel finds it
constant, non-stop and stable, and abandons it if it later falls out of step between CPUs. Which
of the two ran is unrecorded, and the published read costs vary about twofold, against an
order-of-magnitude margin. Second, an offset between clocks large enough to produce the deepest
value of $S$, $\spanDeepestInversionMs$~ms, would have driven $D$ far below zero. The smallest $D$
observed, $\spanSendFloorUs\,\mu$s, bounds any such offset $\spanOffsetMargin$-fold below what that
value needs.

Two observations do the same work on Azure, where the registered campaign and the runs of
\ref{s:recvwait} to \ref{s:sofix} ran, under the Hyper-V hypervisor. First, an x86 guest there
reads the kernel's clocksource built on the processor's timestamp counter (TSC). Where Hyper-V
cannot keep that counter's frequency across a move to another host, the guest reads Hyper-V's own
clock instead. It computes that clock from the TSC with a scale and an offset that the hypervisor
keeps in a page of memory shared with the guest
(\texttt{hyperv\_clocksource\_tsc\_page})~\cite{linux_hyperv_clocks}. Either way every CPU turns
the counter into time with the same scale and offset, and Hyper-V gives the guest the counter's
frequency. An Arm guest reads the system counter that Hyper-V virtualizes for it, through the
same driver it would use without a hypervisor. Second, no message of the registered campaign
arrived before it was published (\ref{s:law-verdicts}), which bounds any offset between the CPUs'
clocks by the shortest end-to-end latency.

\paragraph{The broker's reply order} Both readings follow one write, but the broker then answers
twice, with the acknowledgment to the producer and the message to the consumer. A broker that
delivered the message first would put $S$ below zero with no wait at all. Freeze 41 timed both
arrivals by the kernel (\ref{s:orderscope}). On \kafka{}, which returns the acknowledgment before it
answers the consumer's waiting request, the acknowledgment arrived first in all but a few percent of
messages, and $\osKafkaLatePct\%$ of its values below zero under load had it first: there the rival
is excluded. \redis{} writes the delivery to a waiting consumer before it replies to the producer,
and in $\osRedisFirstRuns$ of its $\osRedisRuns$ runs the delivery came first for most messages;
$\osRedisOrderLoPct$--$\osRedisOrderHiPct\%$ of \redis{}'s values below zero had it first. On
\redis{} the rival is real, and it is the broker's floor at real-time priority. Some brokers
deliver before they confirm by design: a persistent RabbitMQ message ``can be delivered to the
consumer before it's been written to disk''~\cite{macmullen2014rabbitmq}.

\subsection{S3.7. The waits' upper mode survives coarsening, and its bucket holds the derived slice}
\label{s:timescale}

The traced waits have $\stallModeCount$ local maxima (Figure~\ref{sfig:spectrum}), and the upper
one survives a change of bucket edges. How many maxima a histogram shows depends on its bin edges
as well as its counts, and these edges came from \texttt{bpftrace}. The per-event waits were not
retained, so coarsening is the only test. Merging adjacent buckets doubles the bin
width, and starting the merge at the first bucket or the second places the edges differently. The spectrum has $\stallModeCount$ maxima on the tool's own $\stallModeBuckets$ buckets,
$\stallModeCount$ again under both phasings at $\stallCoarsenFactor$ times the width, and fewer at
$\stallLostFactor$ times, so the count holds to within a factor of $\stallCoarsenFactor$ in bin
width.

Buckets that double in width favor the upper ones, so part of the upper mode is the buckets'
doing. Waits spread evenly up to the slice and then up to a tick, as
Equation~\ref{seq:restofslice} has them, would make the bucket that holds the slice
$\tracedModelRatio$ times the bucket below it. The traced bucket is $\tracedModeRatio$ times it,
so the waits gather at the slice more than an even spread would put them there. We have not
explained the middle mode, at $\tracedModeLoB$--$\tracedModeHiB\,\mu$s.

Above the upper mode the counts collapse. They fall $\tracedModeFallA$-, $\tracedModeFallB$- and
$\tracedModeFallC$-fold over the next \tracedModeFallOctaves{} octaves and then
$\tracedLastBucketFall$-fold, each octave a doubling of the wait, whereas a power law would fall by
the same factor in every octave. Power laws fitted below the mode and above it both fail a
bootstrap goodness-of-fit ($p\tracedGofP$ and $p\tracedTailGofP$, over $\tracedGofBoot$ replicates
each).

The slice is derived from the kernel's rule and the campaigns' own data. Each placement condition
loads $k$ cores and records the achieved utilization, so $k/\rho$ recovers $\testbedCpus$ cores
six times over, with a spread of $\cpuEstimateSpread$. Equation~\ref{P-eq:slice} then gives
$\sliceFactor\times\sliceConstantMs\,\mathrm{ms}=\baseSliceMs$~ms. Upstream Linux 6.8 used
$s_0 = 0.75$~ms; the change to $\sliceConstantMs$~ms~\cite{zhou2025slice}, first released in
6.15, reached this kernel through Ubuntu's stable updates (linux-oracle-6.8 6.8.0-1043, January
2026, with no revert before the 6.8.0-1057 every cloud run recorded), and the registered
campaign's Azure kernels, from the same updates, read back the same constant. Either value puts
the slice in the $\tracedModeLo$--$\tracedModeHi$~ms bucket. The build sets
\texttt{CONFIG\_HZ}${}=\kernelHz$, a $\tickMs$~ms tick. No scheduler tunable is written anywhere in
the archived campaigns, so the derived value is the one that applied. That is strong evidence,
short of proof, because the slice was not read off the hosts.

A woken thread that the scheduler does not let run at once waits for the running thread to finish
its slice, and since a task notices its deadline only at the next tick, the waits end in a band a
tick wide. The registered campaign timed the wait itself and found it to be the rest of a slice
(\ref{s:law-after}). One rule of the scheduler would explain the wait, \emph{run-to-parity}, which
Linux turns on by default (\texttt{RUN\_TO\_PARITY}): under it, \texttt{pick\_eevdf} in
\brk{kernel/sched/fair.c} keeps the running task until it either ``becomes non-eligible'', having
had more than its share of the core, or ``gets a new slice'', in the words of its comment in
Linux~6.8. Freeze 41 turned the rule off (\ref{s:orderscope}), and neither the rate of $S$ below
zero nor the acknowledging thread's 99th-percentile delay moved on either broker, against a
registered cut to two-thirds, so the rule alone is not what holds a woken thread off the core. The
rule came with commit 63304558ba5d, ``sched/eevdf: Curb wakeup-preemption'', first released in
Linux~6.6. From Linux~6.17, commit 052c3d87c82e, ``sched/fair: Limit run to parity to the min
slice of enqueued entities'', limits it to the shortest slice queued.

\subsection{S3.8. Timing from the publish call keeps the receiving thread's wait, under a millisecond on the mean}
\label{s:recvwait}

A latency timed from the publish call carries the receiving thread's wait for a core. By
Equation~\ref{P-eq:model}, $D = S + A$ for every message, so the acknowledging thread's wait
cancels: it lengthens $A$ by as much as it shortens $S$. The receiving thread's wait does not
cancel. The consumer reads its clock only after its thread has been woken and given a core, so
every $D$ holds that wait, which is never negative and which the sign check of
Section~\ref{P-sec:gate} cannot see. After the registered campaign we measured it three ways in the
same runs, by a plan and four predictions written before any run.

The runs used the registered campaign's first x86 pair (\ref{s:law-design}), both brokers, $\recvLowLoadPct$ and
$\recvHighLoadPct\%$ load and the base slice at $3$~ms, with no delay added to the network. Eight
setups, each broker at each load with the consumer at normal or at real-time priority, ran in six
shuffled rounds. Three runs added before anything was read gave every setup at least three traced
runs, and all $\recvWaitRuns$ runs passed the integrity rule. Method~1 runs the consumer alone in
the real-time class at priority $80$ and measures how much that lowers $D$ at unchanged load.
Method~2 traces the scheduler in a random half of the runs and measures the receiving thread's own
wait for a core before each timestamp, as \ref{s:tracer} did for the acknowledging thread. Method~3 captures
the receiver's packets in every run with the kernel's nanosecond timestamps, and times each message
from the packet that carries it to the consumer's timestamp. That delay includes the wake-up, the
wait and the client's own work.

\subsection{S3.9. Timed between the kernel's timestamps, a message's latency held still across background load and priority}
\label{s:fullfix}

Every remedy of Section~\ref{P-sec:practice} leaves some wait inside the latency. Timing from the
publish call cancels the wait of the thread that reads the acknowledgment but keeps the receiving
thread's (\ref{s:recvwait}), and real-time priority shortens both waits without ending them. A fix
that keeps every client thread's wait out of the latency has to take both of a message's
timestamps where no such thread reads the clock. The kernel does that: it timestamps each packet
as it leaves or arrives, without waiting for any program. We tested that fix by a plan and five
predictions fixed before any of its runs, including a short pilot that checked the captures.

The runs used the same first x86 pair (\ref{s:law-design}) and both brokers, with no background
load on the client machine or with $\fullFixLoadPct\%$, the base slice at $3$~ms and no delay
added to the network. The producer and the consumer ran together at normal or at real-time
priority. Eight setups ran in six shuffled rounds. All $\fullFixRuns$ runs passed the integrity
rule, and no message arrived more than $150$~ms late. Each run captured the producer's packets as
they left through the client machine's kernel and the receiver's packets as they arrived, with the
kernel's nanosecond timestamps, on the clock the clients read. Each message then carries four
times: the publish call, $t_{\mathrm{pub}}$; the kernel's timestamp of the packet that carried it
out, $t_{\mathrm{tx}}$; the kernel's timestamp of the packet that brought it in,
$t_{\mathrm{rx}}$; and the consumer's timestamp, $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$. The \emph{kernel-timed
latency} $W = t_{\mathrm{rx}} - t_{\mathrm{tx}}$ holds the network and the broker and none of the
client threads' waits. The client machine's time before and after it, $H_{\mathrm{out}} =
t_{\mathrm{tx}} - t_{\mathrm{pub}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{in}} = t_{\mathrm{recv}} - t_{\mathrm{rx}}$,
holds the clients' waits and work, so that $D = H_{\mathrm{out}} + W + H_{\mathrm{in}}$ for every
message.

written by the calling thread, moved $\fullFixSendMoveRedisUs\,\mu$s, within the
$\fullFixSendMoveBarUs\,\mu$s allowed. $H_{\mathrm{in}}$ rose on both brokers, as
\ref{s:recvwait} found, and real-time priority undid most of both rises.

The network card's own receive timestamps were captured beside the kernel's and put on the
system's clock by sampling the card's clock twice a second. They were reported, not tested against
a prediction. Their gap to the kernel's is the part of the client machine's delay that the
kernel's timestamp keeps. The kernel timestamped each packet a median of
$\fullFixKernelDelayLoUs$--$\fullFixKernelDelayHiUs\,\mu$s after the card did, longest with no
background load, as the plan expected of processors that must wake to take the interrupt. The plan
words this delay as the card's arrival less the kernel's. The delay reported is the kernel's less
the card's.

The kernel's receive timestamp can itself be taken late. The kernel takes it as the driver hands a
packet to the network stack~\cite{linux_timestamping}, normally in its \emph{softirq} processing,
the deferred part of the work a packet's interrupt starts. Under heavy load the kernel moves that
processing to its \texttt{ksoftirqd} threads~\cite{linux_ksoftirqd}. With threaded NAPI, a setting
that runs a driver's receive polling in kernel threads of its own, the processing runs in such
threads from the start. Either way the kernel's timestamp is then taken by a thread that itself
waits for a core, and that wait falls inside $W$. The card's own receive timestamp is taken before
any such thread runs, as measurements that split a host's time from the wire's
use~\cite{zilberman2017time}. In these runs, at 50 messages a second on the first x86 pair, $W$
held still under load all the same; on the second pair \redis{}'s rose by a quarter
(\ref{s:orderscope}).

The largest move of the median of $W$, $\fullFixMedianMoveUs\,\mu$s on \redis{} at
real-time priority, was a fall under load. Of that fall, $\fullFixCardMoveUs\,\mu$s lay between
the kernel's departure timestamp and the card's arrival timestamp. We have not tested why. A tool
can read the kernel's timestamps on its own sockets, without a capture, and \ref{s:sofix} tests
whether they agree with the captures. The plan, the register of every run and the reading are in
the repository under \brk{docs/results/full_fix}, read by \brk{scripts/full_fix.py}.

\subsection{S3.10. Read on the clients' own sockets, the kernel's timestamps agreed with the captures to within microseconds}
\label{s:sofix}

A benchmark tool would not run a packet capture. It would ask the kernel for its timestamps on its
own sockets, through the \texttt{SO\_TIMESTAMPING} \emph{socket option}, a setting a program asks
the kernel for on one of its sockets. We tested that form by a second plan and five predictions
fixed before its runs, with \ref{s:fullfix}'s captures, taken in the same runs, as the reference.

The runs repeated the design of \ref{s:fullfix}, with the same pair and both brokers, no
background load on the client machine or $\fullFixLoadPct\%$, and normal or real-time priority for
the producer and the consumer together. Eight setups ran in six shuffled rounds, and all
$\soFixRuns$ runs passed the integrity rule. A small module replaced the socket class in the
producer's and the consumer's processes before their client libraries opened a connection. On
each connection to the broker it turned the option on and read two timestamps for each message.
The first, $t_{\mathrm{tx,so}}$, is the kernel's timestamp of the producer's write that carried
the message out. The kernel returns it on the socket's \emph{error queue}, where it leaves
transmit timestamps for the program to collect, with the byte offset of the write's last byte. The
second, $t_{\mathrm{rx,so}}$, is the kernel's timestamp of the packet that brought the message in,
returned with the consumer's read. A driver takes the \emph{software transmit timestamp} as it
passes a packet to the network card. On these machines none came back, and the reason lies in the
driver. In Linux~6.8, \texttt{hv\_netvsc}, Hyper-V's network driver, hands each packet to the
\emph{accelerated-networking device}, a function of the physical card that Azure gives the machine
directly (\texttt{netvsc\_vf\_xmit}, \brk{drivers/net/hyperv/netvsc_drv.c} line 495). It then
returns without reaching its own call to \texttt{skb\_tx\_timestamp()} (line 662). The drivers of
that device take no such timestamp either. The transmit path of mlx5, the Mellanox driver, has no
\texttt{skb\_tx\_timestamp()} before Linux~6.16, and that of MANA, the Microsoft Azure Network
Adapter's driver, has none in Linux~6.8. So, as the plan allowed, the transmit timestamp was the
one the kernel documentation names \texttt{SOF\_TIMESTAMPING\_TX\_SCHED}, taken before the
\emph{packet scheduler}, the queueing discipline in front of the driver~\cite{linux_timestamping}.
The two socket timestamps give the kernel-timed latency $W_{\mathrm{so}} = t_{\mathrm{rx,so}} -
t_{\mathrm{tx,so}}$, to set beside the captures' $W$. Any time a packet waits in the packet
scheduler's queue therefore falls inside $W_{\mathrm{so}}$.

For a tool's author, the module's choices are these. It asks for the transmit timestamp taken
before the packet scheduler (\texttt{SOF\_TIMESTAMPING\_TX\_SCHED}, or \texttt{TX\_SOFTWARE} where
the driver gives one), the software receive timestamp (\texttt{RX\_SOFTWARE} with
\texttt{SOFTWARE}), an identifier for each write (\texttt{OPT\_ID}), and the timestamp alone on the
error queue, without a copy of the packet (\texttt{OPT\_TSONLY})~\cite{linux_timestamping}. It
turns the option on at a connection's first write or read once the connection is up, so that the
identifier counts bytes from the first byte written. On these machines the transmit timestamp came
back twice for every write, and the module keeps the first. The kernel documentation says a packet
yields one at each packet scheduler it passes, and \texttt{hv\_netvsc} passes each packet through a
second, the accelerated-networking device's own (\brk{netvsc_drv.c} line 459). The module reads the
error queue only when \texttt{poll()} reports something waiting there. On a socket with a timeout,
as \texttt{kafka-python} sets one while it writes, Python waits for the socket to become readable
before any read, so the module held each request past the client's own timeout and the pilot's
first three \kafka{} attempts failed. Linux overwrites a read's receive timestamp with each packet
the read consumes (\texttt{tcp\_update\_recv\_tstamps}, called from the read loop in
\brk{net/ipv4/tcp.c}), so a read returns the timestamp of its last packet.

Three more points matter to a tool's author. First, a driver may report timestamps that it does
not give. \texttt{hv\_netvsc} reports software timestamps to \texttt{ethtool}
(\texttt{ethtool\_op\_get\_ts\_info}, \brk{netvsc_drv.c} line 2012), yet no software transmit
timestamp came back, so a tool should check which timestamps actually arrive rather than trust
\texttt{ethtool -T}. Second, on a stream socket a tool should set
\texttt{SOF\_TIMESTAMPING\_OPT\_ID\_TCP} beside \texttt{OPT\_ID}, as the kernel documentation
advises, because without it the identifier's starting point is ``not entirely trivial'': it can be
off by the bytes still queued when the option is turned on~\cite{linux_timestamping}. The module
does not set this flag. It needs no correction, because it turns timestamping on before the
connection's first write. Third, writes that the kernel merges into one packet share one transmit
timestamp, and the byte offsets show where that happened.

\section{S4. The positivity filter and the retention law in full}
\label{s:failure2}

The benchmark's printed latency describes only the samples its publish schedule lets through the
positivity filter, and each link in that claim can be checked against a source line, a counter or a
registered prediction. Every benchmark run below is in embedded mode, with the whole benchmark in
one JVM.

\subsection{S4.1. Two source lines make the filter, and counting what they drop changes no printed number}
\label{s:filterpatch}

What the positivity filter drops can be counted without changing the filter. In \brk{LocalWorker.java}:294
the benchmark computes its end-to-end latency as
\begin{quote}
\texttt{MILLISECONDS.toMicros(now - publishTimestamp)}
\end{quote}
from two millisecond timestamps, so the value, though in microseconds, is a whole number of milliseconds. In
\brk{WorkerStats.java}:95 the positivity filter,
\begin{quote}
\texttt{if (endToEndLatencyMicros > 0)}
\end{quote}
decides whether that value reaches the histogram. Each message is counted as received; what the
filter drops is not.

We add counters in an \texttt{else} branch of that condition and change nothing else: not the
latency computation, not the filter, not any reported statistic. The benchmark's output is
therefore exactly what it would be unpatched, and its printed summary can be set beside
the retention behind it (Fig.~\ref{P-fig:deletion}b of the paper). The four counters, for zero, negative,
most negative and kept samples, separate sign. A value below zero and an end-to-end latency
shorter than a millisecond both fail a \texttt{> 0} test, and one unsigned total cannot tell them
apart (\ref{s:minus1000}). The patch is \brk{external/omb/workerstats_discard_counter.patch}.

The counts are exact. The counters are shared across the JVM and their totals printed once at
shutdown. A run that fails discards nothing, and its zero count would look measured, so a validity
gate writes it as invalid, with its reason and no count. Unlike the benchmark's histograms, our
counters include the one-minute warmup, yet a matched sweep with the warmup disabled leaves
retention unchanged. The campaign totals $\ombRuns$ instrumented runs, swept one axis at a time
with background load, message size, producer rate and warmup otherwise fixed.

\subsection{S4.2. The phase of the publish schedule sets retention, as a 1970 counter note already showed}
\label{s:retentionlaw}

Where each publish instant falls within a step of the clock decides whether its sample survives. An
end-to-end latency shorter than the step $\tau$ survives only if it crosses a grid instant, where
the timestamp changes value. Otherwise both timestamps round down, or floor, to the same value, and
the positivity filter deletes the zero difference (Fig.~\ref{P-fig:deletion}a of the
paper). When phases are
uniform, retention is $\theta = T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$, which is Equation~\ref{P-eq:retention} of
the paper.

A paced producer does not draw its phases uniformly. If its publish interval $T_{\mathrm{pub}}$
satisfies $T_{\mathrm{pub}}/\tau = a/q$ in lowest terms, it visits only $q$ distinct phases, so
its retention is a whole count out of $q$. Call the retentions $0, 1/q, \ldots, 1$ the vertices of
the configuration's $q$-lattice, and the interval between neighboring vertices, $100/q$ percentage
points wide, a lattice step. At an incommensurate rate, whose ratio has no small denominator, the
vertices are dense and retention is $\theta$. Elsewhere, replicates of one configuration land on
the vertices that bracket $\theta$, and their spread obeys
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:spread}
\text{spread} \;\longrightarrow\;
\begin{cases}
100/q & \text{when } \theta \text{ sits mid-step,}\\[2pt]
0 & \text{when } \theta \text{ sits on a vertex.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The step width is thus an upper bound on the spread, reached only when replicates realize both
bracketing vertices. Retention at the incommensurate rates puts $T_{\mathrm{true}}$ at
$\spreadIncommensurateTrueMs$~ms, half a millisecond to within replicate noise
(Section~\ref{P-sec:extcomp}). One half is a vertex of every $q$-lattice with even $q$ and of none
with odd $q$, so the spread rule predicts full step width for odd denominators and almost none for
even ones.

Hewlett-Packard's Application Note 162-1~\cite{hp1970tia} carries this arithmetic for a counter
timing an interval. For an interval of $Q + F$ clock periods it gives $\Pr[R = Q{+}1] = F$, which is
Equation~\ref{P-eq:retention}, and the denominator of the ratio of repetition rate to clock rate in
lowest terms, our $q$, fixes how many distinct readings the counter can produce, so averaging $N$
readings improves resolution by at most a factor $1/q$, not $1/\sqrt{N}$. Its cure, phase jitter on
the repetition rate, becomes for a benchmark randomized publish instants (the paper's
Table~\ref{P-tab:checks}).

\subsection{S4.4. Two floored clocks disagree by exactly one millisecond, so the sign of a discard names which kind of timestamp failed}
\label{s:minus1000}

The sign of a discarded sample says which clock failed, and the benchmark's two drivers show
both kinds. The \kafka{} driver's $\ombKafkaRuns$ runs discarded $\ombKafkaDiscarded$ samples,
and not one was negative. The \redis{}-driver replication's $\ombRedisRuns$ runs carry all
$\ombNegatives$ of the campaign's negatives, every one exactly $-1000\,\mu$s.

The drivers explain the difference. The \redis{} driver takes its publish timestamp from
\brk{entry.getID().getTime()} in \brk{RedisBenchmarkConsumer.java}:72, the stream-entry
identifier the \redis{} server assigns on \texttt{XADD}. The \kafka{} driver uses a
producer-set timestamp. So the \redis{} span subtracts a server-side timestamp from a
consumer-side one, across two hosts, both floored to the millisecond, while the
\kafka{} span floors two timestamps whose difference is taken inside one JVM.

Two clocks floored to the same resolution and offset by less than one millisecond can disagree by
one millisecond and by no other amount. The only negative a floored cross-host difference can
produce is therefore $-1000\,\mu$s, and every one of the $\ombRedisNegatives$ negatives is that
value. Clock synchronization was clean at every logged minute, so this failure needs no misbehaving
clock, only two clocks whose readings are floored. A difference taken inside one process has no
second clock to disagree with, which is why the \kafka{} discards hold no negative. The same
positivity filter thus hides a loss of resolution under \kafka{} and a clock failure under
\redis{}, and an unsigned discard counter could not tell them apart.

\subsection{S4.5. An independent harness reproduces the deletion, and the benchmark's distributed mode, which failed on the cloud testbed, ran in the registered campaign}
\label{s:crosshost}

The deletion does not depend on the benchmark's code. We reproduced the positivity filter in a
Python harness that shares no code with the benchmark, with the same timestamp, subtraction and
filter. On one clock it recorded $\harnessOneClockNegatives$ negative differences across
$\harnessOneClockSamples$ samples in $\harnessOneClockRuns$ runs, as a causal chain requires, and
the grid reappeared intact. Across two hosts with disciplined clocks, $\harnessCrossHostRuns$ runs
again recorded $\harnessCrossHostNegatives$ negatives in $\harnessCrossHostSamples$ samples and an
intact grid, but the deleted fraction followed the drift of the clock offset. Of the three rates,
only the \harnessXHostArmClass{} one, $\harnessXHostRate$~msg/s, has no lattice spread wide enough
to hide the drift. Over four replicates there, cross-host retention wandered from
$\harnessXHostRetLo$ to $\harnessXHostRetHi\%$, while on one clock the same configuration's
replicates spanned $\harnessOneClockSpread$ points.

On the cloud testbed the benchmark's own distributed mode, where its subtraction spans two clocks,
could not be measured. It never completed a run in eleven attempts: eight at three
background-load levels, including none, and three more run with full diagnostics. The unloaded
attempts failed like the loaded ones, which refutes our own hypothesis that processor starvation
was the cause, and the three diagnosed attempts failed identically, in the coordinator-to-worker
path before any workload started. The first attempt, made before the validity gate of
\ref{s:filterpatch} existed, wrote a vacuous zero that the artifact keeps marked invalid, and the
gate refused every later attempt with its reason. On that testbed the distributed mode's exposure
to the deletion therefore rested on reading its source. The timestamp is written on the
producer's host and read on the consumer's, and the positivity filter drops every difference at or
below zero.

On the registered campaign's machines the distributed mode ran unmodified in October 2026, in the
campaign's tools block (S5). A coordinator and two workers, one producing and one consuming, ran
on one machine against a \kafka{} broker on the other. The benchmark's median followed a delay
added to the path. We moved the consuming worker's clock back at two offsets of a few
milliseconds, and the filter dropped whatever then computed to zero or below. In three of the four
runs at the smaller offset the median fell to 1~ms, the smallest value a surviving sample can
take. Elsewhere only the slow tail survived, and the median rose to the tail's; in one run nothing
survived to print. The unmodified benchmark counts nothing it drops, so these runs show its filter
at work across two processes but do not measure the deletion, which rests on the embedded mode and
on the harness above.

The \harnessSilentWord{} silent disposals take \harnessDisposalClassesWord{} forms. A filter drops
what its comparison excludes and reports no count of what it removed. The test $>0$ deletes every
sample that computes to exactly zero, while $\geq 0$ keeps those and drops only values below zero.
The benchmark and librdkafka's \texttt{rdkafka\_performance} filter at $>0$.
Apache Pulsar's performance client guards at $\geq 0$, as do RabbitMQ Stream PerfTest and the
readers two other tools ship, so the zeros the benchmark deletes pile up visibly at zero. A
substitution keeps the sample count, so any retention computed downstream reads $100\%$, which
makes it harder to detect than a filter. A library refusal leaves the sample unrecorded because
the tool never checks whether its library rejected it. Truncation is a separate loss: it keeps
every sample but loses precision. The benchmark truncates before it filters, and \kafka{}'s
bundled tools and PerfKit Benchmarker's messaging benchmark only truncate. The notes below explain
the verdicts a reader may question, with the versions and dates of the changes we found; the
postmortem, S25.2, keeps a note on every tool.
\begin{itemize}
\item The OpenMessaging Benchmark appears twice because it treats its two spans differently: it
times its \ombGuardedPath{} latency on a \ombGuardedClock{} clock and filters it, and times its
\ombUnguardedPath{} latency in \ombUnguardedClock{}s and records every sample
(Section~\ref{P-sec:extmethod}). librdkafka's \texttt{rdkafka\_performance} appears twice too: its
consumer keeps its own wall clock less the producer's timestamp only if the difference is above zero
and under five minutes, printing a line for each value it drops but keeping no count, and its
producer records every value.
\item The \texttt{EndToEndLatency} tool bundled with \kafka{} cannot produce a value below zero,
because it uses one thread, one monotonic clock and a synchronous publish call. It loses the
measurement anyway, in two ways. Each sample prints in floating point, but the \texttt{long[]} its
percentiles come from is filled by the integer division \texttt{elapsed / 1000 / 1000}, so on a
path faster than a millisecond its p50, p99 and p999 all read zero. And it polls for a message only
once its publish call has returned the acknowledgment, so a message that reached the consumer first
is timed no earlier than its acknowledgment, a later time standing in for its arrival. Nothing
counts it; the registry classes that as a substitution, and K1 below measured how often. The companion
\texttt{ProducerPerformance} takes its publish-to-acknowledgment difference on
\texttt{System.currentTimeMillis()} and casts it to \texttt{int}.
\item Three verdicts rest on a zero that is not a negative, or on what a project ships beside its
tool. BookKeeper's benchmark keeps a publish latency only if it is above zero, because a zero also
marks an add that never completed; its clock is monotonic, so no negative can arise. Strimzi Canary
bins a negative with its fastest messages, and the script it ships to read its latency log matches
durations with \texttt{[0-9]+}, which cannot read a minus sign. The Yahoo Streaming Benchmark writes
every value, but the notebook it ships keeps only windows whose last write, on the job's clock,
comes at or after the window's end on the generator's clock, on another host. A user reported values such as $-70{,}646$~ms in 2018; the replies put them down to
unsynchronized clocks across worker nodes, and the report is still open.
\item Three tools changed their handling recently. \texttt{emqtt-bench}'s \texttt{> 0} guard
protects a counter and leaves its histogram alone; the guard that once deleted from the histogram
was removed in January 2025. Its printed average counts a value at or below zero as zero. RabbitMQ PerfTest's
\texttt{getDifference} returns the absolute value of the difference, so a negative span enters its
metrics as a positive one, uncounted. From version 2.0.0 of 2017 until version 2.25.0 of June 2026
its metrics recorded only values above zero, deleting every trip shorter than a millisecond in its
millisecond mode. RabbitMQ
Stream PerfTest has dropped values below zero since 2 June 2026, release 1.7.0, its fix for a
user's report of a negative latency during a rolling restart; before that, the library it records
into refused them and warned once.
\item In \texttt{fio} three timing functions return zero for a negative interval, under the comment
``time warp bug on some kernels?'', and in \texttt{btt} \texttt{tdelta()} returns one nanosecond
for stage timestamps out of order; neither records that it fired. Rezolus counts the samples its
scheduler sampler discards, those whose two timestamps arrived out of order across CPUs, in a metric
added on 12 August 2026; its other samplers have none.
\item Four further tools we read are not in the count, because they do not make the measurement.
SPECjms2007 defines Delivery Time and never reports it, Redpanda's \texttt{rpk} ships no latency
benchmark, Pepper-Box times only the call handing a message to Kafka's client, and xk6-kafka, four of
whose latency figures are always zero, times whole calls of its script.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The tests.} T1 added a delay to the path in steps, a staircase. It cannot reach a managed
service, so PerfKit and the Pub/Sub load test ran its zero step alone. Every other tool's figure
but the Yahoo Streaming Benchmark's and RocketMQ's followed the delay at a slope inside the band the
plan fixed in advance. T2 used libfaketime, which shifts the clock a process reads, to move back the
clock of the process that subtracts, at two offsets longer than the interval the tool times. A
program registered with the plan in freeze 21, the judge, then chose for each run among five
behaviors: keeps every value, drops the negatives, drops zero and below, replaces the negatives with
zero, and times against one clock. The second block exposed two faults in the judge. It ruled a tool
that kept fewer values than it was asked to publish out of keeping every value but not out of timing
against one clock, which keeps every value too. Thus a run of Pulsar's client that kept
\toolsPulsarOneClockKept{} of \toolsAskedToSend{} latencies was judged to time against one clock.
And it predicts each behavior's figures from the trips of a reference, our own client for the same
server, so a tool with a slow tail of its own can be ruled out of the behavior it shows. Version 34
(\brk{freezes/31-experiment-plan}), public before either block was judged again, corrects the first
fault and decides how many clocks a tool reads from the offset alone, without the reference. A tool
reads two if it lost values or its average, median or minimum moved by more than half the offset,
one if it kept every value and none of those figures moved by a quarter of it, and is undecided
otherwise. Every T2 run of both blocks was judged again under both rules, and the corrected rule
changed two verdicts, both for runs of Pulsar's client: the run above became undecided, and an
undecided one became ``drops the negatives''. A tool counts below as reading one clock, or two, only
if the rule decided some of its runs and every run it decided agrees with the clocks its interval
implies.

Of the tools run, \toolsOneClockWord{} read both timestamps from one clock, and moving the clock
left their figures within a quarter of the offset in $\toolsOneClockHeld$ of their
$\toolsOneClockOf$ runs; in the other, SBK's minimum, printed in whole milliseconds, moved by one.
All but PerfKit take both timestamps in one process. PerfKit's publisher and receiver are two
processes of one program on one machine, and T2's offset moved the clock of both. Freeze 21's
judge, whose allowance for noise comes from a staircase PerfKit has none of, left all its
clock-moved runs undecided, and version 34's rule answers one clock in every one, as version 37
predicted. The \toolsTwoClockWord{} tools counted as reading two clocks timestamp a message in one
process and subtract in another, \texttt{kgo-repeater} on another machine, and in
$\toolsTwoClockRuns$ of their $\toolsTwoClockOf$ runs the moved clock shifted their figures or cut
what they kept. Pulsar's client drops a value below zero: under the larger offset it kept
$\toolsPulsarKeptLo$ to $\toolsPulsarKeptHi$ of $\toolsAskedToSend$ latencies, yet its summary
printed $\toolsAskedToSend$ records received beside figures computed from those few.
\texttt{emqtt-bench}'s histogram kept every value, but its console printed no latency in any offset
run, because it prints its once-a-second average only when a counter of the latencies above zero
changes. \kafka{}'s Trogdor replaces a value below zero with zero, so a moved clock can lower its
figures only by its own latency, less than half the larger offset, and its runs at that offset are
undecided. The judge read librdkafka's \texttt{rdkafka\_performance} as keeping every value in
\toolsKeepsRoundsWord{} of its \toolsRoundsWord{} rounds, but no line reporting a dropped value
appears in any of its runs and none of its values came near zero, so that verdict tested nothing.
The rule could not place three tools, the Pravega benchmark, Apache Artemis's perf client and the
Pub/Sub load test, which are in neither count.

\textbf{Six tools whose results need explaining.} The Pravega benchmark's count, not its clock,
misled the rule. It takes both timestamps in one process, and its T2 moved the machine's own clock

34's rule reads a count shorter than its control's as values dropped. It answered two clocks in the

\toolsPravegaBelowControlWord{} below its control's, undecided in the \toolsCountedUndecidedWord{}

Apache Artemis's perf client reads two clocks but hid the offset by having each refused value
delivered again. Its histogram throws before it counts a value below zero, so the client logs
AMQ132001 and rolls the message back, and the broker delivers it again, up to ten times, until it

The Pub/Sub load test printed nothing under the offsets, because its controller threw first. It
counts each value into a bucket by the logarithm of its milliseconds, so every value at or below a

\textbf{The step test.} A tool whose two timestamps come from one clock meets a value below zero
only if that clock steps back between them, as it did for a user of Stream PerfTest during a
cluster's rolling restart. T2's constant offset cannot make one, so version 37
(\brk{freezes/34-experiment-plan}) added S1. S1's tools ran once as they are and once while the
driver's wall clock stepped back by 20~ms, or 50~ms where their trip is longer, at gaps drawn around
a fifth of a second, with chrony stopped. A message on its way when the clock steps back is timed
short by the step, and comes out below zero if the step is longer than its trip. Once the steps were
undone, the wall clock was within $\sOneClockBackMs$~ms of the hypervisor's PTP clock. Every
prediction versions 37 to 41 fixed from the tools' sources held in all \sOneRoundsWord{} rounds
except Sarama's and the Pub/Sub load test's. Each of the six tools on one clock that the steps
reach, PerfKit, Strimzi Canary, Stream PerfTest, Xinfra Monitor, SBK and the Pravega benchmark,
showed a value below zero its own way in every stepped run, and none in its controls
(Table~\ref{stab:sone}). The Pravega benchmark's statistics task threw at the first such value,
losing every later statistic, and its code then exits with status 0, as after printing any
exception, so a run that lost its statistics ends as one that succeeded. \kafka{}'s end-to-end tool
and \texttt{mqtt-benchmark}, on a monotonic clock, did not see the steps. Sarama's producer tool,
also on a monotonic clock, kept its median, but its stepped 99th percentile fell $\sOneSaramaGapLo$
to $\sOneSaramaGapHi$~ms below its control's, so S1-i, which allowed a millisecond either way,
failed in every round. The millisecond allowed was narrower than that figure's spread from one run
to the next. Artemis's producer, which reads two clocks with its consumer, published
$\sOneArtemisSentLo$ to $\sOneArtemisSentHi$ of its $\toolsAskedToSend$ messages in each stepped run,
stopped, and reported success. A step inside one of its timed publishes makes a value its histogram
refuses, in a thread that catches only the messaging library's own exceptions. Version 41
predicted that the Pub/Sub load test's stepped median would lie in its control's bucket or one
either side (S1-l). That failed in \sOnePubsubFailedWord{} of the \sOneRoundsWord{} rounds,

\textbf{Public reports.}\label{s:reach} Public reports rarely carry the deletion's signature, and
no report we found says how many samples it kept. The audit behind that sentence was registered,
committed and public before any report was read (\brk{freezes/29-published-results-audit}). It fixed
the signature, the places to search, the rule for inclusion, the coding and the outcomes, and
nothing in it changed afterwards. The primary signature is a printed end-to-end median of exactly
1~ms, the smallest value the benchmark can print and the value every retained sample takes when the
true end-to-end latency is shorter than the resolution. The strong signature adds a p99 of exactly
1~ms. Neither says a reported number is wrong, only that it came from an unstated fraction of the
samples. The search covered Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, the web, the
engineering blogs of the vendors whose brokers the benchmark has drivers for, and the benchmark's
repository and public copies, for reports up to 28 September 2026. A report was included
if it named the benchmark or a fork as its tool and printed at least one numeric end-to-end
percentile.

The audit included $\reachReports$ reports, printing $\reachConfigs$ configurations, of which
$\reachMedianConfigs$ print a median. The primary signature occurs in \reachPrimaryConfigsWord{}
configurations in \reachPrimaryReportsWord{} reports, both public repository text: five are
\redis{} rates in a notebook behind a 2023 journal paper that shows its medians only in a chart,
and three are maximum-rate runs in three result files of a 2021 fork. The strong signature occurs
nowhere, and no included report states its retention or a discard count. The rules did not foresee
several patterns. $\reachFinerReports$ of the included reports print medians that are not whole
milliseconds, \reachSubMsReportsWord{} of them below a millisecond, which the original benchmark's
clock cannot produce. They come from copies whose maintainers changed how the span is timed, and
for \kafka{} and Pulsar those copies still take the publish timestamp in whole milliseconds and keep
the positivity filter. Five more reports only look finer: the benchmark's histogram prints a whole
millisecond above about 262~ms as the top of a bucket wider than a microsecond, such as
507.001~ms, and their copies time the span as the original does. One 2024 fork prints p95, p99 and
p999 all at exactly 1~ms beside an average just above it and no median column, so by the registered
rule it does not count. Four configurations print a median of zero, from empty histograms. The
coding, the search log and every excluded report with its reason are in
\brk{docs/results/external/published_results_audit} (postmortem, S25.1).

One more pattern was read after the audit and is not one of its outcomes. The benchmark's
RabbitMQ driver takes the publish timestamp from the message's AMQP timestamp property, which
carries whole seconds, so every RabbitMQ end-to-end value it prints carries an extra zero to one

\section{S6. The registered law campaign}
\label{s:law}

A law for the negative-span rate, fixed before its runs, had one prediction confirmed on every
campaign that tested it, three on one broker only, and the rest not confirmed
(Table~\ref{stab:lawverdicts}). The one confirmed everywhere is that load raises the rate at the
shortest end-to-end latencies. Neither prediction of where the rate falls with the end-to-end latency, at a slice set by hand,
was confirmed. The predictions of how quickly it falls held on one broker at most, and Python's
rate exceeded the registered limit relative to Java's.
Frozen programs judged each prediction over 8,102 runs on machines no earlier campaign used.

\subsection{S6.1. The law, and the design that tested it}
\label{s:law-design}

The registered law says that the scheduler's settings alone fix where the negative-span rate falls
as the end-to-end latency lengthens, and how wide the fall is. It assumes that a thread kept
waiting waits out a whole slice $s$ of another task, and then up to one tick $h$. The rate then
sits on a plateau $c + p$ while the end-to-end latency $D$ is shorter than the slice, falls across
one tick, and rests on a floor $c$ beyond it:
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:law}
r(D) = c + p \cdot \begin{cases} 1, & D \le s,\\ (s + h - D)/h, & s < D < s + h,\\ 0, & D \ge s + h. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here $p$ is the waiting probability of Equation~\ref{P-eq:occupancy}, and $c$ is the floor that ordinary
short delays leave. The slice and the tick predict where the fall starts and how wide it is; the
plateau's height and the floor are measured. In the plan, a reading is negative when the thread
that reads the clock for the acknowledgment waits longer than the margin $D - g$, a run's median
end-to-end latency less its median publish latency $g$, the plan's letter for $A$. The campaign
lengthens only the path to the consumer, so within a setup $g$ stays put and the margin moves one
for one with $D$.

The three Azure pairs of Table~\ref{stab:testbeds} ran the campaign. The plan's blocks A1 to~A9
are written L1 to~L9 here. L1 sets the slice on the first
x86 pair, and L4 on the Arm pair. L2 sets the tick, on three kernels built from one source tree
that differ in the tick alone: 1, 4 and 10~ms. L3 sets the load, L5 the number of online CPUs, L7
real-time priority for the threads that read the clock, and L8 the client, \kafka{}'s own Java
client against our Python one. L6 records every \texttt{python3} thread's waits in half of L1's and
L3's runs, and L9 records each of those threads' scheduling events in half its own runs. Each block
carries numbered predictions, stated and judged in Table~\ref{stab:lawverdicts}. A further block,
M0, on bursty traffic, bears on no claim here.

Each run replays messages at a steady 50 a second and measures its end-to-end latency, publish
latency and negative-span rate after a 30~s warm-up. The end-to-end latency is set by delaying only
the traffic to the receiver's own network address, so the acknowledgment's path is untouched. Each
session first calibrates how far each added delay moves the end-to-end latency, and an end-to-end
latency shorter than the client's own with nothing added cannot be set, and was not run. The
integrity rule judges every run as it ends. A run counts when every condition it was meant to have
took effect, from the load within 3 points of its target to the added delay within $50\,\mu$s of its
setting. Otherwise it is repeated, up to three attempts. A stopping rule on the publish latency
stopped a campaign whose median publish latency moved too far, until the final version made it
only record. The plan's own name for the publish latency, and for the stopping rule on it, is
``got-it''.

A round runs every setup once. Each campaign that tests a prediction took its rounds, at least four
and at most forty, from simulating its own decision rule on data that follow the law. L2 and L9,
which have no rule the simulation can take, ran at the floor of four, and no campaign ran more than
24. A prediction about where the fall sits is read twice, on a free reading, a curve required only
never to rise, and on the law's fitted shape, and it passes only where both agree. The judging read
8,102 runs in 156 campaigns, taken from 16 to 27 September 2026, and 7,996 of them counted.

The plan was first frozen as version~5 on 16~September 2026, before its first run, and the version
in force at the end is the thirty-second. A frozen version is never edited. A change is a new,
numbered freeze, public before the runs it covers, whose first section says what changed, why, and
whether a result already seen prompted it. Three kinds of change followed the campaigns' own
results. Version~14, after L3's first campaign could not place the fall at $50\%$ load, judged P3a
only where the plateau reaches $2\%$. The stopping rule was corrected five times, each time after
it had stopped a campaign. And three versions corrected judging programs that had already judged,
each stating what the verdicts it touches had been. Blocks added as the campaign ran, in versions
24 and 27 to~29, were each read by a program fixed before their runs, and P6 was given its program
in version~30, at a margin no run had been read against. Every freeze is in the repository's
\brk{freezes/} folder, whose index says what each one changed and whether a result prompted it.
The index also says how a reader can check that each freeze came before its runs, and what each
check rests on: fingerprints that depend on the files alone, the commit each run records, and the
time of each push and of the checks it started, which GitHub keeps for about ninety days. The
history was last rewritten on 5 October 2026, which gave every earlier commit, every freeze's
among them, a new identifier, and no archive dated by a third party yet holds a freeze.

\subsection{S6.3. Read after the verdicts: the wait is the rest of a slice, and two judging programs departed from the plan}
\label{s:law-after}

None of what follows, read after the verdicts, changes one. The rate holds no plateau: at half the
slice it is 1.45 to 3.94 times its value at 0.9 of the slice, in all ten slice and broker
combinations whose runs reach half the slice.

L9's recordings, at a 3~ms slice and a 1~ms tick, time the wait of the thread that reads the clock
for each acknowledgment, for $\recWaitAcks$ acknowledgments in $\recWaitRuns$ runs. Of those whose thread waited
more than 1~ms, 24.3 to 35.8\% were still waiting at 3~ms and 3.3 to 6.8\% past 4~ms, over twelve
pair, broker and load parts. A thread preempted for a task starting its slice waits out all of it,
but a thread woken while another task holds the core waits out what is left, and turning off the
kernel's run-to-parity rule did not shorten that wait (\ref{s:orderscope}). The acknowledgment's
reply wakes the thread that reads it, so its wait is the second kind. A whole-slice wait would
leave all of them still waiting at 3~ms; a wait for the rest of the slice leaves $20\%$ waiting at
3~ms and none past 4~ms. That thread waits out what is left of the running task's slice, $R$,
uniform on $[0, s]$, and then up to a tick, $U$, uniform on $[0, h]$, so that for $h \le s$
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:restofslice}
G(x) = \Pr[R + U > x] = \begin{cases} 1 - x^2/(2sh), & 0 \le x \le h,\\ 1 - (x - h/2)/s, & h \le x \le s,\\ (s + h - x)^2/(2sh), & s \le x \le s + h,\\ 0, & x \ge s + h. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The recording raised the rate it recorded, $\recRaisedKafkaLo$ to $\recRaisedKafkaHi$ times for \kafka{} and $\recRaisedRedisLo$ to $\recRaisedRedisHi$ times
for \redis{}, so these waits describe the machines with it on. Fitted to a rate that falls from the
shortest end-to-end latency on, the law's shape puts its fall below the slice and moves it less
than the slice moves, which is why the free and fitted readings of P1 and P9 split.

In two places a frozen program departed from the plan, and in both the frozen answer is the one
reported. The programs judging P2, P2b and P2c group L2's runs by tick alone, so each width they
read spans the falls of both its slices, 1.5 and 3~ms. With Equation~\ref{seq:law} in place of
every run's rate, as if the law held exactly, the P2 program returns $\cliffPooledRedisFree$ free
and $\cliffPooledRedisFitted$ fitted for \redis{}, below its band. One slice at a time, the same
formula gives $\cliffPerSliceFourLo$ to $\cliffPerSliceFourHi$ for P2 and $\cliffPerSliceTenLo$ to
$\cliffPerSliceTenHi$ for P2b, inside both bands. Those three verdicts therefore carry no evidence
about the law either way. P4's program words \redis{} ``not confirmed'' although its plateau,
$0.69\%$, lies below the $2\%$ under which the plan's rule reports a plateau as out of reach.
