\subsection{S1.1. Testbeds and clocks}
\label{s:setup-testbeds}

Each of three testbeds carries a different part of the evidence
(Table~\ref{stab:testbeds}). On the cloud testbed the producer and the consumer run as separate
processes on one host with $\testbedCpus$ online CPUs, and read one wall clock,

\subsection{S1.2. Workload}
\label{s:setup-workload}

Three properties of the football feed drive the experimental choices: it is sparse, it is
bursty with one burst at a known instant, and its concurrency is bounded. The feed is
StatsBomb's open event data~\cite{statsbomb2023}. Our characterization covers $\corpusMatches$
of its matches, and the campaigns replay \replayedMatchesWord{} of them in order at a chosen
send rate. A match produces a median of $3{,}507$ events over about $8{,}412$~s, a mean of
$0.415$ events per second. A match's busiest ten-second window holds $2.70$ events per second
at the median. Each of the eleven replayed plans opens with at least five events at one instant:
two lineup records, two half-start markers and the kick-off pass. Across all $\corpusMatches$
matches, $575$ open with four. That burst meets the producer when it
is coldest, which turns a one-off client start-up cost into the median of a short run
(\ref{s:withdrawn}).

Kick-off times are recorded, so concurrency is observed: across $2{,}346$ kick-off instants at
most $12$ matches kick off together, and at most $21$ are in play at once. The historical
broker comparison, E1, ran at $N \in \{1, 9, 10, 12\}$ feeds. The first is the median kick-off
slot, which is also its upper quartile. The middle two are the final matchdays of an 18-team and
a 20-team league, and the last is the observed maximum. The powered campaigns, sized in advance
for statistical power, kept $1$, $9$ and $12$ (\ref{s:brokers}). The law campaign replays a steady 50
messages a second instead, except M0, which replays a match's bursts.

\subsection{S1.3. Every setting that can move a latency number}
\label{s:setup-settings}

Two clients are comparable only when matched on what they do, because \kafka{} and \redis{}
expose different settings and matching them by name builds in an asymmetry. Published \kafka{}
comparisons are frequently not comparable for this reason. Client configuration and
acknowledgment semantics differ silently between the systems compared~\cite{mohammad2025kafka}.
\kafka{}'s producer buffers and pipelines by default, while our \redis{} producer dispatches to
a worker pool. With one request outstanding, the \kafka{} client would wait a broker round trip per
event and the \redis{} client would not, a difference in the harness that a reader would
attribute to the broker. Table~\ref{stab:settings} states every client setting that can move a
latency number, and why it holds its value.

\subsection{S1.5. Where each timestamp is taken, and what each span covers}
\label{s:setup-timestamps}

Of the spans the harness reports, only the transport proxy lacks a causal order between its two
timestamps, and only it inverts (Table~\ref{P-tab:spans}). Figure~\ref{sfig:pipeline} draws the
four timestamps of Equation~\ref{P-eq:model} and the fifth, $t_{\mathrm{out}}$, at which
Equation~\ref{P-eq:tti} closes the end-to-end latency. The send lag, $A$ and $D$ each start from
a timestamp taken before the event that ends them can happen, so all three are causal chains and
a late thread can only lengthen them. $S$ subtracts two branches of the broker's append, with no
order between them, so it is negative whenever $A > D$ (Equation~\ref{P-eq:negspan}).

\begin{figure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{docs/results/figures/pipeline_schematic.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Five timestamps bracket a message's path.} A replay process paces recorded
match events at a chosen rate into a broker, \kafka{} or \redis{} Streams, and a consumer reads
them back. The upper four spans are the terms of Equation~\ref{P-eq:tti} and sum to the fifth,
the end-to-end latency $E$.}
\label{sfig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The two clients read the acknowledgment timestamp in different places, but in both it is read
only after a thread that was not running has been made runnable and then scheduled. The send
timestamp is read on the calling thread immediately before the client is invoked. Under
\redis{} the acknowledgment timestamp is read on that same thread the moment the synchronous
\texttt{xadd} returns, with no second thread involved. Under \kafka{} it is read, by default, in
the client's I/O thread when the delivery callback fires. Our \kafka{} producer's
\texttt{inline} mode instead timestamps on the calling thread once the send future resolves,
and requires one request outstanding.

The consumer reads $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ when it holds the record, and an output timestamp
$t_{\mathrm{out}}$ once the record is in hand. Our \kafka{} consumer parses the payload inside
\texttt{poll()}, before $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$. Our \redis{} consumer parses it between the two.
The handling span $t_{\mathrm{out}} - t_{\mathrm{recv}}$ is therefore $\spanKafkaHandlingNs$~ns
under \kafka{} and $\spanRedisHandlingNs$~ns under \redis{}, medians of run medians over
$\spanRuns$ runs, and negative on none of the $\spanEvents$ events. The parse thus falls inside
$D$ for \kafka{} only, and it is worth $\spanHandlingSharePct\%$ of \redis{}'s median delivery time of $\spanHandlingDeliveryUs\,\mu$s. A longer $D$ produces fewer negative spans, and \kafka{}
already has the higher negative-span rate, so correcting the asymmetry would widen the gap
between the brokers in Table~\ref{P-tab:spans}. The end-to-end latency runs to
$t_{\mathrm{out}}$, not $t_{\mathrm{recv}}$, so it contains both parses and is unaffected.

\subsection{S2.1. The threshold sweep}
\label{s:signcheck-sweep}

Per-run rates offer no natural threshold, so the line had to be chosen. On the workstation
testbed $104$ runs carry no negative span at all, and the rest spread over more than four decades of
rate (Fig.~\ref{sfig:threshold-sweep}a). Raising the threshold lowers the share of runs
rejected (Fig.~\ref{sfig:threshold-sweep}b), and at one percent the rule rejects
$\auditPctWorkstation\%$ of the $\auditRunsWorkstation$. The verdict on our first result does not
move with it. Swept from $0$ to $20\%$, the threshold makes none of that result's cells usable,
and the best of them keeps $23$ of its $30$ runs (\ref{s:withdrawn}).

\section{S3. Failure 1: the full evidence}
\label{s:failure1}

Failure~1 comes from the scheduler. Every manipulation of the timestamping thread's wait for a
core, or of the delivery time that wait must outlast, moved the negative-span rate the way the
mechanism predicts. A kernel trace that times the wait predicts the rate to within a third,
unfitted. The evidence follows the order of Section~\ref{P-sec:mechanism}: three manipulations,
the model they support, the trace, the rivals and the timescale. The cost, which the paper sets
out among its practical implications (Section~\ref{P-sec:cost}), comes last.

\subsection{S3.1. Real-time priority cuts the rate in every matched pair}
\label{s:priority}

Moving the timestamping threads to real-time priority cut the negative-span rate between
$\rtFactorLow$- and $\rtFactorHigh$-fold in each of \rtPairsWord{} matched pairs, with utilization
unchanged. Under \texttt{SCHED\_FIFO} the threads preempt the background load instead of queueing
behind it, so their occupancy falls while the machine stays as busy. An occupancy mechanism
predicts a collapse toward the running-state floor. Any account in which utilization alone sets
the rate predicts no change, and the M/G/1 waiting-time form we had adopted and registered is
such an account (S8).

The pairs come from three campaigns (Table~\ref{stab:priority}). E-A5 is the original
manipulation, E-A5b widened the range of loads to $\rtRhoLow$ and $\rtRhoHigh\%$, and E-A7
replicated E-A5. A rule fixed in advance withholds a pair whose two achieved utilizations differ
by more than $\manipTol$. It withheld $\rtConfounded$ pairs, and the widest disagreement in the
table is $\manipWorst$, $\manipMargin$ times inside the tolerance.

\subsection{S3.2. One utilization, reached two ways, gives two rates}
\label{s:geometry}

that runs $k$ of the $C=\testbedCpus$ cores flat out leaves the other $C-k$ free, while one that
duty-cycles every core leaves none. If the timestamping thread fails when it cannot find a free
core, the spread load should be worse at equal utilization, and it is
(Table~\ref{stab:geometry}).

the contrast refutes is the single-parameter form, in which $\rho$ alone fixes the rate.

\subsection{S3.4. Two states account for the rate, and the second is the run queue}
\label{s:twostate}

Two states are the smallest model consistent with these data. A timestamping thread is either
running, and timestamps late by a narrow jitter, or waiting for a core. Condition the
identity of Equation~\ref{P-eq:negspan} on the delivery time. Write $p$ for the thread's occupancy of
the waiting state and $G$ for the survival function of its residual stall there, the part of the wait still to
come. To leading
order,
\begin{equation}
\Pr[S<0 \mid T_{\mathrm{true}}] = p\, G(T_{\mathrm{true}}) .
\label{seq:twostate}
\end{equation}
Load moves $p$ without fixing it (S3.2), so the form is written without a load term.
Figure~\ref{sfig:twostate} draws it as the distribution of the timestamping asymmetry
$\Delta = \delta_{\mathrm{recv}} - \delta_{\mathrm{ack}}$, the delay of the receipt's timestamp
less that of the acknowledgment's, which turns negative while the acknowledgment's thread waits
for a core.

corrupted together. A Wald--Wolfowitz runs test on each run's sequence of span signs finds strong

of the load sweep, E-A3's negative-span rate grows $\invGrowth$-fold while the jitter width grows

why it fails here. The highest rate, $\invCeiling$ at $\rho = 1$, is the

not a bound.

The model also predicts a shape. A scale family changes $\sigma$, so its tail curves rescale
horizontally. The two-state model changes $p$, so its curves shift vertically and stay parallel on

Over tail estimates backed by at least $20$ events, that log ratio's spread over thresholds has a
median of $0.29$ across conditions, a factor of $1.34$, and a worst case of $0.91$. The
scale-family collapse fails by $23\times$. The rate therefore separates into $p$ and $G$ to first order, with
the two largest spreads at intermediate load. The test was exploratory, and a confirmatory version
was registered and run, the decisive campaign being the priority manipulation of S3.1.

On the load axis alone the model has no content, since with $p$ unconstrained any monotone curve
in $\rho$ can be written $p(\rho)\,G$. A fit on that axis cannot credit the jitter width either.
Restoring it to the form reaches $R^2=0.9905$, and freezing it at its mean does better,

The campaigns measure $p$ only as a time average. E-A7 registered that real-time priority would

consequences stand without it.

timed the waiting state for every acknowledgment and measured the shape of $G$, in a reading made
after its verdicts (S6.5).

\subsection{S3.5. A kernel trace predicts the rate unfitted, and changes it}
\label{s:tracer}

The negative-span rate matches, to within a third, the probability that a run-queue stall outlasts the delivery time, measured without reference to any negative span. With \texttt{bpftrace} on
the \texttt{sched\_wakeup} and \brk{sched_switch} tracepoints we recorded the delay between a
timestamping thread becoming runnable and reaching a core, for every such transition in a run.
The two quantities share no probe, no estimator and no data. One is a count of negative spans in
application records, the other a kernel histogram of scheduler transitions. Across the three
ordinary configurations of Table~\ref{stab:tracer} the ratio of traced probability to measured

whose probe failed to attach, ran two hours apart with the same design and measured

$0.305$ over its four untraced measurements, and the replication's traced run reads $0.196$. It limits the level, on which no
claim rests. The agreement above compares two quantities from one run, which a common
perturbation moves together, and each prediction is held against the traced run's own rate.

from its untraced comparator by more than $25\%$ is not. The replication's $88\%$ configuration
measures $0.1956$ against the control's $\untracedRate$, a drift of $28\%$, so its comparison is
withheld. Its row stays in the table with the ratio it would have given, $1.06$.

showing none is $3\times10^{-7}$, and of all three below $10^{-17}$. Attaching BPF to

claim rests on that configuration's level. The tracer changed the quantity it came to observe,

\subsection{S3.6. Ten rival explanations, each excluded or bounded}
\label{s:rivals}

No rival to the run queue survives the corpus, and four fall to the manipulations already shown
(Section~\ref{P-sec:threats}). One clock excludes clock skew. Identical utilization at rates
twofold apart excludes utilization. \texttt{SCHED\_FIFO} excludes timer noise, which does not
respect scheduling class, and a longer path that lowers the rate excludes stress. Coarse
kernel timestamps fall to the runs test of S3.4. The other five need an argument each.

\paragraph{The second thread} \kafka{}'s client timestamps the acknowledgment on its I/O thread
when the delivery callback fires, and \redis{}'s on the calling thread the moment \texttt{XADD}
returns (Figure~\ref{P-fig:model}; S1.5). The harness was built expecting that hand-off to cause the
negatives. The hand-off is real. Timestamping \kafka{} on the calling thread shrinks the gap
between the brokers' medians by $\hThreeShrinkage$~ms [$95\%$ bootstrap over runs:
$\hThreeShrinkageCI$], about a quarter, and
by $\hThreeRepShrinkage$~ms in a replication.

It is not the mechanism. \redis{}, which has no
second thread, inverts on $\spanRedisNegAckPct\%$ of its events against \kafka{}'s
$\spanKafkaNegAckPct\%$, and on more of its runs ($\spanRedisRunsAckInvertsPct\%$ against
$\spanKafkaRunsAckInvertsPct\%$). A thread returning from a blocking socket read must also be
woken and scheduled before it reads the clock, and that wait is charged exactly as a callback's
is. The cleaner control, \kafka{}'s rate under both timestamping modes, cannot be computed,
because that campaign kept only its medians.

\paragraph{The scheduler} The scheduler works as designed. A base slice with a wakeup-preemption
check trades wakeup latency for throughput and fairness, and the escapes from it are opt-in:
real-time priority, a preemption-free build, or a pinned busy-polling core. The kernel's
\texttt{SO\_TIMESTAMPING} already returns the time a packet arrived, and none of the tools we
audited uses it.

\paragraph{The hypervisor} The hypervisor is bounded, not excluded, because both testbeds are
virtualized. The cloud instances are virtual machines, and the workstation runs its brokers in
Docker Desktop containers inside a WSL2 virtual machine on Microsoft's hypervisor. Two
observations disagree with an account in which stolen processor time causes the negatives.
Real-time priority is a guest-side control, which stolen virtual-CPU time would not yield to,
and it cuts the rate $\rtFactorLow$- to $\rtFactorHigh$-fold. The upper mode of the stall
spectrum sits on the guest kernel's own slice arithmetic (S3.7), on no quantity the hypervisor
exposes. Steal time may still contribute, and no testbed here can show how much.

\paragraph{The interpreter lock} The interpreter lock is present in principle and bounded in
practice. The timestamping threads are CPython, which releases the lock around blocking I/O and
retakes it on return, and the clock read is a Python call. A timestamp therefore waits for a core
and then for the lock. The kernel trace cannot see the second wait, which happens after the
switch, in user space. Priority does not separate the two, since a \texttt{SCHED\_FIFO} thread
also reaches the lock sooner, and nor does magnitude, since the default switch interval and the
base slice are both of millisecond order.

The direction of the disagreement settles it. A second
wait in series with the run queue would leave an estimator built from the run queue alone short
of the observed rate, and its ratios, $\tracedRatios$, straddle unity instead. The registered
campaign found the same failure in \kafka{}'s own Java client, which has no interpreter lock, at
a lower rate (S6.3).

\paragraph{The clock} Clock incoherence across virtual CPUs~\cite{kvmclock} is excluded twice.
First, a measurement bounds the clocksource. The platform probe's smallest observed increment of
\texttt{clock\_gettime(CLOCK\_MONOTONIC)} was $\clockIncrementNs$~ns, an upper bound on the cost of
one read. That excludes \texttt{acpi\_pm} by arithmetic, since its tick is $\clockPmTickNs$~ns,
and \texttt{hpet} by its published read cost near $1.2\,\mu$s. What remains, \clockAdmitted{},
fits the bound, and each carries the coherence guarantee.

A \texttt{kvm-clock} that fast is on
the vDSO path, which \texttt{kvmclock.c} enables only when the hypervisor sets
\texttt{PVCLOCK\_TSC\_STABLE\_BIT}, the flag that guarantees monotonic time across CPUs. A KVM
guest selects \texttt{tsc} only when the kernel finds it constant, non-stop and stable, and
re-rates it away if it later warps. Which clocksource ran is unrecorded, and the published read
costs vary about twofold, against an order-of-magnitude margin.

Second, the corpus leaves no headroom. An offset between clocks large enough to produce the
deepest negative span, $\spanDeepestInversionMs$~ms, would have driven the delivery time $D$ far
below zero. Its floor, $\spanSendFloorUs\,\mu$s, bounds any such offset $\spanOffsetMargin$-fold below what
the deepest negative span needs.

\subsection{S3.7. The upper stall mode survives coarsening, and its bucket holds the derived slice}
\label{s:timescale}

The upper mode of the traced stall spectrum survives a change of bucket edges, and the bucket it
occupies holds the scheduler's derived base slice. A count of local maxima belongs jointly to the
counts and to the bin edges, and
those edges came from \texttt{bpftrace} as the millisecond came from the benchmark. The per-event
stall durations were not retained, so coarsening is the only test available. Merging adjacent
buckets doubles the bin width, and starting the merge at the first bucket or the second puts the
edges in different places. The spectrum has $\stallModeCount$ maxima on the tool's own
$\stallModeBuckets$ buckets, $\stallModeCount$ again under both phasings at $\stallCoarsenFactor$
times the width, and fewer at $\stallLostFactor$ times, so the count holds to within a factor of
$\stallCoarsenFactor$ in bin width.

Above the upper mode the counts collapse. They fall $\tracedModeFallA$-, $\tracedModeFallB$- and
$\tracedModeFallC$-fold over the next \tracedModeFallOctaves{} octaves and then
$\tracedLastBucketFall$-fold, each octave a doubling of the wait, where a power law would fall by one factor every time. Power laws
fitted below the mode and above it both fail a bootstrap goodness-of-fit ($p\tracedGofP$ and
$p\tracedTailGofP$, over $\tracedGofBoot$ replicates each).

The slice is derived from the kernel's rule and the campaign's own data. Each geometry
condition loads $k$ cores and records the achieved utilization, so $k/\rho$ recovers
$\testbedCpus$ cores six times over, with a spread of $\cpuEstimateSpread$. The kernel's rule
scales a $0.75$~ms constant by one plus the integer base-two logarithm of the CPU count, up to
eight CPUs, giving $\sliceFactor\times0.75\,\mathrm{ms}=\baseSliceMs$~ms~\cite{zhou2025slice}.
The constant is specific to this kernel and became $0.70$~ms in v6.15, after our 6.8. The build
sets \texttt{CONFIG\_HZ}${}=\kernelHz$, a $\tickMs$~ms tick.

With run-to-parity, a woken thread
that loses the wakeup-preemption check waits for the incumbent to finish its slice. The same
posting notes that a task notices its deadline only at the next tick, so waits end in a band a
tick wide. No scheduler tunable is written anywhere in the archived campaign, so the derived value
is the one that applied. That is strong evidence, short of proof, and a future campaign should
read \texttt{base\_slice\_ns} and \texttt{CONFIG\_HZ} off the image under test. How long a woken
thread actually waits was timed later by the registered campaign, which found that it waits out what
is left of a slice, up to a whole one, in a reading made after its verdicts (S6.5).

\section{S4. Failure 2: the full evidence}
\label{s:failure2}

The benchmark's printed latency describes only the samples its send schedule lets through, and
each link in that claim can be checked against a source line, a counter or a registered
prediction. Every benchmark run below is in embedded mode, with the whole benchmark in one JVM.

\subsection{S4.1. Two source lines make the filter, and counting what they drop changes no printed number}
\label{s:filterpatch}

The positivity filter can be counted without being disturbed. In \brk{LocalWorker.java}:294
the benchmark computes its end-to-end span as
\begin{quote}
\texttt{MILLISECONDS.toMicros(now - publishTimestamp)}
\end{quote}
from two millisecond timestamps, so the microseconds decorate a millisecond subtraction. In
\brk{WorkerStats.java}:95 the positivity filter,
\begin{quote}
\texttt{if (endToEndLatencyMicros > 0)}
\end{quote}
decides whether that value reaches the histogram. The message has already been counted as
received, but nothing counts what the condition drops.

We add counters in an \texttt{else} branch of that condition and change nothing else: not the
latency computation, not the filter, not any reported statistic. The benchmark's output is
therefore exactly what it would have been unpatched, and its printed summary can be set beside
the retention behind it. The four counters, for zero, negative, most negative and kept samples,
separate sign. A negative span and a sub-millisecond delivery time both fail a \texttt{> 0} test, and
one unsigned total cannot tell them apart (\ref{s:minus1000}).

The counts are exact. The counters are shared across the JVM and their totals printed once at
shutdown. A run that fails discards nothing, so its zero would look measured, and it is written
as invalid, with its reason and no count. A matched sweep with the benchmark's one-minute warmup
disabled leaves retention unchanged. The campaign totals $\ombRuns$ instrumented
runs, swept one axis at a time with background load, message size, producer rate and warmup
otherwise fixed.

\subsection{S4.2. The phase of the send schedule sets retention, as a 1970 counter note already showed}
\label{s:retentionlaw}

Where each send falls within a step of the clock decides whether its sample survives. A
delivery time shorter than the step $\tau$ survives only if it crosses a grid instant, where the
timestamp changes value. Otherwise both timestamps floor alike, and the positivity filter deletes the zero
(Fig.~\ref{sfig:geometry}a). Uniform phases retain $\theta = T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$, which is
Equation~\ref{P-eq:retention} of the paper.

\begin{figure}[tb]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.55\columnwidth]{docs/results/figures/quantum_geometry.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Where a send falls within a millisecond decides whether its sample survives.}
(a)~One delivery time of $T_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.35$~ms at four phases of one millisecond. Three
finish inside it, compute to zero and are removed. The fourth crosses the boundary and is
recorded as one whole millisecond. (b)~A sample survives from phase $\tau - T_{\mathrm{true}}$
onward, the shaded band, so uniform phases retain $T_{\mathrm{true}}/\tau$. A commensurate
producer visits only $q$ phases and retains a count out of $q$. The delivery time drawn is
off-vertex by choice, since at $0.5$~ms the two answers coincide.}
\label{sfig:geometry}
\end{figure}

A paced producer does not draw its phases uniformly. If its send interval $T_{\mathrm{send}}$
satisfies $T_{\mathrm{send}}/\tau = a/q$ in lowest terms, it visits only $q$ distinct phases, so
its retention is a whole count out of $q$ (Fig.~\ref{sfig:geometry}b). Call the retentions
$0, 1/q, \ldots, 1$ the vertices of the configuration's $q$-lattice, and the interval between
neighboring vertices, $100/q$ percentage points wide, a lattice step. Replicates of
one configuration land on the vertices that bracket $\theta$, and their spread obeys
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:spread}
\text{spread} \;\longrightarrow\;
\begin{cases}
100/q & \text{when } \theta \text{ sits mid-step,}\\[2pt]
0 & \text{when } \theta \text{ sits on a vertex.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The step width is thus an upper bound on the spread, reached only when replicates realize both
bracketing vertices. The incommensurate rates put $T_{\mathrm{true}}$ at
$\spreadIncommensurateTrueMs$~ms, half a millisecond to within replicate noise
(Section~\ref{P-sec:extcomp}). A half lies on every $q$-lattice with even $q$ and on none with odd
$q$, so the rule predicts full step width for odd denominators and almost none for even ones.

None of this arithmetic is new. Hewlett-Packard's Application Note 162-1~\cite{hp1970tia}
carries it for a counter timing an interval, point by point.
\begin{itemize}
\item The retention identity. For an interval of $Q + F$ clock periods the note gives
$\Pr[R = Q{+}1] = F$. The share of readings on the upper of the two straddling counts is the
fractional part. That is Equation~\ref{P-eq:retention}.
\item The class of a repetition rate. The class is the denominator of the ratio of repetition
rate to clock rate in lowest terms, our $q$, and it fixes how many distinct readings the counter
can produce.
\item The ceiling on averaging. Averaging $N$ readings can improve resolution by at most a
factor $1/q$, not $1/\sqrt{N}$. The note names $q = 1$ as the corner where averaging cannot help
at all, and a paced producer on a round interval walks straight into it
(Table~\ref{stab:interval}).
\item The symptom. A reading appears to hang on a round number while the input is known to
vary.
\item The cure. Phase jitter on the repetition rate, so the samples stop landing on the same
points of the clock's cycle. Hewlett-Packard patented it. US~3,938,042, filed in 1974 and
granted in 1976, varies the phase of the counter's own time base ``to destroy coherence between
the clock signal and an unknown signal''~\cite{hp1976dither}. A benchmark's time base belongs to
the operating system, so the benchmark must dither the send instant instead.
\end{itemize}
ADC metrology standardizes the phase lemma. IEEE Std~1241-2010 requires the record of its
histogram test to hold a number of input cycles ``relatively prime'' to the number of samples
(\S6.4.1), and names the practice coherent sampling (\S5.4.1)~\cite{ieee1241}. Neither source
contains the deletion. The counter keeps both readings, and the retained fraction is its
estimator.

\subsection{S4.4. A deleted zero changes what the distribution is conditioned on}
\label{s:zeros}

Deleting the samples that compute to zero conditions the reported distribution on being
positive, so the retained fraction is part of the measurement. Two classical sources come close
to this argument. The GUM models a digital reading of resolution $\delta x$ as a stimulus that
``can lie with equal probability anywhere in the interval $X - \delta x/2$ to
$X + \delta x/2$'' (Clause F.2.2.1)~\cite{jcgm100}. That rectangular model, with its variance
$(\delta x)^2/12$, holds for retained readings. The positivity filter removes one side of the
interval, and neither the shape nor the variance survives the asymmetry it adds.

Pratt showed that discarding zero differences before ranking breaks a natural monotonicity. A
sample not judged significantly positive can become so when every observation is
decreased~\cite{pratt1959zeros}. His procedure ranks all observations, zeros included, drops
only the zeros' ranks, and refers the statistic to its null distribution conditional on the
number of zeros. Our claim is weaker and more practical, that the zero count must be published so a
reader can see what the distribution is conditioned on. The exact
parallel is a reading Pratt reports from Meier, that a zero arises because ``with only our crude
measurement, we do not know'' the sign a finer measurement would resolve. That is the
sub-resolution sample of Failure~2, stated in 1959.

Applied to the population of Table~\ref{P-tab:spans} (Figure~\ref{sfig:deletionhist}), a
positivity filter on millisecond timestamps removes
$\msGuardDropped$ of the $\spanEvents$ events, $\msGuardDeletedPct\%$ ($95\%$ bootstrap
interval, resampling runs: $\msGuardDeletedCI$). Of those, $\msGuardAtZero$ computed to exactly
zero and $\msGuardBelowZero$ to less, and a harness that filters this way counts none of them.
Of the five dispositions in panel~(c), two shrink the sample and publish no count of what left,
two hold the count fixed by reporting a value never measured, and one keeps the data. This is
the rule applied to our spans. What the benchmark's own instrumented runs retained is the other
measurement, in Section~\ref{P-sec:extphase} of the paper.

\subsection{S4.5. Two floored clocks disagree by exactly one millisecond, so the sign of a discard names the failed clock}
\label{s:minus1000}

The sign of a discarded sample says which clock failed, and the benchmark's two drivers show
both kinds. The \kafka{} driver's $\ombKafkaRuns$ runs discarded $\ombKafkaDiscarded$ samples,
and not one was negative. The \redis{}-driver replication's $\ombRedisRuns$ runs carry all
$\ombNegatives$ of the campaign's negatives, every one exactly $-1000\,\mu$s. A single run holds
$\ombRedisWorstNegatives$ of them, half of all the samples it took, beside \ombRedisWorstKept{}
kept.

The drivers explain the difference. The \redis{} driver takes its publish timestamp from
\brk{entry.getID().getTime()} in \brk{RedisBenchmarkConsumer.java}:72, the stream-entry
identifier the \redis{} server assigns on \texttt{XADD}. The \kafka{} driver uses a
producer-set timestamp. So the \redis{} span subtracts a server-side timestamp from a
consumer-side clock reading, across two hosts, with both floored to the millisecond. The
\kafka{} span floors two timestamps whose difference is taken inside one JVM.

Two consequences follow. First, two clocks floored to the same resolution and offset by less
than one millisecond can disagree by one millisecond and by no other amount. The only negative a floored
cross-host difference can produce is therefore $-1000\,\mu$s, and every one of the corpus's
$\ombRedisNegatives$ negatives is that value. Clock discipline was clean at every logged minute,
so this failure needs no misbehaving clock, only two clocks and a floor. Second, a difference
taken inside one process has no second clock to disagree with, which is why the \kafka{}
discards hold no negative. The same positivity filter thus hides a resolution failure under
\kafka{} and a clock failure under \redis{}, and an unsigned discard counter could not tell them
apart. The two drivers also do not share a measurand: client to client on one clock under
\kafka{}, server to client across two under \redis{}. The benchmark does not flag the
difference.

\subsection{S4.6. An independent harness reproduces the deletion, and across two hosts the loss follows the clocks}
\label{s:crosshost}

The deletion does not depend on the benchmark's code, and across two hosts the deleted fraction
drifts with the clocks. We reproduced the positivity filter in a Python harness that shares no
code with the benchmark, with the same timestamp, subtraction and filter. On one clock it
recorded $\harnessOneClockNegatives$ negative differences across $\harnessOneClockSamples$
samples in $\harnessOneClockRuns$ runs, as a causal chain requires, and the grid reappeared
intact. Across two hosts with disciplined clocks, $\harnessCrossHostRuns$ runs again recorded
$\harnessCrossHostNegatives$ negatives in $\harnessCrossHostSamples$ samples and an intact grid,
but the deleted fraction followed the drift of the clock offset. Cross-host retention wandered from $\harnessXHostRetLo$ to $\harnessXHostRetHi\%$ over four
replicates at $\harnessXHostRate$~msg/s, the \harnessXHostArmClass{} configuration and the only
one of the three whose own lattice spread does not swamp the comparison. On one clock the same configuration held to
$\harnessOneClockSpread$ points. Nothing records either the synchronization state or the
fraction it deletes.

The benchmark's own distributed mode, where its subtraction spans two clocks, could not be
measured. It never completed a run in eleven attempts: eight at three background-load levels,
including none, and three more run with full diagnostics. The unloaded attempts failed like the
loaded ones, which refutes our own hypothesis that processor starvation was the cause. The three
diagnosed attempts failed identically, in the coordinator-to-worker path before any workload
started. The first attempt, made before the validity gate of \ref{s:filterpatch} existed, wrote a
vacuous zero that the artifact keeps marked invalid. The gate refused every later attempt, each
with its reason. The
exposure of the distributed binary therefore rests on reading its source: the timestamp is
written on the producer's host and read on the consumer's, and the positivity filter drops
whatever the difference produces.

\subsection{S4.7. A patched benchmark still reads the brokers' millisecond timestamps}
\label{s:wirefield}

Repairing the benchmark does not reach the brokers' own timestamps, because both brokers write
them in milliseconds. \kafka{}'s \texttt{record.timestamp()} is a millisecond field on the wire.
Anything built on it inherits a millisecond resolution and must carry its own finer timestamp in
the payload to escape. Documenting that, or exposing a finer timestamp attached to the record,
is the change to ask of \kafka{}. The \redis{} \texttt{XADD} entry identifier embeds a
server-clock time floored to the millisecond. One documented sentence saying it is not
comparable with a client clock would have prevented the driver defect of \ref{s:minus1000}. No layer here
misbehaves. The benchmark is the only party that assumes something none of them promised: that
observing an event tells you when the event happened. For benchmark authors the checks are those
of Table~\ref{P-tab:checks}, and one more: never difference a server-assigned timestamp against a
client clock without saying so.

\section{S5. The tool registry}
\label{s:tools}

\harnessSilentWordCap{} of the \harnessAuditedWord{} tools we read at source dispose of a
non-positive or sub-resolution sample without counting it, and every verdict traces to a line of
source a reader can check. Each row of the registry file carries one source line, the file and
symbol it came from, the upstream URL and the date it was read.

The class labels are not stored.
They are recomputed on every build and every test run by the classifier that read the
OpenMessaging Benchmark a month before the other tools were audited. A reader who disagrees
with a verdict can therefore check the line against the pattern that matched it. The classifier
sees only what its patterns recognize. They were written against JVM sources and had to be
extended before they could read an Erlang guard or clock call, so the registry bounds the field
from below.

We call a tool's own conditional on the sign of an interval its \emph{guard}. A
guard is a deletion only if what it guards is the sample. Table~\ref{P-tab:tools} of the paper
is the registry, one row per span because one tool can treat two spans differently, generated
from the registry file at build time. Its sources, in row order, are \registryTableSources.

The \harnessSilentWord{} silent disposals take \harnessDisposalClassesWord{} forms. A filter
drops what its comparison excludes. The test $>0$ deletes every sample that computes to exactly
zero, while $\geq 0$ keeps those and drops only negative spans. A filter shrinks the sample the statistic is computed from and
publishes no count of what left. The benchmark filters at $>0$. Apache Pulsar's performance
client guards at $\geq 0$, so it drops negative spans and keeps the zeros the benchmark deletes,
which then pile up visibly at zero.

A substitution keeps the sample count, so any retention computed downstream reads $100\%$, which makes it harder to detect than a filter. A library
refusal leaves the sample unrecorded because nobody checks the library's answer. Truncation is a
separate loss that keeps every sample. The benchmark truncates before it filters, and
\kafka{}'s bundled tools only truncate. The notes below carry what the table cannot.
\begin{itemize}
\item The OpenMessaging Benchmark appears twice because it treats its two spans differently
(Section~\ref{P-sec:extmethod}).
\item The \texttt{EndToEndLatency} tool bundled with \kafka{} cannot invert, because it uses one thread,
one monotonic clock and a synchronous send. It loses the measurement anyway. Each sample prints
in floating point, but the \texttt{long[]} its percentiles come from is filled by the integer
division \texttt{elapsed / 1000 / 1000}. On a sub-millisecond path its p50, p99 and p999
therefore all read zero. The companion \texttt{ProducerPerformance} takes its
send-to-acknowledgment difference on \texttt{System.currentTimeMillis()} and casts it to
\texttt{int}.
\item In \texttt{fio} the guard appears in three timing functions, each returning zero for a
negative interval under the comment ``time warp bug on some kernels?''. The behavior is
defensive and reasonable in isolation. What is missing is any record that it fired.
\item In \texttt{btt}, \texttt{tdelta()} returns one nanosecond whenever the stage timestamps
are out of order, with no comment, no counter and no mention in the manual page.
\item The \texttt{wrk2} load generator detects the negative interval, prints a block that begins
``We are about to crash and die'', and records the value anyway without checking the result.
It builds against its own copy of \texttt{hdr\_histogram.c}, which bounds-checks the derived
index and returns an unchecked \texttt{false}, and whose \texttt{assert()}s are live in the
shipped build. Either the assertion aborts the run or the refusal drops the sample, and nothing
counts the loss.
\item In \texttt{emqtt-bench} the \texttt{> 0} guard protects a counter and leaves the histogram
alone, so nothing is deleted from the sample.
\item RabbitMQ PerfTest hands its difference on with no sign test, but the method that computes
it, \texttt{getDifference}, returns its absolute value, so a negative span enters its metrics as
a positive one of the same size, uncounted. Our first reading stopped at the call site and filed
PerfTest with the tools that pass a negative on. The documentation audit read the method, and
the registry now carries both lines.
\item The NATS CLI appends every difference to the slice its minimum, median and maximum are
read from, negatives included, and is exposed to clock skew between hosts. Its percentile table
is recorded into \texttt{hdrhistogram-go}, whose bound check refuses the index a negative value
maps to. The CLI ignores the error, so the two outputs disagree and nothing counts the
difference. Our first reading stopped at the append. The documentation audit read the histogram.
\item Rezolus counts its discards in a metric that names the cause.
\item Two further tools do not make the measurement. SPECjms2007 defines Delivery Time and never
reports it, and Redpanda's \texttt{rpk} ships no latency benchmark.
\end{itemize}

The same disposal has reached the broker itself. In November 2025 \kafka{}'s coordinator
metrics were found throwing on negative durations after a host clock stepped backwards, and the
merged fix clamps the negative to zero with no counter and no log line~\cite{kafka19888}. That
is silent substitution, adopted in the system whose benchmark opened this audit. Its cause is
genuine wall-clock non-monotonicity, a different mechanism from Failure~1. The same project
makes the opposite choice a few directories away. \kafka{} Streams' end-to-end latency metric
records the wall clock less the record timestamp with no filter, so a record
stamped in the future is recorded as a negative latency~\cite{kafka_streams_e2e}. One project
clamps the negative in one metric and keeps it in another, so the disposal is a choice.

The positivity filter persists downstream and upstream. Of the $\forkChecked$ readable public
forks of the benchmark surveyed on \forkCheckedOn{}, $\forkUnchanged$ carry the filter's
expression unchanged. That bounds how few have diverged and says nothing about why. Confluent's
fork, one of the vendor distributions practitioners run, was archived by its owner on
\forkArchivedOn{} and is read-only, with $\forkArchivedForks$ forks of its own (read \forkArchivedRead{}). A live fork
may drop the filter tomorrow. An archived one cannot, and neither can anything downstream of it.
Upstream, a search of the benchmark's issue tracker for the variable the filter tests, run on
\ombTrackerChecked{}, returns \ombTrackerItems{} item: pull request \#\ombTrackerFirstItem{},
which introduced the filter~\cite{openmessaging_pr56}. No issue or pull request has named it in
the \ombTrackerYearsWord{} years since. That is why the checks of Table~\ref{P-tab:checks} are
addressed to benchmark authors and to their readers.

Other runtimes document the structure behind Failure~1, a timestamp written when a thread next
runs. The \texttt{librdkafka} client library serves its delivery-report callback from
\texttt{rd\_kafka\_poll()} and the other queue-serving calls~\cite{librdkafka}. The callback
therefore runs on the application's own thread, at whatever moment that thread next polls, rather than when the
broker's acknowledgment arrives. The gap between the two plays the part of
$\delta_{\mathrm{ack}}$ in Figure~\ref{P-fig:model}. The \texttt{fio} documentation defines
completion latency for asynchronous ioengines as running to ``the time \ldots when the I/O's
completion was reaped by fio''~\cite{fio_howto}, so a reaping timestamp is written into the
definition of the metric. Neither frames this as a hazard. The property is public, and its
consequence for a subtraction across two processes is drawn nowhere we could find. An origin
timestamp written by a thread that must first be woken is late by that wait, and the span it
opens inverts whenever the wait outlasts the delivery time.

Reading a tool tells what it does with a negative value; running it tells whether it ever meets
one. The registered law campaign ran \toolsRunWord{} benchmarking tools on the first x86 pair,
\toolsRoundsWord{} rounds each, under predictions fixed from their source before any run (its
tools block, frozen with the plan; the amendments that touched
it are in Table~\ref{stab:amendments}). Section~\ref{P-sec:toolsrun} of the paper reports them
in a table. Every staircase slope lies in the band fixed in advance, \toolsOneClockWord{} tools
were judged to time on one clock in every run that decided, and the one tool spanning two clocks
kept every value, negatives included, in each round that decided it. The per-run verdicts are in
\texttt{docs/results/tools/tools\_t1.csv} and \texttt{tools\_t2.csv}, folded by
\texttt{scripts/tools\_block.py} from the runs, and the write-up with its five recorded faults is
\texttt{docs/results/tools/t1-t4-eleven-tools.md}.

\section{S6. The registered law campaign}
\label{s:law}

A law for the negative-span rate of Failure~1, fixed before its runs, had one prediction confirmed
on every campaign, three on one broker, and the rest not. What held on every campaign that
tested it, five on \kafka{} across the three pairs, is that load raises the plateau. No test of whether the fall follows a slice set by hand was confirmed. Frozen programs
judged every prediction over 8,102 runs on machines the earlier campaigns never ran on. The paper
gives the outcome in Section~\ref{P-sec:threats}, and the postmortem, Part~V, keeps the run-by-run
record.

\subsection{S6.1. The law, and the design that tested it}
\label{s:law-design}

The registered law says that the scheduler's settings alone fix where the negative-span rate falls
as the delivery time lengthens, and how wide the fall is. It assumes that a timestamping thread kept
waiting waits out a whole slice $s$ of another task, and then up to one tick $h$. The rate then
sits on a plateau $c + p$ while the delivery time $D$ is shorter than the slice, falls across one
tick, and rests on a floor $c$ beyond it:
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:law}
r(D) = c + p \cdot \begin{cases} 1, & D \le s,\\ (s + h - D)/h, & s < D < s + h,\\ 0, & D \ge s + h. \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here $p$, how often the timestamping thread is kept waiting, is the occupancy of
Equation~\ref{seq:twostate}, and $c$ is the floor that ordinary short delays leave. The slice
and the tick predict where the fall starts and how wide it is. The plateau's height and the floor
are measured. In the plan, a reading is negative when the wait of the thread that
timestamps the acknowledgment outlasts the margin $D - g$, a run's median delivery time less its
median acknowledgment lag $g$, the plan's letter for $A$.

Three pairs of Microsoft Azure machines in one region ran the campaign, each a driver with eight
virtual CPUs and a broker with two; two pairs have AMD EPYC 9V74 processors and one has Arm Neoverse
N2. The plan's blocks A1 to~A9 are written L1 to~L9 here. L1 sets the slice on the first x86 pair,
and L4 on the Arm pair. L2 sets the tick, on three kernels built from one source tree that differ in
the tick alone: 1, 4 and 10~ms. L3 sets the load, L5 the number of online CPUs, L7 real-time
priority for the timestamping threads, and L8 the client, \kafka{}'s own Java client against our
Python one. L6 records every \texttt{python3} thread's waits in half of L1's and L3's runs, and L9
records each of those threads' scheduling events in half its own runs. M0 asks why, when
messages come in bursts, the delivery time does not grow one-for-one with the added delay.

Each block carries numbered predictions, judged in Table~\ref{stab:lawverdicts}. P1 and P9
place the fall at the slice, on the first x86 pair and on the Arm pair. P2, P2b and P2c tie its
width to the tick, and P2d keeps its start at the slice at every tick. P3a and P3b ask what load
does to the fall and to the plateau, P4 what real-time priority does, P7 whether the slice and the
fall follow the core count, and P8 whether \kafka{}'s Java client behaves like our Python one. P6
and L9's three predictions ask whether recorded waits predict the rate.

Each run replays messages at a steady 50 a second, or a football match's bursts in M0, and measures
its delivery time, acknowledgment lag and negative-span rate after a 30~s warm-up. The delivery time
is set by delaying only the traffic to the receiver's own network address, so the acknowledgment's
path is untouched, and each session first calibrates how far each added delay moves it. A delivery
time shorter than the client's own with nothing added cannot be set, and was not run.

The integrity rule judges every run as it ends. A run counts when every condition it was meant to
have took, from the load within 3 points of its target to the added delay within $50\,\mu$s of its
setting. Otherwise it is repeated, up to three attempts. A run whose median acknowledgment lag
moved too far stopped its campaign, a rule called the lag brake. In its final form, from version~20, the lag brake held a run
against its setup's earlier runs in the campaign. The plan's own name for the lag, and for the
brake on it, is ``got-it''.

Each campaign that tests a prediction took its rounds, at least four
and at most forty, from simulating its own decision rule on data that follow the law. L2 and L9,
which have no rule the simulation can take, ran at the floor of four, M0 ran a fixed ten, and no
campaign ran more than 24. A prediction about where the fall sits
is read on a free curve required only never to rise and on the law's fitted shape, and it passes
only where both agree. The judging read 8,102 runs in 156 campaigns, taken from 16 to 27 September
2026, and 7,996 of them counted.

\subsection{S6.4. Aberrant runs are marked, not removed, and one verdict moves without them}
\label{s:law-marks}

The plan keeps every run, and re-runs none whose conditions held. A program reads a mark for each
aberration from the records, and an aberrant run carries its mark wherever it is drawn. Five marks
keep a run out of every judge. One marks void runs, set aside before anything read them. One marks
the 18 runs of six false starts, each replaced at once by a campaign of the same design. Three
mark runs repeated, stopped by the lag brake, or ended before the integrity rule read them.

Four marks leave a run
counted: flagged, paused, far from its setup's other repeats, and traced by L9's or M0's recording.
The brake would have stopped the 14 flagged runs, taken while it only recorded. In the 222 paused
runs a message arrived more than 150~ms late, when the receiving side's disk stalled, for a cause
not identified, or \kafka{}'s broker could not reach its own controller. No run had a message arrive
before it was sent.

Without the flagged runs, or without all 1,110 taken while the brake only
recorded, no verdict moves. Without the paused runs every verdict stands but P3a on the first x86
pair, whose fitted interval sits on the edge of its $\pm 0.25$~ms bar. It is just outside the bar
with them and just inside without them. The verdict on all the runs is the one reported. The postmortem, S36,
counts every mark pair by pair, says why each was given, and draws, in four figures, all 4,823 runs of the law's blocks that measured a
delivery time and a rate, of their 4,838.

\subsection{S6.5. Read after the verdicts: the wait is the rest of a slice, and two judges departed from the plan}
\label{s:law-after}

Everything here is post hoc. It was read after the verdicts, and none of it changes one. The rate
holds no plateau. At half the slice it is 1.45 to 3.94 times its value at 0.9 of the slice, in each
of the ten slice and broker combinations whose runs reach half the slice.

L9's recordings, at a 3~ms
slice and a 1~ms tick, time the wait of the thread that timestamps each acknowledgment, for
1,516,342 acknowledgments in 304 runs. Of those whose thread waited more than 1~ms, 24.3 to 35.8\%
were still waiting at 3~ms and 3.3 to 6.8\% past 4~ms, over twelve pair, broker and load parts. A
thread preempted for a task starting its slice waits out all of it, but a thread woken while
another task holds the core waits out what is left. The acknowledgment's reply wakes the thread
that timestamps it, so its wait is the second kind. A whole-slice wait would keep all of them to
3~ms. A wait for the rest of the slice keeps $20\%$ to 3~ms and none past 4~ms. That thread waits
out what is left of the running task's slice, $R$, uniform
on $[0, s]$, and then up to a tick, $U$, uniform on $[0, h]$, so that for $h \le s$
\begin{equation}
\label{seq:restofslice}
G(x) = \Pr[R + U > x] = \begin{cases} 1 - x^2/(2sh), & 0 \le x \le h,\\ 1 - (x - h/2)/s, & h \le x \le s,\\ (s + h - x)^2/(2sh), & s \le x \le s + h,\\ 0, & x \ge s + h. \end{cases}
\end{equation}

A rate that falls from the start pulls a fitted
plateau's fall below the slice and moves it less than the slice moves. That is the split between the
free and fitted readings of P1 and P9 (postmortem, S16.10). The recording raised the rate it
recorded, 1.36 to 1.49 times for \kafka{} and 3.69 to 4.33 times for \redis{}, so these waits
describe the machines with it on.

In two places a frozen program departed from the plan, and in both the frozen answer is the one
reported. The programs judging P2, P2b and P2c group L2's runs by tick alone, so each width they
read spans the falls of both its slices, 1.5 and 3~ms. Fed Equation~\ref{seq:law}, the P2 program
returns $\cliffPooledRedisFree$ free and $\cliffPooledRedisFitted$ fitted for \redis{}, below its
band, while one slice at a time the same formula gives $\cliffPerSliceFourLo$ to
$\cliffPerSliceFourHi$ for P2 and $\cliffPerSliceTenLo$ to $\cliffPerSliceTenHi$ for P2b, inside
both bands. Those three verdicts therefore carry no evidence about the law either way. P4's program words
\redis{} ``not confirmed'' although its plateau, $0.69\%$, lies below the $2\%$ at which P4 makes a
claim, which the plan's rule reports as out of reach. What explains M0's slope on \redis{} was
also found afterwards (postmortem, S37).

\section{S7. The broker comparison, gated}
\label{s:brokers}

Once the runs the sign check condemns are removed, \kafka{} and \redis{} are equivalent on the
end-to-end latency, and a consumer setting that a co-located testbed hides decides their order.

The comparison is stated first on the end-to-end latency $E$ of Equation~\ref{P-eq:tti}, because $E$
is a causal chain and the transport proxy $S$ is not (Section~\ref{P-sec:proxy}). Over the runs the
sign check leaves standing, $E$ is equivalent between the two brokers against a
$\ttiTostMargin$~ms margin, one broadcast frame at $25$ frames per second, fixed three days before
the campaign ran. The Hodges--Lehmann shifts are $\ttiTostHLRange$~ms, \kafka{} slower, and the
highest upper bound of any $90\%$ interval of the three estimators is $\ttiTostCIHigh$~ms. $E$ runs to $t_{\mathrm{out}}$, so it also contains both clients' payload parse, which $S$
and $D$ carry for \kafka{} alone (Section~\ref{P-sec:brokers}). On $S$ the two sit within a
millisecond. At one feed the Hodges--Lehmann shift is $\tostHL$~ms ($90\%$ bootstrap interval
$\tostHLCI$), and two one-sided tests (TOST) reject both nulls against a $\tostMargin$~ms margin at $p < 0.001$. The shift holds between
$\tostHLRange$~ms across \tostLevelsWord{} concurrency levels, and Welch's test, a percentile bootstrap
and the Hodges--Lehmann estimator agree on equivalence at every level.

The two brokers are still distinguishable. The numbers on $S$ come from a powered campaign with
15 replicates at each of the three concurrency levels and a median of 125 events per run. It
replays at a rate derived from the plan and verified against wall time. It timestamps in
microseconds on one clock, so Failure~2 cannot touch its numbers. Failure~1 can, and the sign
check removed the runs it condemned (\ref{s:signcheck-selection}). On $S$, \kafka{} sits near $0.54$~ms and
\redis{} near $0.11$~ms, and the $0.41$~ms shift, \kafka{} slower, sits far from zero at every
level, in the direction the gray-literature comparisons report.

A second powered campaign, run ten hours later with the same
protocol, reproduces the shift and fails the criterion registered for it, that its shift fall
inside the first campaign's $90\%$ interval at every level. Over the runs the check keeps it gives
$0.381$, $0.414$ and $0.416$~ms. The first, from the two \redis{} runs kept at one feed, falls
$0.008$~ms below the interval. We saw only after the result that the criterion was a poor test.
A second estimate carries its own sampling error, so even identical shifts would satisfy it at all
three levels only about $38\%$ of the time. Restoring the runs the sign check condemned moves the shift by at most
$0.016$~ms (\ref{s:signcheck-selection}).

Part of even this shift is our harness. Timestamping \kafka{}'s acknowledgment on the sending
thread, as \redis{}'s already is, with one request outstanding under matched load, shrinks the
gap by $\hThreeShrinkage$~ms ($95\%$ bootstrap over runs: $\hThreeShrinkageCI$), and by
$\hThreeRepShrinkage$~ms ($95\%$ bootstrap over runs: $\hThreeRepShrinkageCI$) in a replication at $\hThreeRepRuns$ runs per
cell. What remains is a broker difference of $\hThreeResidualRange$~ms. The bias never turns a
value negative, so the sign check, which rejects $40$ of the $160$ runs behind these numbers,
cannot see it. Sizing a bias of a few hundredths of a millisecond took a controlled experiment
and a replication.

A consumer that acknowledges each message and waits for the reply reverses the order, and
injected delay exposes it (Table~\ref{stab:netem}).
With one-way delay injected identically at both brokers, \redis{}'s median end-to-end latency
reaches $4{,}651$~ms at 5~ms of delay and $103{,}143$~ms at 50~ms, against \kafka{}'s $12.4$ and
$336.6$~ms. \redis{}'s transport proxy grows about $930$ to $2{,}050$ times the delay applied, while
\kafka{}'s tracks it almost additively. The cause is the consumption pattern, not the broker. Our
\redis{} consumer acknowledges each entry and waits for the reply, the pattern the tutorials teach,
so a read of two hundred entries is followed by two hundred sequential round trips. Each trip is
free at $0.22$~ms and hopeless at 20~ms, where the service rate falls below the arrival rate and
backlog sets the delay. \kafka{}'s consumer serves from a prefetched buffer and commits on a timer,
so it pays the delay once per batch.

The falsifier was recorded before the run. If the acknowledgment pattern is the cause, batching the
acknowledgments must remove most of the effect without touching the broker, the network or the

about $32$~ms, so the constraint sits in the acknowledgment path. At five feeds batching left

A co-located testbed cannot see any of this. The round trip the two patterns multiply is a few
hundred microseconds there, so paying it once per event or once per batch differs by less than the
noise. The setting that decides the comparison under any realistic separation is therefore invisible
where most comparisons are run. That is the shape of the paper's two failures: a configuration
decides the result, and nothing in the output records it.

\subsection{S8.2. The twentyfold end-to-end gap was send lag at the kick-off}
\label{s:withdrawn-gap}

attributed it to the client's sender thread waking from idle, and built a practitioner
recommendation on it. E1's \kafka{} runs all passed the sign check, and its four cells'
medians agreed within half a millisecond, $105.3$ to $105.7$~ms.

\section{S9. Related work in full}
\label{s:related}

Clock resolution and scheduling delay are each well understood, and rules of the form we propose
already exist in neighboring fields. What the literature lacks is their consequence for the
sub-millisecond comparisons that broker benchmarks now publish, whose tools discard the evidence
that would show it.

\subsection{S9.1. Benchmark variability is structural, and here it has a mechanism}
\label{s:related-variability}

Run-to-run instability in benchmarks is structural, and the literature that shows it supports our
reading. Mytkowicz et al.\ showed that factors as innocuous as link order and environment-variable
size flip published conclusions~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}, and Georges et al.\ that JVM results
without confidence intervals routinely mislead~\cite{georges2007rigorous}. Barrett et al.\ found
that virtual machines frequently never reach the steady state their benchmarks assume, so the
distribution being sampled is itself non-stationary~\cite{barrett2017warmup}. That is the closest
published analogue of the mobility we see between identical runs. Maricq et al.\ found hundreds of
trials necessary for stable comparisons~\cite{maricq2018taming}.

Against this stands only common practice. The surveyed norm of a handful of repetitions presumes a stable
distribution~\cite{vanderkouwe2019sok,hoefler2015benchmarking}. What we add is the mechanism.
Where the send interval is commensurate with the timestamp resolution, the instability is not
variance around a value. It is a two-point support whose weights move between runs, so no
replicate count converges (Section~\ref{P-sec:extcomp}).

Tail latency under load is established, and what is new is the measurement it corrupts. Li et
al.\ decompose the tail latency of three Linux servers and find measured tails substantially worse
than a queueing model alone predicts~\cite{li2014tales}. The excess comes from background
processes, request re-ordering, interrupt routing, power management and NUMA effects. We add that
the stall distribution propagates into the measurement of an unrelated quantity, and that load
moves its tail far faster than its core. From idle to the knee of the load sweep the
negative-span rate grows $\invGrowth\times$ and the jitter's width $\coreGrowth\times$ (S3.4). A
benchmark can therefore sit at a utilization where its measurements look tight, because the
jitter is narrow, while the tail that corrupts them is already growing.

\subsection{S9.2. The resolution literature knew the problem, and the deletion is new}
\label{s:related-resolution}

The problem behind Failure~2 is older than every tool Section~\ref{P-sec:tools} reads. Danzig and
Melvin built microsecond timers for workstations in 1990 because the available clock was too
coarse for the interval being timed~\cite{danzig1990highres}. The arithmetic is older still.
Hewlett-Packard's Application Note 162-1 carries the retention identity, the reduced fraction the
lattice turns on, the ceiling on resolution gain, the symptom and the cure~\cite{hp1970tia}. The one
thing it does not contain is the failure this paper reports, because its counter keeps both
readings and uses the retained fraction as its estimator. Deleting the population that carries
the fractional part turns a solved metrology problem back into an open one.

The mathematics is a century old. Weyl's equidistribution theorem~\cite{weyl1916gleichverteilung}
governs the incommensurate regime of the send interval over the timestamp resolution in the
long run, and the three-distance theorem~\cite{sos1958distribution} the phases a finite run of
sends visits. Schuchman's
condition~\cite{schuchman1964dither}, generalized by Wannamaker et al.~\cite{wannamaker2000dither},
underlies the dithering that Table~\ref{P-tab:checks} recommends. The timing literature treats
a coarse timestamp as staleness, not deletion.

Coordinated omission is the closest neighbor, and its mirror image~\cite{tene2015latency}. There
the worst samples are never taken, so percentiles are optimistic by orders of magnitude. Under
Failure~2 the samples are taken and the latency is computed, and a downstream filter rejects it.
One loses the slow tail, the other the fast bulk, and a correction for one does nothing about the
other. The benchmark we instrument records into an HdrHistogram, which ships coordinated-omission
machinery and none for this failure~\cite{tene2015hdrhistogram}. Its sound rejection of negative
values is what motivated the positivity filter, so a protection against one measurement error
created the conditions for another.

What we claim as new is narrow. Quantization is elementary, and Cloudprofiler exists because
milliseconds are known to be too coarse~\cite{yang2022cloudprofiler}. The new element is the
interaction of a quantized timestamp, a producer with few send phases and a positivity filter. It
yields a retained fraction that is bimodal, irreproducible between identical runs and invisible
in the reported summary, with its damage set by the lowest-terms denominator of the send interval
over the timestamp resolution.

\subsection{S9.3. Rules of the right form exist, in other fields}
\label{s:related-rules}

Standardized benchmarking already contains rules of the form we propose. CoreMark-PRO requires
each workload to run for at least 1000 times the minimum timer resolution~\cite{coremark_pro}.
That is a floor on the measured interval relative to the timestamp resolution, the ratio
Equation~\ref{P-eq:retention} turns on. Network measurement goes further and keeps the impossible
value as data (Table~\ref{stab:related-rules}).

The flagship standardized benchmark for message-oriented middleware has none of this.
SPECjms2007 records delivery time in a histogram of $100$~ms default
granularity~\cite{specjms2007,sachs2009specjms}. Its run rules, design document and user's guide
contain no resolution clause, no minimum measurable interval and no counter of discarded latency
samples. Their only timing-quality requirement, clause~3.2, bounds inter-node clock agreement at
$100$~ms, which is a skew limit. The guide does discard throughput samples, by configured
constants, but it never counts the latency samples its own resolution makes unusable.

The nearest prior sighting of a negative span inside a benchmark was blamed on a clock. Klenik
and Pataricza report negative derived durations in a Hyperledger Fabric benchmark, call them a
serious violation of event causality, and trace them to a drifting peer clock~\cite{klenik2021}.
The durations oscillate smoothly around zero, a drift signature, which puts them in the channel
of our \redis{}-driver $-1000\,\mu$s negatives and outside Failure~1. Their check is post-hoc data
validation, with no per-run gate and no campaign rate.

\subsection{S9.4. The run queue inverts spans that a backlog cannot}
\label{s:related-skew}

A span can invert with no backlog at all, and that is where our reading parts from the concurrent
report. Sharma et al.\ count negative timing spans in distributed inference systems and propose a
\texttt{causality\_health} check~\cite{sharma2026causality}. Injecting skew, they observe no
violations up to $3$~ms and clear ones by $5$~ms, and read that as a skew threshold. We do not
dispute their measurements, only one sentence of the reading: ``queueing alone cannot produce
negative timing spans''.

Under the queue they model, a backlog at a service stage, the sentence is true, and
Section~\ref{P-sec:mechanism} refutes that account of our own corpus as well. As written it is
false, because two queues are in play. A message waits in a service backlog. The thread that
reads the clock waits in the kernel's run queue for a core. The second wait delays a
timestamp whether or not any backlog exists, and our rates are highest at high utilization with
no backlog at all.

If a backlog cannot invert a span, removing the backlog leaves skew as the only explanation, and a
millisecond threshold on skew becomes a sufficient check. On a loaded machine it is not. On a
single host, where one clock leaves no inter-host offset at all, we see a negative-span rate of
$\rtHighBasePct\%$ at $\rtHighLoadPct\%$ utilization, and a scheduling parameter moves it
fiftyfold without touching the clock (Table~\ref{P-tab:mechanism}). Our cross-host clocks cannot
settle the question either way. Between our hosts \texttt{chrony} reports an offset of
$\interHostOffsetUs\,\mu$s, far inside the $3$~ms at which Sharma et al.\ saw no violations, but
it bounds its own error at $\chronyHostBoundLo$--$\chronyHostBoundHi$~ms per host. We therefore
claim no cross-host bound (Section~\ref{P-sec:threats}), and the single-host rate needs none.

The skew reading is older than this dispute. The benchmark's maintainer gave it in 2021,
attributing publish latency above end-to-end latency to inter-node skew on a report from a single
node~\cite{omb_issue216}. It does not consider whether the thread that took the timestamp
was running.

Their own formulation is nearer ours than the dichotomy allows. They note that the minimum
separation between the two events ``exhibits variability due to scheduling jitter'', and that
violations grow likelier as the skew exceeds its quantiles. That is the condition of
Equation~\ref{P-eq:negspan} with skew written where we write the stall. A threshold in
milliseconds of skew cannot transfer to a machine where the stall is the term that grows.

\subsection{S9.5. Production tracing reached the conclusion without the mechanism}
\label{s:related-tracing}

Production tracing concluded, from the symptom alone, that rewriting an inverted span's timestamps
does harm. Distributed tracing systems ship clock-skew adjusters that ``modif[y] start time and log timestamps for spans that appear to be
`off' with respect to the parent span''~\cite{jaeger_clockskew}. An issue titled ``Clock Skew
Adjuster considered harmful'' called the result ``wrong data and really hard to
diagnose''~\cite{jaeger_harmful}, and Jaeger has defaulted the adjustment off since 2020. A July
2026 bug against the OpenTelemetry eBPF instrumentation reports child
spans starting before their parents on same-node resources, from a reporter who had checked for
clock skew and found none~\cite{otel_obi_2568}. Its deltas of seconds to minutes are orders of
magnitude beyond any run-queue stall, so their cause is not Failure~1. The report shows the
absence of a second hypothesis: a practitioner who has ruled out the clock, on one node, has
nowhere else to look. If the negative span comes from a late timestamp, an adjuster moves evidence instead of
correcting an error, by an amount Section~\ref{P-sec:mechanism} measures.

\subsection{S9.6. A runnable thread does not always run}
\label{s:related-scheddelay}

Work-conservation violations leave runnable threads waiting while cores sit idle. Lozi et al.\
document Linux scheduler bugs that do so for seconds, invisible to conventional testing because
throughput stays healthy~\cite{lozi2016wastedcores}. Those are CFS-era defects. Our kernel's
scheduler is EEVDF, and we make no claim that they are present.

Queueing gives the leading-order account of waiting, and it is right about what matters here:
utilization alone does not fix a waiting time, because pooling and geometry matter at fixed
$\rho$. The geometry contrast of Section~\ref{P-sec:twostate} therefore refutes a single-parameter
law and leaves multi-server queueing, which is geometry-dependent at fixed $\rho$, standing.
Chandrasekar and Kramberger apply such a single-parameter M/G/1 framing to benchmark
bias~\cite{chandrasekar2026bias}. We adopted it too, and on the load axis it fitted worse than the
mean (Table~\ref{stab:what-fell}). On a second axis they reach half of our mechanism, concurrently
and independently: their client's own interpreter lock ``artificially inflates'' the latencies it
reports, charging the harness's scheduling to the server under test. That is the inflation half.
What a positive number cannot show remains ours: the negative span, and the sign channel that
shows the acknowledgment was timestamped late.

Li et al.\ show real-time priority collapsing a tail that renicing leaves alone, on the system
under test~\cite{li2014tales}. We apply the same lever to the harness
(Section~\ref{P-sec:twostate}). An agent acting on a lagged observation is a problem of partial
observability, studied in this journal for load balancing under delayed
feedback~\cite{tahir2022delayed}. There the lag is a known property of the system. Here it sits
inside the benchmark, unknown to those reading its output.
